The RER Formula: Where Cat Calorie Math Comes From

Veterinarians calculate a cat's energy needs in two steps. Step one: RER (Resting Energy Requirement) —the calories a cat burns just existing: breathing, circulating blood, maintaining body temperature. The formula, derived from metabolic studies across species:

RER (kcal/day) = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75

Step two: multiply RER by an activity factor to get DER (Daily Energy Requirement) —the calories the cat actually needs. For a sedentary indoor cat, the multiplier is 1.2. For weight loss, 0.8. For a growing kitten, 2.5. For a nursing queen with a large litter, up to 6.0.

A 5 kg (11 lb) cat: RER = 70 × 50.75 = 70 × 3.34 ≈ 234 kcal. DER for a sedentary indoor cat = 234 × 1.2 ≈ 280 kcal/day. That's about 3 cans of average wet food, or ¾ cup of average dry food. The calculator above does this math for your cat's exact weight, then shows you what that looks like in real food.

The exponent 0.75 isn't arbitrary —it's Kleiber's Law, the observation that metabolic rate scales to the ¾ power of body mass across mammals from mice to elephants. A cat needs more calories per pound than a dog, which needs more per pound than a horse. The smaller the animal, the faster the metabolism per unit of weight.

Why the Feeding Guide on the Bag Is Wrong

The feeding guide printed on your cat food bag is a starting point, not a prescription. Here's why it's usually wrong for your specific cat:

It assumes an "average" cat that doesn't exist. The guide is calculated for an intact (not spayed/neutered) adult cat with moderate activity —a cat that goes outside, hunts, patrols territory. Your spayed indoor cat has a metabolism roughly 20–25% lower than that theoretical cat. Feeding per the bag will make her gain weight. A 2009 study found that spayed/neutered cats fed according to bag guidelines gained an average of 12% body weight over 12 months —roughly 1.5 pounds on a 12-pound cat. That's the difference between BCS 5 (ideal) and BCS 7 (overweight).

It's rounded to convenient cup increments —½ cup, ¾ cup, 1 cup —that don't correspond to the actual calorie math. A 10-pound cat who needs 220 calories on a 400 kcal/cup food should get 0.55 cups. The bag says ½ to ¾ cup. Most owners go with ¾ cup "to be safe" —that's 300 calories, or 80 extra calories per day. Over a year, that's 29,200 extra calories, or about 8 pounds of body fat. On a 10-pound cat.

It doesn't account for treats. The bag assumes 100% of calories come from the food in the bag. If you give your cat 10 Temptations treats per day (roughly 20 calories), the bag math is already off by 10%. A tablespoon of wet food as a topper on dry food? Another 15 calories. Two Greenies dental treats? 30 calories. It's easy to add 50–60 calories per day in "extras" without realizing it —and for a cat who only needs 220 calories, that's a 25% overfeed.

Wet vs Dry: The Weight-Loss Math

Wet food is the single most effective weight-loss tool for cats —not because it's magically low-calorie, but because it's mostly water. A 3-ounce can of pâté is roughly 78% water. A cup of dry kibble is 6–10% water. The water in wet food takes up stomach volume without adding calories, so the cat feels physically full on fewer calories. This matters enormously for weight loss, because the main reason cat diets fail is owner compliance —the cat begs, the owner caves. A cat on wet food begs less because they're not hungry. The stomach is full.

The carb difference compounds this. Dry food needs starch to hold the kibble shape —corn, wheat, rice, potatoes, peas, lentils. Even "grain-free" dry foods are 25–35% carbohydrate by calories because you need starch to extrude a kibble. Wet food needs no starch —meat, water, vitamins, and a gelling agent (carrageenan, agar, guar gum) in trace amounts. The result: a typical wet food is 40–50% protein, 40–50% fat, and 5–10% carbohydrate. A typical dry food is 30–35% protein, 20–25% fat, and 35–45% carbohydrate. A cat's natural diet —mice, birds, lizards —is roughly 50% protein, 40% fat, and 5% carbohydrate. Wet food is closer to the evolutionary norm.

If your cat absolutely refuses wet food —and some do, especially cats who've eaten dry exclusively for years —don't force a hunger strike. Switch to a high-protein, low-carb dry food (aim for <20% carbs by calorie, which usually means <25% carbs on the guaranteed analysis), measure portions by weight with a kitchen scale, and split into 3–4 small meals per day to keep the cat feeling fed. Automated feeders are worth the $60–80 —they eliminate the "the cat is meowing at me at 5 AM" variable from the weight-loss equation.
