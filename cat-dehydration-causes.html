💧 Why Cats Get Dehydrated 7 Causes — and What Actually Prevents Each One

Three of these are diseases. Four are the way we keep cats. | 2026-09-30 · MeltPet · 9 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-30

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for education, not diagnosis. Dehydration is a symptom, not a disease, and the cause matters more than the number. If your cat is weak, wobbly, breathing hard, won't eat, straining to urinate with little or nothing coming out, or has collapsed, skip the rest of this page and call an emergency vet.

This page answers "why does this keep happening". If you need to answer "is my cat dehydrated right now", that is a different page with a different method — the four at-home checks and the severity chart are in our cat dehydration test guide.

A Cause and a Symptom Are Not the Same Thing

Most dehydration advice online is about water bowls. Fill them, clean them, move them, buy a fountain. That advice is not wrong, and we give it below — but it treats dehydration as a single condition with a single fix, and it is not.

Dehydration is what happens when a body loses more fluid than it takes in. That can go wrong in three structurally different ways, and they need different responses:

The cat is not taking enough in. This is the classic version, and it is the one lifestyle changes address. The cat is fine; the water delivery system is not.

The cat is losing too much. Kidney disease, diabetes, vomiting, diarrhoea, a blocked bladder. Here the problem is output, and no amount of bowl-placement advice will fix it. Adding water to food helps manage it. It does not treat it.

The cat has stopped drinking. Pain on eating, nausea, or an environment the cat avoids. This is a behaviour and welfare problem that presents as a fluid problem.

Water makes all three look identical on the outside. What separates them is that the first is fixed by changing what you provide, and the other two are fixed by finding out what is actually wrong. Which is why the honest structure of this page is a list of causes rather than a list of tips.

The Three That Are Medical Conditions

1. Chronic kidney disease. This is the big one, and the numbers are not subtle. Roughly 30 to 40 percent of cats over ten have chronic kidney disease. The mechanism is straightforward: healthy kidneys concentrate urine so the body keeps water. Damaged kidneys lose that ability, so the cat produces more dilute urine, loses more water, and drinks to compensate. The trap is that the compensation can look like nothing at all for years — a cat with 50% kidney function looks exactly like a cat with 100%, which is why the disease is usually found when an owner mentions "drinking a lot lately" or "lost a little weight".

There is a diagnostic detail worth knowing if you have an older cat. Creatinine, the standard marker, does not rise until roughly 75% of kidney function is gone. SDMA rises at around 40%. That gap of years is the entire argument for screening older cats rather than waiting for symptoms — the full arithmetic is in our kidney disease guide.

2. Hyperthyroidism. An overactive thyroid raises metabolic rate, which means the cat burns through energy and fluid faster. The signature sign is a cat who is eating normally or more than usual and still losing weight — which feels counter-intuitive enough that owners often wait, assuming a good appetite means health. It does not. Hyperthyroid cats also frequently have vomiting or diarrhoea alongside, which adds fluid loss on top of the increased turnover.

3. Diabetes mellitus. When blood glucose exceeds what the kidney can reabsorb, glucose spills into the urine — and glucose in urine pulls water with it. This is called osmotic diuresis and it produces exactly the pattern owners report: a cat drinking a great deal and urinating enormous clumps in the litter box, often with weight loss despite a good appetite. Obesity is a major risk factor, which makes this the one condition on this list that a management decision meaningfully prevents. The British Shorthair guide is blunt about the breed's version of this: free-feeding a cat with no off switch is how you get diabetes.

The One That Kills in 48 Hours

4. A urethral blockage, in a male cat. This is not a dehydration story that becomes an emergency. It is an emergency that presents as dehydration, and it belongs at the top of the list for a different reason than the three above: those take months or years, and this one takes a day.

The obstruction is usually a plug of mineral and cellular debris in the narrow urethra. Urine backs up, pressure builds, and the consequences escalate fast — within 24 to 48 hours a blocked cat can be in acute kidney failure and shock. It is far more common in male cats because their urethra is narrower and longer. Treatment runs $1,500–$3,500 for unblocking, catheterisation and 48–72 hours of hospitalisation with IV fluids, and it is money well spent, because the alternative is not a worse outcome. It is no cat.

The reason this cause belongs on a dehydration page is what it looks like from across the room. A blocked cat crouches in the litter box repeatedly, straining, often for minutes at a time, producing little or nothing. Most owners read this as constipation. The tell that separates them: constipation produces a cat that is uncomfortable and may defecate small hard stools; a blockage produces repeated, unproductive trips with no stool and no urine, and the cat usually stops eating. If your cat is doing that, it is not a hydration problem. It is a today problem.

The Two Acute Lifestyle-Adjacent Ones

5. Vomiting and diarrhoea. These are the fastest route to dehydration because they work on both sides of the equation at once — removing fluid directly and stopping the cat from replacing it. A cat that drinks and vomits the water back up within 20 minutes, twice in a row, is absorbing nothing at all. This is why the timeline matters more than the volume: our vomiting colour guide uses the line of more than 12 hours of vomiting or diarrhoea as the point to call a vet on the same day, and a single large episode in a small animal moves body weight fast. By the time many owners bring a vomiting cat in, it is down a pound and a half — which on a ten-pound cat is 15% of body weight, deep into the territory where oral rehydration is no longer realistic.

6. A dry-food-only diet. This is the cause that is entirely ours to change, and it is the largest single factor in feline hydration on an ordinary day. The numbers are stark. A cat needs roughly 40–60 ml of water per kilogram per day — about 200–300 ml for a 5 kg cat. Dry food is around 8% moisture, so a dry-fed cat takes in roughly 5 ml from the food itself and has to drink the remaining 195–295 ml actively, from a bowl, every day. Wet food is around 78% moisture: a wet-fed cat clears more than half the target at the bowl. That is a fourfold difference in the amount of water a cat has to choose to drink.

Put that alongside the reason cats are bad at this in the first place. Their ancestors took water from prey, not from standing water, and a mouse is roughly 70% water. A dry bowl of kibble is the opposite of everything their hydrology was built around. The consequence is not that dry-fed cats are all sick. It is that they run a permanent deficit that a disease, a hot week or a few days of vomiting can tip over. If you take one thing from this page, take this one: the lever is food moisture, not bowl placement.

7. Water the Cat Will Not Drink

The last cause is the one that hides inside the others. A cat that has access to water and declines it is not being difficult — there are several concrete reasons a cat stops drinking, and they are all fixable or at least identifiable.

Pain in the mouth. Dental disease is not rare in cats: around 70% have some form of dental disease by age three. A 2015 study of apparently healthy cats with mouths that looked normal found that 41% had clinically significant dental disease visible only on radiographs. A cat whose mouth hurts when it eats or drinks will often keep eating just enough to survive while quietly reducing both — and the fluid side of that is invisible until it is not.

Nausea. Cats that feel sick stop drinking before they visibly stop eating. If a cat is lip-smacking, drooling, or hanging its head over the bowl without actually drinking, that is a nausea pattern, not a hydration preference.

The bowl itself. Wide and shallow rather than deep and narrow, because a lot of cats will not press their whiskers against the sides of a bowl. Ceramic or stainless rather than plastic, cleaned daily — biofilm you cannot see makes water taste off to a cat well before it bothers you.

Where the water sits. Cats are descended from animals that do not drink where they kill and do not drink where they eliminate. Water next to the food bowl or the litter tray gets ignored more often than people expect, and the fix costs nothing but a walk across the room. One water station per cat, in separate places, matters in multi-cat households where a nervous cat will not contest a resource.

Still versus running. Many cats drink more from a fountain, and it is a cheap experiment. Be honest about the size of the effect, though: a fountain increases drinking at the margins. It does not close a gap that food created.

The two factors that multiply all seven

Age. At one end, a kitten with diarrhoea can go from playing to flat in a few hours because small bodies lose fluid proportionally faster and have fewer reserves. At the other, the two most common chronic causes on this list — kidney disease and hyperthyroidism — are largely diseases of cats over ten, and older cats also often drink less as their thirst response blunts. Everything on this page shifts down a level for both groups.

Heat. A cat in a warm room loses fluid through respiration and panting, on top of everything above. Cats do not sweat meaningfully — they groom and rely on evaporation — so a hot day combined with any other cause on this list produces the outcome faster than the same cat would reach it in winter. Water intake goes up in hot weather, which means every problem in this article gets worse in July.

What Actually Prevents Each Cause

Same seven causes, mapped to the specific thing that changes them. Notice how few of these are about water bowls.

The asymmetry in that third column is the real lesson. Four of the seven causes are things you cannot fix at home, and the only thing that changes their outcome is how early someone looks. That is true of kidney disease, of hyperthyroidism, of diabetes, and of a tooth that has been hurting for months — and it is loudly true of the blocked bladder, where the timeline is hours rather than months.

How to Know Whether Any of This Is Working

There is exactly one measurement that settles the question, and it is not how much the cat seems to drink. Drinking is impossible to estimate by eye — a cat can double its intake and you will not notice.

Weigh your cat. Same scale, same time of day, before breakfast, written down with the date. On a 4 kg cat, 200 grams is 5% of body weight, and 5% is roughly where mild dehydration begins. The full method, plus the gum and eye checks that work when a scale is not available, is in the dehydration test guide.

And if the reason your cat is losing water is that it is eating too little of something it will actually drink, the food maths is worth running properly rather than guessed at. Our cat calorie calculator will give you the daily intake a given cat needs, and the water target on this page cross-checks against it: the maintenance rule of thumb is roughly one millilitre of water per kilocalorie eaten. Run the numbers on a 10 lb cat and the two methods agree — 260 kcal and about 180–270 ml, which is the same target reached from two different directions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do cats dehydrate so easily?

Because their thirst drive is weak and their diet often has no water in it. Cats evolved as desert hunters that took their water from prey, and prey is roughly 70% water — a mouse is a meal and a drink at the same time. Dry kibble is about 8% moisture, so a dry-fed cat has to drink roughly four times as much water as a wet-fed cat, from a bowl, by choice, every day. The arithmetic is stacked against them and the signal that is supposed to make them drink is quiet.

What medical conditions cause dehydration in cats?

Three are common and chronic: chronic kidney disease, which affects 30 to 40 percent of cats over ten and destroys the kidney's ability to concentrate urine, so the cat urinates more and loses fluid it cannot replace; hyperthyroidism, which raises metabolic rate; and diabetes mellitus, where glucose spilling into the urine drags water out with it. A fourth is acute and much more dangerous: a urethral blockage, which is far more common in male cats and can kill within 24 to 48 hours.

How much water should a cat drink per day?

Roughly 40 to 60 millilitres per kilogram of body weight per day, which is about 200 to 300 ml for a 5 kg cat. Dry food is around 8% moisture, so a dry-fed cat gets almost none of that from food and has to drink most of it actively. Wet food is around 78% moisture, so a wet-fed cat clears more than half the target at the bowl. This single difference is the largest factor in whether a cat stays hydrated on a normal day.

How fast can a cat become dehydrated?

A healthy adult can take days. A kitten, a senior, or any cat that is vomiting, has diarrhoea or has a blocked bladder can go from normal to serious in hours. Vomiting and diarrhoea are the fastest route because they remove fluid directly and also stop the cat replacing it — a cat that vomits water back up within 20 minutes, twice in a row, is not absorbing anything. A urethral blockage is faster still: a cat can be in shock inside 24 hours.

Will a cat drink more water if I add a fountain?

Often yes, and it is a cheap experiment worth running — but be clear-eyed about the size of the effect. A fountain increases drinking at the margins. It does not close a gap that food created, and the gap between dry and wet food is roughly four-fold. Running water is a useful nudge for a cat that already drinks a little; it is not a substitute for changing what they eat.

Can dehydration in cats be prevented?

Most of it, yes — but not by making the cat drink. The lever that matters is food moisture, not water bowl placement. A cat on at least 50 percent wet food takes in more than half its daily water at the bowl without ever visiting the water dish, which is exactly what our kidney disease guide recommends for every cat under three as a starting point. Bowl placement, bowl material, fountains and water stations all help at the margins. Food is the line item that changes the arithmetic.
