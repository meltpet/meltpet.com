🐱 Is Your Cat Dehydrated? 4 Checks, Ranked by How Much They Actually Tell You

Four at-home checks, ranked by reliability — and the two ways the popular one lies. | 2026-09-27 · MeltPet · 9 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-27

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for education, not diagnosis — nothing here replaces a hands-on exam. Dehydration is a symptom, not a disease, and the cause matters more than the number. If your cat is weak, wobbly, breathing hard, won't eat, straining to urinate, or has collapsed, skip the rest of this page and call an emergency vet.

The First Time I Ran the Pinch Test on My Own Cat, I Got the Answer Wrong

Nell is thirteen. She's a small cat — 3.6 kg on a good week, and a good week is getting rarer. Last spring she went off her food for two days and I did what every article tells you to do: I pinched the skin between her shoulder blades, let go, and watched it snap back. It did. Fast, clean, no hesitation. So I told myself she was fine and waited another day before calling the clinic.

She was not fine. She was down 260 grams — about 7% of her body weight — and the vet could feel it in her gums before she ever put a needle in. Her skin had been snapping back the whole time.

That is the thing nobody puts in the infographic. Cat skin is looser than dog skin, and it is loose on purpose. Cats are built to compress through gaps barely wider than their own skull, which means their skin slides over the muscle underneath instead of being anchored to it. It also loses recoil with age and with weight loss — the exact two things that were true about Nell. You are testing the elasticity of a structure that was designed to stretch, on an animal whose elasticity is already changing for reasons that have nothing to do with water.

This is not an argument that the pinch test is worthless. It is an argument that it is one data point, and that it happens to be the weakest of the four you can check at home without equipment. So let's do all four, in the order that makes sense.

Before You Check Anything: You Need a Baseline

Dehydration is a change. You cannot measure a change without knowing where your cat started, and this is where most at-home checks fall apart — people run the tests on a cat they have never actually measured, decide the result looks "normal-ish", and go back to their evening.

Two baselines are worth having, and the good news is you can collect both in five minutes tonight, while your cat is healthy and unbothered.

Your cat's real weight. Not the number from the last vet visit eighteen months ago. Weigh yourself holding the cat, weigh yourself alone, subtract. A kitchen scale works too if your cat tolerates a bowl. Write the number down with the date. On a 4 kg cat, 200 grams is 5% of body weight — and 5% is roughly where mild dehydration begins. That is a difference you will not see by looking, and will absolutely see on a scale.

What normal looks like on your cat, specifically. Lift the upper lip and touch the gums while your cat is fine. Note the colour and how slick they feel. Look at the eyes straight on and note how full they sit in the socket. These are the reference images you will compare against later — and they are not interchangeable between cats, which is exactly why the guides that show you one photo of "normal pink gums" fail so often in practice.

#1 — Body Weight: The Only Measure That Doesn't Guess

Fluid is heavy. Lose a litre of it and the scale knows long before the skin, the gums, or the eyes do. That makes body weight the closest thing you have to a real measurement at home, and the reason every veterinary assessment of dehydration starts with a percentage of body weight lost rather than an eyeball estimate.

The threshold to remember is 5%. Below that, your cat can be mildly dry and look entirely normal. Around 5%, you are into mild dehydration — 200 grams on a 4 kg cat, 250 grams on a 5 kg cat. Between 6% and 8% is moderate, and that is usually where a vet starts talking about subcutaneous fluids. Above 9%, you are in severe territory, and the signs get loud enough that you will not need a scale to notice them.

Two caveats. A single weight is useless — you need the baseline from the section above, taken while your cat was well. And weight loss is not automatically fluid. A cat with untreated hyperthyroidism or cancer can lose weight for weeks while staying perfectly hydrated. If the loss is gradual rather than sudden, the scale is telling you something, but it is not necessarily telling you this.

#2 — Gum Moisture and Capillary Refill: The Best Test Most People Never Try

This is the check that would have caught Nell two days earlier, and it takes about four seconds.

Gently lift your cat's upper lip. Press a fingertip against the gum above a canine tooth until the spot goes white, then lift your finger and count how long it takes to come back to pink. On a well-hydrated cat that is essentially instant — under two seconds, and usually under one. Then run your fingertip along the gum line and pay attention to how it feels.

A hydrated gum is slick and glossy, like the inside of your cheek. A dehydrated gum is tacky — it grips your finger slightly, and the surface looks dull rather than wet. The difference is not subtle once you have felt both on the same cat, which is why the baseline matters so much. If you have never touched your cat's gums at all, tacky may just be what you are feeling.

The colour column is doing separate work from the moisture column, and it is worth being clear about that. Moisture tells you about fluid. Colour tells you about circulation — and a pale or grey gum line is a shock sign, not a dehydration sign. If your cat's gums are pale and the refill is slow, that combination means blood is not getting where it needs to go, and the vet needs to see it now regardless of what any other check says.

#3 — Eye Position and the Third Eyelid: Reliable but Late

Look at your cat's face straight on, at eye level. On a hydrated cat the eyeballs sit full in the socket, with the lids resting normally around them.

As fluid is lost, the eye begins to sink backwards into the socket — a slightly sunken eye, with the lid opening taking on a narrower, more oval shape than usual. At the same time, the third eyelid (the pinkish-white membrane that normally hides in the inner corner) can start sliding across the eye. When you see a persistent third eyelid, you are usually looking at moderate dehydration or worse.

Here is the catch, and it is why this one sits third rather than first: it is a late sign. A cat can be 5% down and have perfectly normal-looking eyes. What eyes are good at is being a hard signal when they do change — there is not much else that makes a third eyelid slide out, so if you see it, you are not guessing. It is just that by the time it appears, you have lost the window where drinking more water would have fixed the problem.

One more thing, for anyone with an older cat: orbital fat shrinks with age, so older cats can look slightly sunken-eyed all the time. This is another reason the baseline matters. You are not asking whether your cat's eyes look sunken. You are asking whether they look more sunken than last month.

#4 — The Skin Pinch Test, Done Properly (and Understood Properly)

Now the one you came for. Nothing above replaces it — it just puts it in its place.

Where to pinch. Between the shoulder blades, or along the spine on the upper back. Do not use the loose skin at the side of the neck or the flank; that skin is slack on every cat alive and will tent even on a fully hydrated one.

How. Lift a small fold of skin — not a handful, just a fold about the width of your finger — raise it about two centimetres, and release it. Watch how it falls.

How to read it. Skin that drops straight back and settles is the normal result. Skin that takes a beat to come down, or that stays up for a moment before slowly falling, is a real signal and belongs in the "recheck now" column. Skin that holds a ridge or "tent" and does not fall back at all is a serious sign.

The correction worth making. You will see videos that tell you the skin should return "within three seconds" and that anything slower means dehydration. There is no such threshold in the veterinary literature, and treating seconds as a pass/fail line is how people get this wrong in both directions. Skin recoil differs enormously between individual cats, and the only comparison that means anything is this cat, right now, versus this cat on a normal day. If you have no baseline, all you can reasonably say is "that looked slow" or "that looked normal" — and "looked normal" is not evidence your cat is hydrated.

The two blind spots. Thin, older cats have lost the subcutaneous fat and skin collagen that give young cats their snap, so their skin tents mildly even when they are perfectly hydrated — a false alarm. Overweight cats have the opposite problem: the fat layer holds the skin under tension, so it springs back even when the cat is down several percent of body weight — a false all-clear. Nell was the first case. If I had known to weigh her instead, I would have caught it two days earlier.

What the chart is good for is deciding which of three things you do next — nothing, call today, or drive now.

🚩 Red Flags That Skip the Waiting Room

Everything above is about degrees. These are the ones that stop being about degrees. If any of the following is true, put the guide down and make the call.

  • The skin between the shoulder blades stays tented and does not fall back. This is not a mild result with a slow reading. It is a severe one.
  • Gums that are pale, grey, or blue — or a refill time over three seconds. That combination is a circulation problem, not just a fluid one.
  • A third eyelid that stays across the eye. Both eyes, persisting for more than a few minutes.
  • Weakness, wobbling, or an inability to stand. Collapse is an emergency in any species.
  • Fast breathing, or breathing with the mouth open. Cats do not pant the way dogs do. Open-mouth breathing is always serious.
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea for more than 12 hours, or anything that will not stay down. Replacing fluid by mouth is not realistic when it is coming straight back up.
  • Not eating for more than 24 hours. Cats are not small dogs here. A cat that stops eating for 24 to 48 hours can develop hepatic lipidosis, a liver condition that is far more dangerous than the missed meals themselves.
  • A male cat crouching in the litter box over and over, straining, with little or no urine coming out. This is the one to memorise. A urethral blockage can be fatal within 24 to 48 hours, it is far more common in male cats, and it looks like constipation to a lot of owners. It also causes dehydration rather than being caused by it — and it is the most common reason a cat's hydration collapses in a single day.
  • Any of the above in a cat who already has kidney disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, or who is on a diuretic. These cats have less reserve. Everything on this page shifts down a level for them.
  • Any of the above in a kitten or a senior. Small bodies lose fluid proportionally faster. A kitten with diarrhoea can go from playing to flat in a few hours.

What You Can Do Tonight (Without a Vet Visit)

If your cat is in the mild column — tacky gums, slightly slow skin, still eating, still themselves — there is a real amount you can do before you call anyone. In rough order of how much difference each one makes:

Change what they eat, not just what they drink. This is the single biggest lever and it is not close, which is why it gets its own section below. A cat eating wet food takes in more than half their daily water at the bowl without ever visiting the water dish.

Put water in more places, in the right containers. Wide, shallow bowls rather than deep narrow ones — whiskers are sensitive, and a lot of cats will decline to drink from a bowl that presses on their face. Ceramic or stainless rather than plastic, cleaned daily, because a biofilm you cannot see makes water taste off to a cat long before it bothers you.

Move the water away from the food and the litter box. Cats are descended from animals that do not drink next to a kill and do not drink next to where they eliminate. This sounds like folklore; it is one of the more reliably observed behaviours in multi-cat households.

Try running water. Many cats drink more from a fountain than a still bowl, and it is a cheap experiment. Be clear-eyed about the size of the effect, though — a fountain increases drinking at the margins. It does not close a gap that food created.

Add liquid to the food you already feed. A tablespoon of warm water stirred into wet food, or a splash of unsalted broth, is easy to test. Add it gradually — dump too much in at once and a lot of cats will simply walk away, which loses you more than you gained.

Weigh your cat tomorrow morning. Same scale, same time, before breakfast. This is the only way to know whether any of the above is working, and it takes ninety seconds.

What not to do: Do not give water by syringe unless a vet has shown you how and told you to — cats cannot swallow safely while fluid is being pushed into the side of the mouth, and the resulting aspiration pneumonia is a worse problem than the dehydration. Do not use human electrolyte drinks; the sugar and sodium ratios are wrong for a cat's kidneys. And do not assume rehydration fixes anything on its own. If the underlying cause is vomiting, diarrhoea, or blocked urine, adding water without treating the cause just delays the vet visit.

The Thing That Prevents Almost All of This

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic behind everything above. A cat needs roughly 40 to 60 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day — about 200 to 300 ml for a 5 kg cat. Dry food is around 8% moisture, which means a dry-fed cat gets something in the range of 5 ml from their food and has to drink the remaining 195 to 295 ml actively, from a bowl, every single day.

Wet food is around 78% moisture. A wet-fed cat gets more than half their target from the food itself and only has to top up the rest.

That is a four-fold difference in how much water a cat has to choose to drink — and cats are the wrong animal to rely on making that choice. Their ancestors were desert hunters who took their water from prey, not from standing water, and the thirst drive that produced is genuinely weaker than a dog's. This is the mechanism underneath almost everything on this page: cats drift into mild chronic dehydration not because anything is wrong with them, but because the maths is stacked against them and their thirst signal is quiet.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if my cat is dehydrated at home?

Weigh your cat. Acute fluid loss shows up on the scale faster than it shows up anywhere else — a drop of about 5% of body weight is the rough line into mild dehydration, which on a 4 kg cat is only 200 grams. If you cannot weigh your cat, check gum moisture and capillary refill time next: lift the upper lip, touch the gums, and see how quickly they go from white back to pink after you press them. The skin pinch test comes last, because cat skin is looser than dog skin and its recoil changes with age and body condition.

Does the skin pinch test work on cats?

It works as a screening cue, not as a diagnosis, and it is the least reliable of the at-home checks on a cat specifically. Cat skin is built to stretch — cats compress through gaps barely wider than their skull, so the skin moves independently of the muscle underneath. That elasticity also declines with age and weight loss and increases with body fat, so thin older cats tent their skin even when hydrated, and overweight cats snap back even when they are not. A slow return is worth acting on. A fast return does not prove your cat is fine.

How much water should a cat drink per day?

Roughly 40 to 60 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day, and the answer depends heavily on what your cat eats. A 5 kg cat needs about 200 to 300 ml total. Dry food is only around 8% moisture, so a dry-fed cat gets almost none of that from food and has to drink most of it. Wet food is around 78% moisture, so a wet-fed cat clears more than half the target at the bowl. This is also why cats on dry-only diets have to drink roughly four times as much water as cats on wet food — and why cats, who evolved to take water from prey rather than from puddles, so often fall short.

What are the first signs of dehydration in a cat?

Weight loss is first and easiest to miss, because it requires a baseline you probably do not have. After that: gums that feel tacky or dry instead of slick, skin between the shoulder blades that returns a beat slower than usual, and a cat that is quieter or hiding more. Sunken eyes and a visible third eyelid are later signs, and they usually mean moderate dehydration or worse — by the time you can see them, you are past the point of managing this at home.

Can I give my cat water with a syringe?

Do not do it unless a veterinarian has shown you how. Cats cannot swallow safely while liquid is being pushed into the side of their mouth, and fluid that goes down the wrong way causes aspiration pneumonia — a serious secondary problem that is far worse than the dehydration you were trying to fix. Offering water, adding water to food, and letting your cat drink at their own pace is slow but safe. If your cat is genuinely too weak to drink, that is a sign they need subcutaneous or intravenous fluids from a vet, not a syringe at home.

When should I take my cat to the vet for dehydration?

Go to the emergency clinic immediately if the skin between the shoulder blades stays tented and does not fall back, if the gums are pale, grey, or blue, if you see a third eyelid sliding across the eye, if the cat is weak, wobbly, or cannot stand, or if they are breathing fast with the mouth open. Call your regular vet today if your cat has stopped eating for more than 24 hours, has been vomiting or having diarrhoea for more than 12 hours, or has lost about 5% of body weight. And go immediately if a male cat is repeatedly crouching in the litter box straining with little or no urine coming out — a urethral blockage can kill within 24 to 48 hours.
