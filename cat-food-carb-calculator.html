How the Math Works: Carbs by Difference

Here's the part food companies don't print on the bag: carbohydrates are not a required line item on a US pet food label. You can't read the carbs — you have to calculate them. The formula has two steps.

Step 1 — remove the water. Dry matter is everything in the food that isn't moisture. If a food is 10% moisture, it's 90% dry matter:

Dry Matter (DM) % = 100 − Moisture %

Step 2 — convert each nutrient to dry matter, then subtract. Convert protein, fat, fiber, and ash to their dry-matter percentage, then whatever is left over is carbohydrate:

Carbs % (DM) = 100 − Protein(DM) − Fat(DM) − Fiber(DM) − Ash(DM)

Where each nutrient is converted with: Nutrient(DM) = Nutrient(as-fed) ÷ Dry Matter × 100.

Worked example — the dry food pre-filled above: 10% moisture, so DM = 90%. Protein DM = 34 ÷ 90 × 100 = 37.8%. Fat DM = 16 ÷ 90 × 100 = 17.8%. Fiber DM = 4 ÷ 90 × 100 = 4.4%. Ash DM = 7 ÷ 90 × 100 = 7.8%. Carbs DM = 100 − 37.8 − 17.8 − 4.4 − 7.8 = 32.2% — a high-carb food for a cat, exactly what the label never tells you.

Why You Can't Compare Wet vs Dry Without This

The moisture content is the trap. Dry kibble is about 10% water. Canned food is about 78% water. If you line up the crude protein numbers straight off two labels, the dry food's "34% protein" looks like it crushes the wet food's "10% protein." It doesn't. The wet food is 78% water, so its 10% protein is actually about 45% protein on a dry-matter basis — higher than the dry food.

This matters because cats don't eat water. They eat the dry matter. A cat's natural diet — mice, birds, lizards — is roughly 50% protein, 40% fat, and 5% carbohydrate. Wet food lands near that (5–15% carbs). Raw is even closer (0–5%). Dry kibble, which needs starch to hold its pellet shape, runs 35–45% carbohydrate — several times higher than anything a cat evolved to eat. That's the number this calculator reveals, and it's why a cat who lives on high-carb kibble is fighting their own biology.

Dogs are different — they've been eating human scraps for tens of thousands of years and tolerate carbohydrate far better. A dog food at 30% carbs is unremarkable. The same food fed to a cat is a slow problem. This is why the calculator's warning thresholds shift when you switch the species toggle.

Two Label Traps to Watch For

Trap 1 — ash is missing on US labels. The AAFCO "guaranteed analysis" panel lists protein, fat, fiber, and moisture, but not ash (the mineral content left after burning). EU labels do list it. For a US label, you have to estimate ash: use roughly 7% for dry food and 2% for canned food. It's an estimate, so your carb figure is accurate to within a couple of percentage points — more than enough to tell a 10% food from a 35% one. (The calculator pre-fills this for you.)

Trap 2 — "guaranteed" means "minimum" or "maximum," not exact. Crude protein is a minimum guarantee; crude fiber is a maximum. The actual batch varies. This is why the carb figure is an estimate by difference, not a lab value — treat it as a reliable ballpark, not a decimal-point measurement. If a food's protein, fat, fiber, ash, and moisture add up to more than 100%, the label numbers are inconsistent and the carb estimate will be off — the calculator will flag this.
