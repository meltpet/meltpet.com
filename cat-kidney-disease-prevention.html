🐱 Your Cat's Kidneys Are Failing 3 Years Before You'll Notice

Prevention that actually works —hydration math, diet strategies, and the bloodwork marker that catches it early. | 2026-07-06 · MeltPet · 8 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-06

First, the Number That Should Keep You Up at Night.

30 to 40 percent. That's how many cats over ten have chronic kidney disease —and the real number is probably higher, because most cats don't get diagnosed until the disease is advanced and the owner brings them in for "drinking a lot lately" or "lost some weight." By that point, roughly 75% of kidney function is gone. The remaining 25% is what the cat lives on —for months or years, depending on when you catch it and what you do next.

I worked in pet food for five years. I've sat in rooms with veterinary nutritionists who specialize in renal disease. And here's the thing they all say, the thing that doesn't make it into the cheerful "Top 10 Tips for Cat Hydration!" blog posts: chronic kidney disease is not reversible. The nephrons that die are dead forever. But the speed at which they die? That, you can control.

Cats diagnosed at IRIS Stage 2 who receive consistent treatment routinely live three to four years with good quality of life —playing, eating, sitting in sunbeams. Cats diagnosed at Stage 4 who receive no treatment live weeks to months. The difference isn't luck. It's early detection and a prevention strategy that starts years before the cat looks sick. Here's what you actually need to know.

Why Cats' Kidneys Fail: It's Not Bad Luck. It's Biology.

Cats are descended from desert animals. Felis silvestris lybica, the African wildcat —the ancestor of every domestic cat currently shedding on your couch —evolved in an environment where water was scarce and prey was wet. A mouse is roughly 70% water. A lizard, similar. A small bird, the same. The wildcat got almost all of its water from its food. Its kidneys evolved to concentrate urine to an extraordinary degree —far more than dogs or humans —to conserve every possible drop of water. This is an elegant adaptation when your diet is fresh-killed rodents and the nearest water source is a seasonal riverbed three miles away.

It's a terrible adaptation when your diet is dry kibble that's 6–10% moisture and your water bowl is across the room.

A cat eating exclusively dry food lives in a state of chronic, low-grade dehydration. Their kidneys are constantly working at the upper limit of their concentrating ability —and they do this for years. The nephrons don't regenerate. The damage accumulates. And because cats are descended from solitary prey animals that can't afford to show weakness, they hide it. A cat with 50% kidney function looks exactly like a cat with 100% kidney function —same appetite, same energy, same litter box behavior, just slightly larger urine clumps that you'd only notice if you were tracking them. Nobody tracks urine clump sizes. So nobody notices.

This is also why Persian cats, Abyssinians, Siamese, Ragdolls, and Maine Coons show up disproportionately in CKD statistics —some lines carry a genetic predisposition, especially polycystic kidney disease (PKD) in Persians and related breeds, which causes fluid-filled cysts to form in the kidneys starting at birth and progressively destroy functional tissue over the cat's lifetime.

The Hydration Math Nobody Does

A cat needs roughly 40–60 milliliters of water per kilogram of body weight per day —on the lower end if they eat wet food, on the higher end if they eat only dry. For a 5-kilogram cat (about 11 pounds), that's 200–300 ml per day, or roughly 7–10 ounces. Let's look at where that water actually comes from.

Dry food scenario. A typical dry cat food is 8% moisture. If your 5 kg cat eats 60 grams of dry food per day, they get about 5 ml of water from their food —essentially nothing. The remaining 195–295 ml has to come from drinking. That's a full cup of water, every day, from a bowl. How many cats do you know who drink a full cup of water from a bowl every day?

Wet food scenario. A typical canned cat food is 78% moisture. If your 5 kg cat eats 200 grams of wet food per day (roughly two 3-ounce cans), they get about 156 ml of water from their food —more than half their daily requirement. The remaining 44–144 ml comes from drinking. That's a couple of sips from the fountain. Most cats on wet food hit their hydration target without you ever seeing them drink.

This isn't a marketing pitch for wet food. It's arithmetic. A cat on a dry-only diet has to drink roughly four times as much water as a cat on a wet-food diet —and cats, as a species, have a weak thirst drive. They evolved to get water from prey, not from drinking. You can buy three fountains and place them in every room of your house, and your dry-food cat will still probably drink less water than a wet-food cat who has one bowl. The fountain helps. It doesn't close the gap. The food closes the gap.

If your cat refuses wet food —and some do, especially cats who've eaten dry kibble exclusively for years —transition slowly. Mix a teaspoon of wet food into the dry. Increase the ratio by a teaspoon every three days. It may take a month. Some cats won't switch fully, and for those cats, even a partial switch —one wet meal a day, dry for the other —makes a measurable difference in total water intake. Every milliliter matters.

The Bloodwork Window: SDMA Catches It 2–4 Years Before Creatinine

For decades, the standard screening test for kidney disease was creatinine —a waste product of muscle metabolism that healthy kidneys filter out of the blood. Creatinine works as a marker, but it has a fatal flaw: it doesn't rise until roughly 75% of kidney function is gone. A cat with "normal creatinine" on bloodwork could have lost two-thirds of their kidney function and you'd get a report that says "within normal limits." You'd go home relieved. The kidneys would continue deteriorating. Three years later, the cat would start losing weight and drinking more, you'd test again, and now the creatinine is elevated —but the window for early intervention closed years ago.

In 2015, IDEXX Laboratories introduced SDMA —symmetric dimethylarginine —as a new kidney biomarker. SDMA rises when roughly 40% of kidney function is lost, not 75%. That's a two-to-four-year early-warning window compared to creatinine alone. It's also less affected by muscle mass than creatinine —important for senior cats who often lose muscle as they age, which can artificially lower creatinine and mask kidney decline.

Here's what to ask your vet: "Can we add SDMA to the blood panel?" It's available through IDEXX reference labs and some in-house analyzers. It typically adds $15–$25 to the cost of a senior blood panel. If you have a cat over 7, run a full senior panel —CBC, chemistry with creatinine and SDMA, T4 (thyroid), and urinalysis with urine specific gravity —once a year. The panel costs roughly $120–$200 depending on your location. A three-day hospitalization for a cat in uremic crisis costs $2,500–$5,000. The math, like most preventive care math, is not close.

Urine specific gravity (USG) is the other underused marker. Before creatinine rises, before SDMA rises, the kidneys lose their ability to concentrate urine. A USG below 1.035 in a cat —especially a cat over 7 —is a red flag even if all other values are normal. It means the kidneys are struggling to conserve water. This is often the very first measurable sign of kidney dysfunction, and it's visible on a $30 urinalysis years before anything shows up in bloodwork.

Diet: The Three Things That Actually Reduce Kidney Workload

1. Phosphorus —the Nutrient That Accelerates Kidney Damage

Phosphorus is the most important dietary variable in kidney disease progression. Healthy kidneys excrete excess phosphorus. Failing kidneys don't —phosphorus builds up in the blood, which triggers secondary hyperparathyroidism, which leaches calcium from bones, which further damages the kidneys in a feedback loop that accelerates decline. High phosphorus intake doesn't cause kidney disease in a healthy cat, but it absolutely makes existing kidney disease progress faster —and since early CKD is usually undiagnosed, a high-phosphorus diet essentially pours fuel on a fire you didn't know was burning.

Meat is naturally high in phosphorus. There's no way around that. But the phosphorus in whole meat is less bioavailable than the inorganic phosphates added to many commercial cat foods as preservatives and texture agents —sodium tripolyphosphate, tetrasodium pyrophosphate, phosphoric acid. These are the ones to worry about. They're not listed on the guaranteed analysis, but they're in the ingredients list. If your cat food contains "sodium phosphate," "phosphoric acid," or any ingredient with "phosphate" in the name, it's adding unnecessary phosphorus load to kidneys that may already be struggling. For senior cats, choose foods without added inorganic phosphates.

2. Protein —the Myth That Won't Die

For thirty years, the standard advice was "low protein for kidney cats." This was based on the observation that protein metabolism produces nitrogenous waste products that failing kidneys struggle to excrete. The logic seemed sound. The problem is that cats are obligate carnivores —their metabolism is built around high protein intake, and restricting protein in cats leads to muscle wasting, weight loss, and a spiral of frailty that shortens life more than the kidney disease itself would have.

The modern consensus, reflected in the IRIS (International Renal Interest Society) treatment guidelines and every veterinary nutrition textbook published after 2015, is moderate, high-quality protein —not low protein. You want enough protein to maintain muscle mass, from sources that are highly digestible (animal proteins, not plant proteins), and you want to manage the phosphorus that comes with that protein. Prescription renal diets —Hill's k/d, Royal Canin Renal Support, Purina NF —are formulated to balance these competing goals: moderate protein from high-quality sources, severely restricted phosphorus, plus added omega-3 fatty acids (which reduce renal inflammation), potassium (which kidney cats waste in urine), and B vitamins (which kidney cats also lose). They're not a marketing gimmick. Multiple clinical trials show they extend survival time compared to standard maintenance diets in cats with CKD.

3. Omega-3s —the Thing You Can Add Today That Has Evidence Behind It

Omega-3 fatty acids —specifically EPA and DHA from fish oil —reduce glomerular inflammation and oxidative stress in the kidneys. A 2011 randomized controlled trial published in the Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine found that cats with experimentally induced CKD who received omega-3 supplementation had slower progression, less proteinuria, and less glomerular damage than cats who received omega-6 fatty acids or a control diet. The effect was measurable and significant.

If you do one supplement for a senior cat, make it fish oil. Not flaxseed oil —cats can't efficiently convert plant-based ALA into EPA and DHA. Dose: roughly 250 mg of combined EPA+DHA per day for an average cat. Use a product made for pets, or a human-grade liquid fish oil without added flavorings or citrus. (Nordic Naturals makes a pet-specific liquid that's widely used.) Check with your vet before starting, especially if your cat has any bleeding disorders or is on medication —fish oil has mild anticoagulant effects.

The Non-Diet Things That Matter

Dental disease accelerates kidney decline. Bacteria from an infected mouth enter the bloodstream continuously —this is called bacteremia —and the kidneys, which filter the blood, bear the brunt of the inflammatory load. Cats with untreated periodontal disease have higher systemic inflammatory markers and are more likely to develop or worsen CKD. A dental cleaning under anesthesia isn't risk-free for a senior cat, but the calculus changes when you factor in what untreated dental disease does to kidneys over two or three years. Discuss the risk-benefit with your vet.

Hypertension (high blood pressure) is both a cause and a consequence of CKD. Damaged kidneys activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, which raises blood pressure. High blood pressure further damages the glomeruli, which worsens CKD, which raises blood pressure further. It's a loop. Cats over 10 should have their blood pressure checked at annual visits —it takes two minutes and a Doppler cuff, and uncontrolled hypertension is a leading cause of acute blindness in cats (retinal detachment from hypertensive retinopathy). Blood pressure medication (amlodipine) is cheap, effective, and well-tolerated in cats.

Urinary tract infections are more common in CKD cats because dilute urine is less bacteriostatic than concentrated urine. A cat with CKD who suddenly declines —stops eating, vomits more, seems painful —may have a UTI tipping them into decompensation, not a progression of the kidney disease itself. Urine culture, not just urinalysis, is the right test here.
