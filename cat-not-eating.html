Why Is My Cat Not Eating?

First decide whether your cat will not eat or cannot eat. Then measure what actually went into the bowl — and what stayed there. | 2026-11-26 · MeltPet · 12 min read

Last updated: 2026-11-26

Do Not Wait for the Bowl to Stay Full for Days

If your cat has not eaten properly for about 24 hours, contact a veterinarian now. Cornell warns that sustained food avoidance can seriously affect a mature cat in as little as 24 hours; for a kitten under six weeks, 12 hours without food can be life-threatening. Reduced intake matters too — a cat eating a few bites is not necessarily eating enough.

Go to an emergency clinic immediately if poor appetite comes with collapse, open-mouth breathing or other breathing difficulty, repeated vomiting, a swollen or painful abdomen, yellow gums or eyes, known or suspected poisoning, a swallowed string or foreign object, or repeated trips to the litter box with little or no urine. Do not pull string from the mouth or anus, do not induce vomiting, and do not give human medicine.

The Two Questions That Organize Everything Else

Question one: does the cat want food but fail to eat it, or has interest in food disappeared? Veterinarians sometimes call the first pattern pseudo-anorexia. Hunger is present, but picking up, chewing, or swallowing is difficult or painful. In true anorexia or hyporexia, appetite itself is absent or reduced.

Question two: how much is the cat actually eating? "She picked at it" is not an amount. Weigh the food before serving and weigh the leftovers, including food another pet may have stolen. Note treats, table food, a second feeder, and food eaten outdoors. A measured 20 grams eaten from 80 grams offered is useful information; an untouched-looking bowl after several people topped it up is not.

Common Causes, Grouped by Mechanism

Cornell describes anorexia as a broad clinical sign rather than a disease. The useful way to sort it is by what is preventing adequate intake.

Pain, especially in the mouth

Periodontal disease, tooth resorption, stomatitis, oral ulcers, a broken tooth, or a jaw problem can make a hungry cat stop after the first bite. Cats often continue eating through oral pain, so a normal appetite yesterday does not rule it out. Drooling, halitosis, face rubbing, chattering, dropping food, or preferring soft textures raises the value of a careful oral examination. A conscious look at the front teeth does not exclude disease farther back or below the gumline.

Nausea and gastrointestinal disease

Hairballs are an easy explanation and a poor default. Gastritis, inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, constipation, ulcers, a foreign body, and gastrointestinal cancer can all reduce appetite. Nausea may look like lip licking, swallowing, drooling, turning away from the bowl, or vomiting — but vomiting is not required. Repeated unproductive retching or a painful, swollen abdomen is urgent.

Illness outside the digestive tract

Kidney disease, liver disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, infection, fever, respiratory disease, heart disease, cancer, and pain anywhere in the body can suppress appetite. This is why replacing the food repeatedly can miss the real problem. In an older cat, increased drinking or urination, weight loss, new night vocalization, or bad breath makes blood and urine testing particularly relevant.

Smell, stress, medication, and the food itself

Smell contributes strongly to feline appetite. Nasal disease can make food less interesting, while a new formula or even a different kibble shape can disrupt an established preference. Boarding, moving, construction, visitors, a new pet, loss of a companion, or another cat guarding the feeding area can do the same. Some medications also reduce appetite or cause nausea.

Stress is a diagnosis to support with a history, not a reason to wait. A new kitten and a vet visit can coincide with kidney disease just as easily as with a simple feeding-location problem.

Why Cats Cannot Simply "Eat When Hungry Enough"

When a cat stops eating, the body mobilizes stored fat for energy. Feline livers can become overwhelmed by that fat load, causing hepatic lipidosis, or fatty liver disease. Overweight cats are at particular risk, but Cornell emphasizes that cats of any age and body condition can be affected.

The trap is that hepatic lipidosis then causes further appetite loss, nausea, lethargy, and sometimes jaundice. The original problem and its complication reinforce each other. Cornell reports that in more than 90 percent of cases it follows another condition or event, including illness, an abrupt unacceptable diet change, stress, boarding, or accidental confinement.

Do not wait for yellow gums or eyes before acting. Those are later warning signs, not the starting line for a vet visit. Equally, do not assume that a few treats or a lick of gravy removes the risk: total calories and protein still matter.

What You Can Safely Try While Arranging Veterinary Advice

These steps are for an alert cat with no emergency signs, ideally after you have called the clinic. They are ways to reduce friction around eating, not a treatment for the underlying cause.

  1. Start with a familiar, complete cat food. Fresh food in a clean bowl is usually a better first offer than a buffet of unfamiliar products. If a therapeutic diet will be needed long term, ask whether the transition can wait until the cat feels better; International Cat Care warns that introducing it during illness can create a lasting aversion.
  2. Offer a small portion. A tablespoon-sized serving can be less overwhelming than a mountain of food. Replace stale food rather than continuously topping it up, and measure what disappears.
  3. Change the room, not just the recipe. Use a quiet, low-traffic place away from litter trays and other pets. Let the cat eat without an audience. Some cats prefer hand feeding or company; others eat better alone overnight.
  4. Try a wide, shallow dish. This prevents sensitive whiskers from repeatedly touching the sides. Ceramic, glass, or stainless steel avoids some odor retention associated with worn plastic, but a familiar preferred bowl is fine.
  5. Adjust aroma cautiously. Slightly warming wet food to about body temperature can release aroma. Stir thoroughly and test for hot spots. If the cat may be nauseated, stronger smells can make matters worse; room-temperature or slightly chilled food may be better.
  6. Keep water available. Wet food contributes water, but it does not solve inadequate calorie intake. Do not force water or food into the mouth.

If your veterinary team approves a small amount of a favorite strong-smelling food to restart interest, use it as a short-term bridge, not a complete diet. Plain chicken or fish lacks the complete nutrient profile a cat needs. Avoid baby food, gravy, and stock unless every ingredient has been checked; onion and garlic are common hidden problems.

Five Common Fixes That Backfire

  • Do not force-feed or syringe-feed on your own. It can create a stronger food aversion and can send food into the airway, causing aspiration pneumonia. AAHA no longer recommends oral syringe feeding because of these risks.
  • Do not wait for a hunger strike to end. Offering only a new diet until the cat "gives in" is especially dangerous for an overweight cat.
  • Do not give human appetite, nausea, or pain medicines. Feline dosing and safety differ, and some human drugs are toxic to cats. Even a veterinary appetite stimulant should follow an assessment.
  • Do not turn treat acceptance into a passing grade. A cat that licks treats but refuses meals is still eating an incomplete and possibly inadequate amount.
  • Do not keep changing everything at once. Ten bowls, ten flavors, hand feeding, warming, and hovering create a confusing experiment and may increase stress. Change one variable and record the result.

What the Vet Needs — and What the Visit May Involve

A useful history often shortens the diagnostic path. Bring the food packaging or a photograph of the label, a list of medicines and supplements, and a short video of the cat approaching or chewing food. Note the last normal meal, measured intake since then, water and litter-box changes, vomiting or retching, weight change, possible toxins or foreign objects, and any household disruption.

The examination usually begins with weight, hydration, temperature, heart and lung assessment, abdominal palpation, and an oral examination. Depending on those findings, the veterinarian may recommend a complete blood count, chemistry and electrolytes, urinalysis, thyroid testing, or imaging such as radiographs or ultrasound. There is no single "not eating" test: the pattern determines the workup.

Treatment can involve pain control, anti-nausea medication, fluids, dental treatment, management of an underlying disease, or removal of an obstruction. An appetite stimulant may help in selected cases, but interest in food is not the finish line. The goal is enough balanced nutrition, measured over the whole day.

A Feeding Tube Is a Bridge, Not a Last Resort

Owners sometimes hear "feeding tube" as evidence that the situation has become hopeless. In feline medicine it is often the opposite: a way to deliver nutrition while the mouth, nausea, or underlying disease is treated, without repeatedly forcing food into a resisting cat.

The 2021 AAHA nutrition guidelines strongly recommend assisted enteral feeding within 72 hours when a hospitalized patient has consumed no more than one third of resting energy requirements, including the time before admission, if feeding techniques and medication do not restore intake. That is a clinical threshold for hospitalized patients, not permission for an owner to wait three days at home.

Short-term tubes can pass through the nose into the esophagus or stomach. Esophagostomy or gastrostomy tubes can support feeding for longer periods. The veterinary team chooses the diet, calculates the amount, monitors electrolytes, and teaches home care. After prolonged inadequate intake, refeeding also needs supervision because electrolyte shifts can cause refeeding syndrome.

Four Situations That Change the Urgency

A kitten

Young kittens have limited reserves. Cornell notes that food avoidance for just 12 hours can be lethal in a kitten under six weeks. Check that the kitten is warm and call a veterinarian promptly; do not improvise a milk formula or force-feed a weak kitten.

An overweight cat on a new diet

Stop treating refusal as a compliance problem. Contact the clinic that recommended the diet and report exactly how much is being eaten. Safe weight loss must be gradual, and a sudden complete refusal can be more dangerous than the original excess weight.

A senior cat or a cat with known kidney disease

Reduced appetite can reflect nausea, dehydration, constipation, dental pain, changing kidney values, medication, or another illness. Do not assume it is the expected next stage of aging or kidney disease. A measured intake log and an updated examination are more useful than switching among renal foods.

A new cat hiding in a quiet home

Stress is plausible, but the response should still be active. Place familiar food, water, and a litter tray in a secure room, keep other animals away, measure intake, and arrange advice if the cat is not eating adequately. Hiding plus not eating is not proof that the problem is only emotional.

The Two-Line Food Log Worth Keeping

For each offer, record time + food and amount served + amount left + other intake and symptoms. For example: "07:30, usual wet food 40 g, 28 g left, ate 12 g; no treats; one small urine clump; hid after sniffing." Repeat at the next meal. A kitchen scale makes this more reliable than describing the bowl as half empty.

Also note the cat's weight on a scale that can resolve small changes. Weighing yourself with and without a cat is too inaccurate for the modest but meaningful losses that matter here. The log gives the veterinary team a trend and prevents a few visible treats from disguising a day of inadequate nutrition.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can a cat go without eating?

There is no safe waiting period that applies to every cat. Cornell notes that sustained food avoidance can seriously affect a mature cat in as little as 24 hours, and that 12 hours can threaten a kitten under six weeks old. Contact a veterinarian promptly when intake changes, and seek urgent care sooner for vomiting, breathing trouble, collapse, possible poisoning, straining without urine, or yellow gums or eyes.

Why does my cat act hungry but walk away from food?

Approaching food and then leaving can happen with nausea, a learned food aversion, dental or mouth pain, difficulty chewing or swallowing, reduced smell, stress, or dislike of the food. The behavior narrows the possibilities but cannot identify the cause by itself. A short video and a measured food log are useful for the veterinary team.

My cat will eat treats but not cat food. Is that okay?

Eating treats shows that the cat can swallow something, but it does not show that the cat is receiving adequate or balanced nutrition. Do not let treat intake hide a continuing drop in total calories. Contact your veterinarian, especially if meal intake remains low or the change persists.

What if my cat is not eating but is still drinking water?

Drinking is helpful but does not remove the risk of inadequate nutrition or hepatic lipidosis. Increased drinking can also accompany kidney disease, diabetes, or hyperthyroidism. Track both food and water changes and arrange veterinary advice rather than waiting for the cat to stop drinking too.

Should I syringe-feed a cat that will not eat?

No, unless your veterinary team has assessed the cat and specifically trained you to do it. Force-feeding can create food aversion and can cause aspiration pneumonia. AAHA no longer recommends oral syringe feeding because of those risks. A veterinary feeding tube is often safer when assisted nutrition is needed.

Will an appetite stimulant fix a cat that is not eating?

An appetite stimulant may be part of treatment, but it is not a diagnosis or a substitute for adequate calories. Nausea, pain, obstruction, dental disease, organ disease, and other causes still need attention. A cat that appears more interested in food may still not eat enough, so actual intake must be measured.
