🐱 Cat Vomiting: What the Color Actually Means

A practical diagnostic guide —from someone who's cleaned up every color on this list. | 2026-09-13 · MeltPet · 13 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-13

Medical disclaimer: This guide is for education, not diagnosis — nothing here replaces a hands-on exam. If your cat is collapsed, breathing hard, continuously retching without producing anything, or has blood in the vomit, skip the rest of this page and call an emergency vet. Vomiting is a clinical sign, not a disease; the color narrows the possibilities, and a vet confirms which one it is.

3:14 AM. I'm on My Hands and Knees With a Paper Towel, Staring at Yellow Foam.

Gus, my tabby, had thrown up again. Third time this month. I'd been telling myself it was just a hairball —cats throw up, right? It's what they do. But this wasn't a hairball. It was yellow bile with a few flecks of undigested kibble, and he'd done it three times in two hours. That's when I called the emergency vet at 3:30 AM, described the color over the phone, and heard something I've never forgotten: "Yellow means empty stomach. The fact that it's yellow is actually good news. It means the obstruction is less likely. But the frequency in one night —that's what I'm concerned about."

That call taught me something that most cat owners never learn: the color of your cat's vomit is a diagnostic tool. Vets use it. ER triage nurses use it. And once you know how to read it, you'll stop panicking over the harmless episodes —and stop dismissing the dangerous ones as "just a hairball."

I spent five years in pet food. I've cleaned up cat vomit in every color on this list, on every surface you can imagine —carpet, hardwood, couch cushions, once directly into a heating vent (that was a long night). Here's what I learned, organized the way a vet thinks about it: by what came out, and what it's telling you.

The Three-Question Triage System (Run This Before You Google)

Before we get into colors, there's a quick framework that ER vets use to decide whether vomiting is a "monitor at home" situation or a "come in now" situation. It's not about the color. It's about three things:

1. How is the cat acting right now? A cat that vomits once and then eats, grooms, and naps —normal. A cat that vomits and then hides under the bed, refuses food, or crouches in a "meatloaf" position (hunched, head down, paws tucked) —that's pain, and it's a vet visit regardless of what came up.

2. Is the cat keeping water down? A cat that drinks water and keeps it down for 2+ hours is not obstructed. A cat that drinks and vomits the water back up within 20 minutes —twice in a row —needs imaging to rule out a foreign body. This is the single most reliable home test for obstruction.

3. What's the frequency? One isolated vomit per month, even if it looks concerning, is almost never an emergency. Three episodes in four hours, even if it's just clear liquid, is worth a call to the vet. Repeated vomiting depletes electrolytes fast in cats —they weigh ten pounds, not two hundred, and they crash faster than you'd think.

Run those three questions first. Then look at the color. The color tells you the why. The triage questions tell you the urgency.

First: Is It Vomiting, or Regurgitation?

These two look almost identical on the floor and mean completely different things. Getting it wrong sends you down the wrong diagnostic path before you've even started —and it's the single most common mix-up I see in owner reports.

Vomiting is active. There's a warning phase: lip-licking, repeated swallowing, drooling, then visible heaving through the belly and ribs. What comes up is partially digested and acidic —yellow or brown, sometimes with bile or foam. Vomiting is a stomach or upper-intestinal event.

Regurgitation is passive. No warning, no abdominal effort. Food and saliva simply slide back out of the esophagus, usually within minutes to a couple of hours of eating, often in an elongated tubular shape coated in thick mucus. Kibble frequently comes back looking almost exactly as it went in. Regurgitation points at the esophagus: eating too fast, a motility problem, or a stricture.

Why the distinction matters: regurgitation doesn't respond to the anti-nausea medication or diet changes that help vomiting, because the stomach was never the problem. The "scarf and barf" version —inhaling food, then bringing whole kibble back up ten minutes later, with no heaving —is the most common thing owners mistake for something serious. The fix is a slow-feeder bowl or scattering the ration across a cookie sheet, not a vet visit.

The exception: regurgitation that happens daily, or that comes with weight loss, needs the esophagus imaged. Passive doesn't automatically mean harmless.

🟡 Yellow / Greenish-Yellow Bile: Empty Stomach, Probably Fine

Yellow vomit is bile —a digestive fluid the liver produces and the gallbladder stores. It means the stomach was empty when the cat vomited. The most common scenario: your cat goes 10–12 hours without food overnight, bile accumulates and irritates the stomach lining, and the stomach contracts to expel it. The cat vomits a small puddle of yellow or greenish-yellow foam, and then goes back to normal. This is especially common in cats fed once a day or free-fed dry food —some cats don't graze enough overnight and end up with an empty, irritated stomach by morning.

The fix: Add a small meal right before bed. Wet food is better here —it stays in the stomach longer than dry. A tablespoon of wet food at 11 PM is usually enough to prevent the 5 AM bile vomit. If your cat is on a prescription diet or you're managing weight, split the daily portion differently —the last meal of the day matters more than the first one for bile-vomiting cats.

When to worry: Yellow vomit more than twice a week despite meal adjustments, or yellow vomit paired with lethargy, hiding, or refusing food. Chronic bilious vomiting can be a symptom of inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, or liver issues —all of which need a vet workup. If the vomit is actually bright green (not yellow-green, but the color of grass or spinach), that suggests bile from further down the small intestine and may indicate a partial obstruction —call the vet.

⚪ Clear Liquid or White Foam: Irritated Stomach, Usually Benign

Clear, watery vomit and white froth are the same story at different concentrations: the stomach is empty and irritated. The white part is air whipped into gastric juice and saliva as the cat heaves. A cat who drains a bowl of cold water after a hard play session and brings up clear liquid ten minutes later has done nothing wrong —the stomach simply overfilled faster than it could empty. So did the cat who licks a patch of floor cleaner and foams once. Suspect something else when clear vomiting starts without any trigger at all: early nausea from pancreatitis or kidney disease, or a foreign body the cat keeps trying to bring up.

White foam is gastric juice mixed with air and saliva. The stomach is irritated but empty —there's nothing to bring up except foam. Common triggers: eating a bug, licking a cleaning product residue off the floor, a plant nibble, or the beginning of a hairball episode (some cats foam before producing the hairball itself). If the cat acts normal afterward —eats, grooms, naps —you can almost always chalk this up to a transient gastric irritation and move on.

However, white foam that happens multiple times in one day —or white foam in a cat over 10 —deserves more attention. Senior cats with early chronic kidney disease often present with "occasional white foam vomiting" that the owner has been dismissing for months. Kidney disease causes uremic gastritis: waste products build up in the blood, irritate the stomach lining, and trigger nausea. The cat foams, you assume it's a hairball, and the kidneys continue deteriorating. If your cat is over 8 and vomiting white foam more than once every few weeks, get bloodwork —our guide to preventing kidney disease in cats covers which numbers to ask for and when screening pays off. A creatinine and SDMA panel costs about $60–$100 and catches kidney decline years before physical symptoms appear.

🍗 Undigested Kibble or Wet Food: Eaten Too Fast

Whole, barely chewed kibble minutes after a meal is usually a mechanical problem, not a disease. Hungry cats inhale food without chewing, the kibble absorbs stomach fluid and expands, and the stretched stomach wall trips the ejection reflex. A slow feeder, a ration scattered across a cookie sheet, or the daily portion split into 4–5 small meals solves most of it —the kibble never had a chance to sit still long enough to swell.

If slow feeding doesn't fix it, the timing tells you which problem you actually have. Food back up 10–15 minutes after eating, still recognizable, is usually regurgitation —an esophageal issue, not a stomach one (see the section above). Food that comes back an hour or more later, already broken down, repeatedly, points more toward food intolerance or early IBD, and that's a diet conversation: one novel protein, 6–8 weeks, no treats, no exceptions.

🟤 Brown Liquid: Food That Didn't Make It —But Check What's in It

Brown vomit is partially digested food. Your cat ate, the food reached the stomach, and something triggered the stomach to eject it before it moved into the small intestine. This is the most common "benign" vomit —the cat ate too fast, the food didn't agree with them, or they were mildly nauseous from a hairball that was forming lower in the digestive tract.

The thing to check: the actual content. Is it just kibble mush? Probably fine. Is there fur mixed in? Could be a hairball that didn't fully form. Is there something in there that shouldn't be —a piece of string, a twist tie, a chunk of a toy? Take a photo. Show it to your vet. Foreign body ingestion is the second most common reason for exploratory surgery in cats, and the earlier it's caught, the less invasive the removal. If the brown vomit has a foul, fecal smell —not just "food smell," but genuinely foul —that indicates the small intestine contents are backing up into the stomach, which can happen with an obstruction. That's an ER visit.

Brown vomit from a cat who's otherwise healthy and normal-acting? Adjust the feeding. Smaller, more frequent meals. Slow-feeder bowls help cats who inhale their food and then regurgitate it ten minutes later —the kibble never even made it to the stomach, it was just sitting in the esophagus. If your cat does the "scarf and barf" routine, a $12 slow feeder or a cookie sheet spread with kibble solves it.

🔴 Red / Pink / Coffee-Ground Specks: Blood. Don't Wait.

Blood in vomit comes in two forms. Bright red blood (hematemesis) means active bleeding somewhere in the esophagus, stomach, or upper small intestine —the blood hasn't been digested yet, so it's still red. Causes range from a small esophageal tear from retching too hard (self-limiting, resolves in 24 hours) to a bleeding gastric ulcer (needs medication) to rat poison ingestion (immediately life-threatening —get to the ER now). You can't tell the difference at home. Bright red blood = vet visit. If there's more than a few specks, or if it's paired with dark, tarry stool (melena —digested blood in the feces), go to the ER.

Coffee-ground specks are digested blood —the blood sat in the stomach long enough for stomach acid to break it down. This usually indicates a slower bleed from a gastric ulcer or erosion. Coffee-ground vomit in a cat who's been on NSAIDs (like Onsior or Metacam) is a drug side effect that requires immediate discontinuation and vet follow-up. Cats are extremely sensitive to NSAIDs —never give a cat ibuprofen, aspirin, or any human pain medication. One tablet of ibuprofen causes acute kidney failure in a cat.

🟢 Neon Green: Two Causes, Both Worth a Call

Green vomit has two very different explanations and you cannot tell them apart by looking. The first is bile that never got acidified because it came from further down the small intestine —a hint that intestinal contents may be backing up, which is what a partial obstruction looks like early on. The second is dye: a lot of rodenticide bait is deliberately dyed green or blue as a safety marker, some antifreeze is green, and a cat who chews a treated houseplant brings up dyed plant matter.

If there's any chance your cat got into bait, antifreeze, or a lily, read the color as a toxin signal and go to the emergency clinic —bring a photo of the vomit plus the container or the plant. And yes, eating grass produces green vomit too, and that version is harmless. The question is never "is it green," it's "could my cat have swallowed something dyed, toxic, or blocking?"

The Hairball Trap: What Most Owners Get Wrong

A true hairball is a cigar-shaped tube of packed fur with a small amount of liquid. It looks like a furry thumbs length. Your cat retches a few times, produces it, and then acts completely normal —eats, grooms, plays. That's a hairball. It's not an emergency. It happens every few weeks for most cats, more often for long-haired breeds.

What is not a hairball: vomit that contains some fur mixed in with food and liquid. If the substance on your floor is mostly liquid or food with a little fur in it, that's vomit —your cat happened to have some fur in its stomach when it vomited for a completely different reason. I see owners dismiss real vomiting as "just a hairball" for months. The cat has food intolerance or early IBD, and every episode is written off because there's a little fur in the puddle. By the time they bring the cat in, it's lost a pound and a half —which on a ten-pound cat is 15% of body weight. That's the equivalent of a 180-pound human losing 27 pounds without trying.

If your cat is "producing hairballs" more than twice a month, it's probably not hairballs. It's vomiting. Track the frequency. Write down dates and what came up. A log of 8 "hairballs" in 3 months is more useful to your vet than a photo of one.

A note on hairball remedies: petroleum-based laxatives (Laxatone, Petromalt) work by coating the fur in the stomach so it slides through instead of forming a ball. They're safe for occasional use. But giving them weekly to a cat who's actually vomiting from food intolerance masks the real problem. Hairball diets (high fiber) help some cats, but if your cat is vomiting from IBD, adding fiber can make things worse. Try it for two weeks. If the "hairballs" don't decrease, it's not a hairball problem.

🚩 Five Red Flags That Skip the Waiting Room

The cheat sheet below covers the everyday cases, where color and timing decide whether you wait or call. These five are different —they don't need a color analysis, they need a car ride.

1. Three or more episodes in 12 hours. Frequency outranks appearance. A ten-pound cat carries almost no fluid reserve for repeated vomiting, and the electrolytes go with the fluid.

2. Retching without producing anything —repeatedly. Crouched low, heaving, nothing comes up, over and over. This is the classic presentation of a linear foreign body (thread, ribbon, tinsel, hair tie anchored somewhere in the gut) or of a twisted stomach. This one does not resolve on its own, and waiting is the thing that makes it worse.

3. Vomiting plus diarrhea. Fluid is leaving from both ends at once. Small animals dehydrate far faster than people expect —a cat can go from "a bit off" to shock inside 24 hours.

4. Vomiting plus straining to urinate, especially in a male cat. A urethral blockage kills within 24–48 hours. It's the most common "I wish I'd come in sooner" cat emergency there is. If your cat is in and out of the box producing nothing, that's not constipation.

5. Known access to a toxin or a string. True lilies —any part, pollen included —cause fatal acute kidney failure in cats. Antifreeze, rodenticide, and sewing thread belong in the same bucket. Call the clinic before you do anything else, and don't try to induce vomiting at home.

One more, because it's easy to miss: the "meatloaf" posture —hunched, head down, paws tucked, no interest in a treat. That posture is pain. Add vomiting and it's a same-day visit no matter what color it was.

What You Can Do at Home (That Actually Helps)

For the one-off vomit: Rest the stomach for 2–3 hours, not longer. Cats are not dogs here —the "fast for 12 to 24 hours" advice that circulates for dogs is genuinely risky for cats, because a cat that stops eating for more than two or three days can develop hepatic lipidosis, a potentially fatal liver condition. Keep water available the whole time, unless your cat vomits water straight back up within twenty minutes, in which case call your vet rather than withholding more. After the rest, offer a teaspoon of bland food: plain boiled chicken breast, no seasoning, shredded small. If it stays down for an hour, offer a little more, then move to small portions every 3–4 hours instead of one big bowl. Transition back to normal food over 3–5 days once the stomach has fully settled.

For the chronic vomiter (weekly episodes, no red flags): Start with diet. Food intolerance is the #1 cause of chronic low-grade vomiting in cats, and it's almost never the protein you'd expect —it's usually a thickener, a preservative, or a plant protein filler in cheap food. Switch to a limited-ingredient diet with a single novel protein (rabbit, duck, venison) for 6–8 weeks. No treats. No table scraps. No "just a little bit" of the old food. A true elimination trial. If the vomiting stops during those 8 weeks, you've identified the trigger. If it doesn't, the next step is bloodwork and possibly an abdominal ultrasound. You've ruled out diet, so the cause is likely medical.

Don't self-medicate. Human antacids (Pepcid, Prilosec) are sometimes prescribed for cats at specific, weight-adjusted doses —but the dosing is not intuitive. A 10-pound cat gets roughly 2.5 mg of famotidine (Pepcid), which is one-sixth of the human tablet. Guessing the dose is dangerous. Let your vet prescribe.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does yellow cat vomit mean?

Yellow vomit is bile —a digestive fluid produced by the liver and stored in the gallbladder. It means your cat threw up on an empty stomach. The most common cause is going too long between meals: the stomach empties, bile accumulates and irritates the stomach lining, and the cat vomits. This is especially common in cats fed once or twice a day. The fix is usually simple: add a small meal or snack before bed so the stomach isn't empty for 8–10 hours overnight. If your cat vomits yellow bile more than twice a week despite meal adjustments, or if the vomiting is paired with lethargy, hiding, or refusing food, see a vet —chronic bilious vomiting can indicate inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, or liver issues.

When should I take my cat to the vet for vomiting?

Go to the emergency vet immediately if you see: blood in the vomit (bright red or coffee-ground dark specks), a string or foreign object in the vomit or hanging from the mouth, repeated vomiting over 2–3 hours with no water kept down, vomiting paired with straining to urinate (especially in male cats —this is a urethral blockage and fatal within 24–48 hours), or a cat that can't stand, has pale or grey gums, or has a heart rate over 220 BPM. Call your regular vet within 24 hours if: the vomiting has persisted for more than 24 hours, your cat is eating less or hiding more than usual, the vomit is consistently bright green (not yellow —actual green suggests intestinal contents backing up), or your cat has known kidney disease, diabetes, or hyperthyroidism. One isolated vomit with no other symptoms, where the cat eats normally afterward and behaves normally, is almost never an emergency regardless of what color it was.

What's the difference between cat vomit and a hairball?

A hairball is a cigar-shaped tube of packed fur, usually with a small amount of liquid. Actual vomit is mostly liquid or food with or without fur mixed in. The distinction matters because people dismiss real vomiting as 'just a hairball' for months —and the underlying condition (food intolerance, IBD, hyperthyroidism) progresses untreated. If what your cat brings up is mostly food or liquid with some fur in it, that's vomit with fur, not a hairball. True hairballs are more fur than anything else and the cat acts completely normal before and after. If your cat is retching repeatedly without producing anything, that's also concerning —it can indicate an obstruction, not a hairball.

Why is my cat throwing up white foam?

White foam is gastric juice mixed with air and saliva —essentially, the stomach is irritated but empty. The most common causes: gastritis from eating something they shouldn't have (a plant, a bug, a piece of plastic), a food intolerance that's been simmering for weeks or months and finally triggered an episode, or hairball prelude —cats sometimes foam before producing a hairball. Occasional isolated white foam episodes —once every few weeks, with the cat otherwise normal —are usually benign. Foam plus hiding, refusal to eat, or lethargy is a vet visit. Foam multiple times in one day is a vet visit. White foam in a cat over 10, even if everything else seems normal, warrants bloodwork to rule out early chronic kidney disease, which is notorious for causing subtle nausea that owners dismiss as 'just an occasional hairball.'

How can I tell if my cat is vomiting or regurgitating?

Vomiting is active —you'll see lip-licking, repeated swallowing, and visible heaving through the belly and ribs before anything comes up, and what comes up is partially digested and often yellow or brown. Regurgitation is passive —no warning, no effort, and food or saliva simply slides out, usually within minutes to a couple of hours of eating, often in a tubular shape coated in mucus with kibble still recognizable. Regurgitation points at the esophagus (eating too fast, a motility problem, a stricture) and won't respond to the diet changes or anti-nausea measures that help vomiting. If your cat regurgitates daily, or is losing weight, the esophagus needs imaging.

Should I withhold food after my cat vomits?

Rest the stomach for 2–3 hours, then offer a small amount of bland food —plain boiled chicken, no seasoning, shredded small. Do not follow the 12-to-24-hour fasting advice written for dogs: a cat that stops eating for more than two or three days can develop hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver condition, so a prolonged fast is a real risk rather than a safe default. Keep water available throughout unless your cat vomits water straight back up within twenty minutes —that's a reason to call the vet, not to withhold more.
