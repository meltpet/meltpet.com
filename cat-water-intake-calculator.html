Calculate Your Cat's Water Needs

A standard 3 oz can holds about 85 g, so two cans is roughly 170 g. If you free-feed dry food, weigh one day's scoop once and use that number.

How Much Water Does a Cat Actually Need?

The working number in feline medicine is 40 to 60 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day. For the average 5 kg cat — about 11 pounds — that is 200 to 300 ml, or roughly 7 to 10 ounces. It is the same figure whether you arrive at it from body weight or from calorie intake: run the numbers on a 10 lb cat and the two methods agree at about 180 to 270 ml, which is the same target reached from two different directions.

Notice that it is a range, not a target, and that the range is wide on purpose. It is not a rounding artifact. Where your cat sits inside that 40–60 band is decided mostly by what it eats — the lower end is a cat on wet food, the higher end is a cat on nothing but dry kibble. That single fact is what this calculator is built around.

Below is the total daily water requirement by body weight. These are the total figures — food plus bowl — not the amount you need to pour.

3 kg (6.6 lb)120–180 ml4–6 oz
4 kg (8.8 lb)160–240 ml5–8 oz
5 kg (11 lb)200–300 ml7–10 oz
6 kg (13.2 lb)240–360 ml8–12 oz
7 kg (15.4 lb)280–420 ml9–14 oz

Total daily water at 40–60 ml per kg. Multiply your cat's weight in kilogrammes by 40 for the low end and by 60 for the high end. This assumes a healthy adult cat at a normal body condition score; a cat with kidney disease, diabetes, or hyperthyroidism is a different calculation and belongs with your vet.

One caveat worth stating plainly, because it is where most online advice goes wrong: this is a requirement, not an observation. It is what the body needs. It is not a promise that your cat is drinking it. Those are two different measurements, and the second one is the one that matters on any given day.

Where the Water Actually Comes From — Food First

Cats evolved as desert hunters that took their water from prey, and prey is roughly 70% water — a mouse is a meal and a drink at the same time. A cat has never needed to seek out standing water, and the thirst drive reflects it. Modern food broke that arrangement, and the size of the break depends entirely on the format you choose.

Two numbers explain almost everything about feline hydration:

  • Dry food is about 8% moisture. Some kibbles run as low as 6% and some as high as 10%, but 8% is the working figure. A cat eating 60 g of dry food a day gets roughly 5 ml of water from the food. Essentially nothing.
  • Wet food is about 78% water. A cat eating 200 g of wet food a day — roughly two standard cans — gets about 156 ml from the food, which is more than half of a 5 kg cat's daily requirement.

Put those two side by side, against the same 5 kg cat with the same 200–300 ml total requirement, and the shape of the problem appears:

What you feedMoistureWater from foodLeft to drink
60 g dry food~8%~5 ml195–295 ml
200 g wet food78%~156 ml44–144 ml

Both rows for a 5 kg cat with a 200–300 ml total requirement. The "left to drink" column is the total minus the water the food already supplies.

That is the whole calculation, and it is why two cats of identical weight can have completely different drinking requirements. The dry-fed cat has to find 195 to 295 ml from a bowl, every single day, by choice. The wet-fed cat is already most of the way there before it touches the water.

Why Dry-Fed Cats Are the Ones at Risk

Everything above sounds like arithmetic until you add the one behavioural fact that makes it a welfare issue: cats have a weak thirst drive. A dog that is short on water gets thirsty and drinks. A cat that is short on water often just stays short, because the instinct to seek water was never strongly selected for in an animal whose meals came pre-hydrated.

The practical consequence is a number that surprises most owners. A dry-fed cat has to drink roughly four times as much water as a wet-fed cat does, from a bowl, voluntarily, every day, to reach the same total. Not four times as often — four times as much volume. And it has to do it while running a thirst system that is not especially interested in helping.

This is why the dry-versus-wet question keeps coming back to the kidneys. Chronic kidney disease is the most common cause of illness and death in older cats, and roughly 75% of kidney function is gone before symptoms appear — the bloodwork that catches it early is measuring a system that has already lost most of its reserve. Chronic dehydration is one of the few levers an owner can actually pull, because the other risk factors are age and genetics.

The honest version of the fountain question belongs here too. A pet water fountain does help — cats instinctively prefer running water, and many drink more from one; a stainless steel fountain is also easier to clean than a plastic one and less prone to bacterial biofilm — but be clear about the size of the effect: a fountain increases drinking at the margins. You can place three fountains in every room and a dry-food cat will still usually drink less than a wet-food cat with a single bowl. The lever that moves the number is food moisture. Fans and fountains are supporting tools, not the fix.

If you have ruled out kidney disease and your cat is simply a dry-food addict, the highest-leverage change is not a better bowl. It is a slower one: transition part of the daily ration to wet food, or add water or broth to the kibble, and let the arithmetic above do the work for you. And if you are leaving her for the day, the arithmetic gets sharper rather than looser — the setup for leaving a dry-fed cat alone is mostly about giving her more than one place to find that water.

Reading Your Result: What the Number Means

The calculator gives you two numbers that matter. The first is total daily water. The second — the one people actually came for — is what is left to drink after the food has been counted.

There is no single "good" answer, because a low drinking requirement is not a low requirement. It means the food is already carrying the water. Here is how to read the three diet patterns:

  • Wet food only. The left-to-drink number is small — often 40 to 150 ml on a normal-sized cat. That is the diet working as intended. It does not mean your cat is fine and you can stop paying attention; it means the bowl is not the problem.
  • Dry food only. The left-to-drink number is large, and it is almost the entire requirement. This is the pattern most associated with chronic low-grade dehydration, and the one worth changing if you can.
  • A mix. You land somewhere in between, in proportion to how much of the diet is wet. Shifting even one meal a day from dry to wet moves the number meaningfully, because wet food is close to 80% water by weight.

One thing the calculator cannot tell you is what your cat actually drank today. If you want that number, measure it directly: fill a measured bowl each morning, top it up from a marked jug through the day, and note how much you added. Do it for three days. Cats are creatures of habit, and a stable three-day average is more informative than any single day — especially because the interesting signal is not the absolute number but a change from your cat's own baseline.

When the Number Does Not Fit: Drinking Too Much or Too Little

The calculator describes a healthy cat. Two patterns fall outside it, and both are reasons to call a vet rather than adjust the food.

Drinking a lot more than the maths predicts is the more important of the two, because it is often the first visible sign of a disease. Vets hear it constantly — "she has been drinking a lot lately" — and it is exactly the complaint that triggers the bloodwork which catches chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, or diabetes early. A cat that suddenly empties a bowl that used to last two days is not being dramatic. If the number you calculated is far below what the bowl says, the bowl is the more trustworthy of the two.

Drinking much less, or stopping entirely, is the acute version. A cat that stops drinking and stops eating for more than 24 hours is at real risk, and a cat that drinks and then vomits the water back up within 20 minutes, twice in a row, is absorbing nothing at all. That is a same-day vet call.

There is also a weight-based checkpoint that is worth memorising, because it is the fastest at-home signal available. A drop of about 5% of body weight is roughly where mild dehydration begins — and because cats are small, that is a surprisingly small amount. On a 4 kg cat it is 200 g; on a 5 kg cat it is 250 g. Between 6% and 8% loss is moderate, and that is usually where a vet starts talking about subcutaneous fluids. A digital pet scale that reads in 10 g increments turns this from a guess into a measurement.

Nothing on this page replaces a vet visit for a cat that is actually unwell. The calculator is for healthy cats and for owners trying to get the daily prevention right before there is a problem.

Signs of Dehydration to Watch For

The calculator describes a healthy cat. The list below is what to check when you suspect this one is not. None of these is a diagnosis on its own — they are the observations that tell you whether the change you noticed is worth a phone call.

  • A drop in water intake. This is the signal the whole page is built around, and it is the one cats are worst at making obvious. A dry-fed cat has to drink almost its entire requirement, so a bowl that suddenly stops emptying is the clearest at-home evidence that something has changed. A cat that stops drinking and stops eating for more than 24 hours belongs at a vet that day, not on a wait-and-see.
  • Dry or tacky gums. Lift the upper lip and run a fingertip along the gum line. A hydrated gum is slick and glossy, like the inside of your cheek; a dehydrated one is tacky — it grips your finger slightly and looks dull rather than wet. This is the check most owners never try, and it is the one that tends to catch a cat before the scale does. One caveat: gums that are pale, grey, or blue are a circulation sign rather than a hydration one, and they are an emergency on their own.
  • Lethargy — a cat that is quieter, hiding more, or less interested than usual. Cats hide illness, so a drop in activity is often the first thing an owner notices and frequently shows up before the drinking habit visibly changes. On its own it is vague; read alongside a shrinking bowl or skin that recoils slowly, it stops being vague.
  • Vomiting or diarrhoea that will not stay down. Fluid lost through the gut cannot be replaced at the bowl — a cat that is vomiting or has diarrhoea for more than 12 hours is losing water faster than it can drink it, and water that comes straight back up is a same-day call rather than a tomorrow one.

The order matters less than the trend. Any one of these in isolation may be nothing; two of them together, or any one of them alongside the 5% weight drop above, is a reason to stop reading and call the clinic. Weight is still the most reliable check you have at home, because it is the only one that gives you a number instead of an impression — which is exactly why the full dehydration test guide ranks it first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should a cat drink per day?

Roughly 40 to 60 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day, which is about 200 to 300 ml for a 5 kg cat. But the useful answer depends on the food. A cat on wet food gets most of that from the food and may only need 44 to 144 ml from a bowl, while a dry-fed cat has to drink almost the entire 195 to 295 ml. So "how much should my cat drink" is really two questions: what does the body need, and how much of it does the food already supply.

Do cats on dry food drink more water to compensate?

They need to drink roughly four times as much as a wet-fed cat — about 195 to 295 ml from a bowl for a 5 kg cat, versus 44 to 144 ml on wet food. Whether they actually do it is the open question. Cats have a weak thirst drive, so a dry-fed cat often runs at a mild deficit rather than making up the difference, which is why dry-food-only diets are associated with chronic low-grade dehydration and why wet food is the lever vets reach for first.

Does a water fountain actually make a cat drink more?

Often yes, and it is a cheap experiment worth running, because cats instinctively prefer moving water. But be clear-eyed about the size of the effect: a fountain increases drinking at the margins. It does not close the gap between a dry-fed cat and a wet-fed cat. The lever that moves the number is food moisture; bowl placement, bowl material, and fountains all help around the edges.

Is wet food better than dry food for hydration?

For hydration specifically, yes, and it is not close. Wet food is about 78% water, so a 200 g daily ration supplies roughly 156 ml — more than half of a 5 kg cat's total requirement. Dry food is about 8% moisture and supplies about 5 ml from a 60 g ration. That is not a small gap. It is the difference between a cat that mostly drinks water and a cat that mostly eats it.

How can I tell if my cat is dehydrated?

The fastest at-home check is weight: a loss of about 5% of body weight is roughly where mild dehydration begins, which on a 4 kg cat is only 200 g and on a 5 kg cat is 250 g. Between 6% and 8% is moderate and usually means a vet conversation about fluids. Other checks — skin elasticity, gum moisture, capillary refill — tell you about the moment rather than the trend. A kitchen scale that reads in 10 g steps is the most reliable tool most owners can actually use.

My cat suddenly started drinking much more. Should I worry?

Yes, take it seriously — it is one of the most useful early signals there is. Increased drinking is a classic first sign of chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, and diabetes in cats, and it often appears before any other visible symptom. "Drinking a lot lately" is frequently the exact sentence that triggers the bloodwork which catches these conditions early, when there is still something to be done. If the bowl suddenly lasts half as long as it used to, book the appointment rather than adjusting the diet.

Should I add water to my cat's dry food?

It is one of the simplest ways to shift the number, and many cats accept it once they get used to it. Start by adding a small amount of warm water or unsalted broth to a portion and reduce the dry volume slightly so the total calorie intake stays the same, since soaked kibble is heavier and a cat fed the same weight of dry matter will be getting the same calories in a wetter package. If your cat refuses, moving one meal a day to wet food accomplishes the same thing more reliably.
