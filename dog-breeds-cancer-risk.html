🎗️ Dog Breeds With the Highest Cancer Risk Ranked by the Studies That Actually Counted

One breed comes out at 42% and 63.8% in two credible studies. Here is why. | 2026-09-30 · MeltPet · 11 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-30

Medical disclaimer: This page is about population-level statistics, not diagnosis, and nothing here tells you anything about your individual dog. Breed risk figures are averages across thousands of dogs and hundreds of breeding lines. For anything about your own dog's health, talk to a veterinarian who has examined them.

The Baseline Nobody Gives You First

Every "most cancer-prone dog breeds" list has the same defect: it ranks breeds against each other and never tells you what the average is. So let's set that first, because without it the whole exercise is meaningless.

Cancer accounts for roughly 30% of deaths across all dogs. That figure comes from a 2018 comparative-oncology review in Pharmacology & Therapeutics, drawing on the largest North American mortality dataset (Fleming, Creevy & Promislow, 2011), which put it at 27.2% for purebred dogs and 27.6% for mixed-breed dogs. A separate British study of 15,881 pedigree deaths found cancer as the cause in 27%.

So the honest framing of this entire topic is not "which breeds get cancer". It is which breeds sit meaningfully above one in three, and by how much. A breed at 9% is not a cancer breed. A breed at 58.3% is a different animal entirely, and the gap between those two numbers is the only thing worth ranking.

One more piece of context, because it reframes the emotional weight of the question. Neoplasia is the leading cause of death in 70 of 81 dog breeds studied. Cancer is not the exception in dogs. It is close to being the norm — which is precisely why 30% is the number to hold in your head rather than the exception.

Two things to take from this table before you read any other list. Same Breed, Two Numbers: 42% and 63.8%

The single most useful thing you can learn about breed cancer statistics is how unstable they are. The Flat-Coated Retriever is the cleanest example, because it has been studied more than once.

A Dutch study found malignant tumours associated with 63.8% of Flat-Coated Retriever deaths. A British owner survey recorded cancer as the cause in 54.3% of 610 reported deaths. And a UK cohort study that followed 174 individual dogs from 1996 to 2007 found 42% died from confirmed neoplasia, with another 11.6% dying of unconfirmed tumours.

That is a spread of nearly twenty-two percentage points for one breed. All three studies are legitimate. They disagree because of four things:

Whose deaths were counted. A breed-club membership in the Netherlands, a UK postal survey, and a research cohort that recruited specific dogs are three different windows onto the same breed, and breed populations differ by country.

How hard anyone looked. The 42% figure is confirmed neoplasia — cases with histopathology. The higher figures include clinical diagnoses without laboratory confirmation. Requiring proof lowers the number, and that is not a flaw; it is the difference between what is known and what is assumed.

How small the sample was. The 174-dog cohort is small enough that a dozen dogs move the percentage by seven points. The Bernese figures show the same pattern from the other direction: a Swiss cohort of 381 dogs gave 58.3%, a Dutch population gave 55.1%. Similar, but not the same, and both are more trustworthy than either alone.

What was counted as the cause of death. Some studies record the immediate cause, some record the underlying disease, and some record every condition present at death. The Swiss Bernese study could not establish a cause in 23.4% of the cases at all — nearly a quarter of the dataset was simply unknown.

The practical consequence: when a list gives you one precise number for a breed without a source, treat it as decoration. When it gives you a range and names the studies, you can actually reason with it.

A Different Question: How Much More Likely Than Average

The second kind of breed data asks a completely different question. Instead of "what share of this breed dies of cancer", it asks "does this breed account for more cancer deaths than its share of the dog population would predict". A ratio above 1.0 means over-represented; below 1.0 means under-represented.

This is the most-cited breed table in canine oncology research, derived from a UK owner-based questionnaire — it runs from 2.9× at the top down to 0× at the bottom. The selection below keeps every ratio this page goes on to discuss, so nothing claimed further down is unverifiable here. Now put the two tables side by side. The Airedale Terrier appears at 40% of deaths in one and at 0.3× — strongly under-represented — in the other. Same breed. The Irish Setter is at 41% in one, 0.3× in the other. They are not contradicting each other; they are answering different questions. A breed can have a high share of cancer deaths simply because it is long-lived and dies of old-age diseases, while still accounting for fewer cancer deaths than its popularity would predict.

This is why a page that lists "the most cancer-prone breeds" without saying which metric it used has told you almost nothing.

The Size Pattern That Explains Most of the Ranking

Look past the breed names and a single variable does most of the work.

In the cause-of-death data, the breeds where cancer accounts for more than 40% of deaths — Bernese Mountain Dog, Golden Retriever, Scottish Terrier, Bouvier des Flandres, Boxer, Bullmastiff, Irish Setter, Airedale Terrier — have an average weight of roughly 31 kg. The breeds where cancer accounts for under 10% — Maltese, Dachshund, Pekingese, Pomeranian, Chihuahua, Miniature Dachshund, Miniature Pinscher — average roughly 5 kg.

Cancer is overwhelmingly a disease of age, large dogs age faster and die younger, and body size in dogs is largely controlled by a single gene region. That means a large part of "which breeds get cancer" is really "which breeds are big", dressed up as breed identity.

The research is explicit that the two cannot be cleanly separated, and this is where most breed lists go wrong. They attribute to a breed what actually belongs to body mass. It also produces the counter-intuitive result that the giant breeds with genuinely grim cancer reputations — Bernese, Irish Wolfhound — may look worse than they are because their short lifespans compress everything into fewer years.

The Breeds These Lists Usually Get Wrong

German Shepherd. Ask most dog owners which large breeds are cancer-prone and the German Shepherd comes up early. The data says the opposite: 0.8× in the over-representation table — meaningfully below average. It does have a signature tumour, haemangiosarcoma, and its hip dysplasia risk is very real at roughly 20% of the breed. But cancer burden is not the breed's defining health problem, and the reputation has outrun the evidence.

Bulldog. Appears at 0.4× — apparently one of the safest breeds for cancer. This is almost certainly an artefact, and the researchers say so directly: a breed that dies young of other things accumulates fewer cancer deaths by simple arithmetic, because cancer needs years to develop. A low cancer figure in a short-lived breed is not reassurance. It is a different cause of death winning the race.

Dachshund. At 0.2× it is genuinely low for cancer — and it is a perfect illustration of why one risk number is never the whole story. Roughly one in four Dachshunds develops IVDD, and a Dachshund that has had one disc rupture has about a 30% chance of a second within two years. It is a low-cancer breed with a severe, breed-defining spinal risk. Which lottery you prefer is a real question; only one of them shows up in the cancer tables.

Golden Retriever. This one the reputational lists get right, and it is the only breed here with a purpose-built longitudinal study following thousands of dogs across their entire lives. The cost of that burden is on the money side: our insurance breakdown prices a lymphoma chemotherapy protocol at $6,000–$12,000, and the Golden Retriever guide treats insurance as non-optional for the breed rather than as a choice.

What Actually Changes the Odds

There is no diet, supplement or lifestyle change that prevents cancer in a predisposed breed, and anyone selling one is selling you something. Three things do have evidence or breed-level reasoning behind them.

Body condition. This is the lever our own breed guides put first, for good reason, and it is the one with the strongest general evidence. Roughly 60% of Labradors seen by vets are overweight or obese, and the Labrador carries a gene variant that means about a quarter of the breed genuinely does not register fullness. Excess weight drives orthopaedic disease directly, and chronic inflammation and obesity are associated with elevated cancer risk across species. Keeping a predisposed breed lean through its whole life is the single highest-value thing an owner controls.

Breeding-line history, asked properly. Cancer clusters in families, not in breeds. When you talk to a breeder, the useful question is not "does the breed get cancer" but "what did the grandparents, aunts and uncles die of, and how old were they". A breeder who cannot answer that question is telling you something. A breeder who can, and who has lost dogs young, is telling you something more useful than any breed statistic on this page.

Screening matched to the breed's signature tumour. Not screening for "cancer" vaguely, but knowing which one this breed actually gets and asking your vet to look for that. Our kidney disease guide makes the same argument for cats with SDMA versus creatinine: the value of a screening test is entirely in how early it moves the decision. Senior bloodwork and imaging for a Bernese Mountain Dog at six are not the same protocol as for a Standard Poodle at nine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which dog breed has the highest cancer risk?

On the cause-of-death studies — the ones that actually counted how dogs died — the Flat-Coated Retriever and the Bernese Mountain Dog are consistently at the top. A Dutch study found deaths associated with malignant tumours in at least 63.8% of Flat-Coated Retrievers and 55.1% of Bernese Mountain Dogs. A Swiss study of 381 Bernese Mountain Dogs found neoplasia was the most frequent cause of death in 58.3% of them. For context, cancer accounts for roughly 30% of deaths across all dogs, so these breeds sit well above the baseline.

Do Golden Retrievers really have a 60% cancer rate?

That figure measures something different from the breed rankings people compare it to. Approximately 60% is a lifetime risk of developing cancer, from the Morris Animal Foundation's Golden Retriever Lifetime Study. The cause-of-death figures for other breeds measure what dogs died of. Developing a cancer and dying of it are not the same number, and a develop figure is always higher. When one review grouped breeds by share of deaths, the Golden Retriever came out around 50% — same breed, a lower number, because it is answering a different question.

Why does the same breed have different cancer rates in different studies?

Three reasons. First, the populations differ — a Swiss breed-club cohort, a Dutch owner-and-vet survey and a British insurance database are not the same dogs, and breed popularity varies by country. Second, the definitions differ — a confirmed tumour on histopathology, an unconfirmed clinical diagnosis and a questionnaire answer from an owner are three different levels of evidence. Third, the samples are often small: one flat-coated retriever cohort was 174 dogs, where a handful of extra cases moves the percentage several points. A range is honest. A single precise number is usually not.

Which dog breeds have the lowest cancer risk?

Small breeds, consistently. In one analysis of cause-of-death data, the breeds with the lowest share of cancer deaths were the Maltese and Dachshund at 9%, the Pekingese and Pomeranian and Chihuahua at 8%, the Miniature Dachshund at 6% and the Miniature Pinscher at 4% — against a baseline of roughly 30% across all dogs. The breeds above 40% averaged about 31 kg; the breeds below 10% averaged about 5 kg. Size, not breed identity, explains much of the pattern. But low cancer risk is not low health risk: the Dachshund's real lottery ticket is spinal disease, not tumours.

Does keeping my dog at a healthy weight reduce cancer risk?

Body condition is one of the few levers with real evidence behind it, and it is the factor our own breed guides put first — particularly for the Labrador Retriever, where roughly 60% of dogs seen by vets are overweight or obese. Beyond that: ask breeders about grandparents, aunts and uncles rather than parents alone, because cancer clusters in lines rather than in individuals. And screen for the breed's signature cancer rather than for cancer in general — a Bernese Mountain Dog and a Standard Poodle do not need the same tests.

Is a breed cancer ranking useful for my own dog?

Only as a prior, and a weak one. Breed figures are population averages across thousands of dogs from many different breeding lines. Two litters of the same breed from different lines can carry very different cancer burdens, which is why a breed-level percentage cannot tell you about the animal in front of you. What it can do is tell you what to screen for and how seriously to take insurance. If a breed figure makes you buy insurance before a diagnosis exists, it has done its job.
