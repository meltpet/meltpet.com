🦷 Dog Dental Care Guide

A $15 toothbrush habit vs. a $1,500 dental extraction. | 2026-06-08 · MeltPet · 4 min read

Last updated: 2026-06-26

My Vet Held Up a Tooth. It Was Loose. My Dog Hadn't Complained Once.

Dogs don't show dental pain the way humans do. They don't stop eating. They don't paw at their mouth. They just chew on the other side. Or swallow their kibble whole. Or adapt in some quiet way that you won't notice until the vet lifts their lip and shows you gums so inflamed they bleed at the touch of a probe.

I knew about dog dental disease, intellectually. But I wasn't brushing my dog's teeth. Nobody I knew brushed their dog's teeth. It felt like one of those things —like flossing —that you know you should do and don't. Then my vet showed me a loose premolar on my four-year-old dog and told me a dental with extractions would run $900–$1,200 depending on how many teeth had to come out. I bought a toothbrush on the way home. This guide is what I wish I'd known before that conversation.

The Numbers That Should Scare You

By age three, 80% of dogs have some degree of periodontal disease. By age six, it's most of them. Periodontal disease starts as plaque —a biofilm of bacteria, saliva, and food particles that hardens into tartar within 24–48 hours. Tartar below the gumline triggers inflammation. Inflammation destroys the periodontal ligament and the bone that holds teeth in place.

This isn't a cosmetic problem. The bacteria from an infected mouth enter the bloodstream and damage the heart, liver, and kidneys. Dogs with untreated dental disease live shorter lives. The study that gets quoted most in vet school: dogs who received regular dental care lived roughly 2–3 years longer than those who didn't, with the effect being most pronounced in small breeds —who are also the most prone to dental disease.

A professional dental cleaning under anesthesia costs $500–$1,500 depending on your location and whether extractions are needed. Most dogs need one every 2–3 years even with good home care. Without home care, it's annual —and the extractions pile up.

Brushing: The Only Thing That Actually Works

Dental chews help. Water additives help. Prescription dental diets help. None of them replace mechanical plaque removal —brushing. The mechanical action of bristles against tooth surface is what disrupts the biofilm before it mineralizes into tartar. Enzymatic toothpaste helps, but the brushing itself is what matters. Your goal is to brush the outside surfaces of the teeth —the side facing the cheek —because that's where plaque accumulates fastest and the tongue doesn't naturally clean it. The inside surfaces (tongue side) get some natural cleaning from saliva and tongue movement. If you only have 30 seconds, do the outsides of the upper molars and canines. That's where disease starts.

Start slow. Day 1: let the dog lick enzymatic toothpaste off your finger. Day 2–3: rub your finger along the outside of the gums with toothpaste on it. Day 4–5: introduce the toothbrush —let the dog sniff it, lick toothpaste off it. Day 6–7: brush for 10 seconds, reward heavily, stop before the dog gets annoyed. Week 2: work up to 30 seconds per side. By week 3, you should be able to do a full 60-second brushing session without the dog fighting you.

What to buy: a soft-bristled dog toothbrush or a finger brush. CET enzymatic toothpaste in poultry or beef flavor —dogs like the taste and the enzymes continue working after brushing. Human toothpaste is not safe for dogs. It contains fluoride and sometimes xylitol, both toxic.

How often: daily is ideal. Every other day is the minimum for meaningful prevention. Once a week is better than nothing but won't prevent tartar buildup on its own —you're slowing the accumulation, not stopping it.

The Stuff That Helps (But Doesn't Replace Brushing)

VOHC-approved dental chews. The Veterinary Oral Health Council puts its seal on products that actually reduce plaque and tartar in clinical trials. Look for the VOHC seal on the package. Greenies, OraVet chews, and Virbac C.E.T. chews all have it. The chews that don't have the seal might still help —they might also be expensive treats with no dental benefit. The seal is the differentiator.

Prescription dental diets. Hill's t/d and Royal Canin Dental are kibbles designed to fracture in a specific way that scrubs the tooth surface as the dog chews. The kibble size and texture are engineered —it's not just "bigger kibble." These diets are clinically proven and worth considering if your dog is prone to tartar buildup despite brushing, or if brushing is impossible for medical or behavioral reasons.

Water additives. These reduce bacterial load in the mouth. They don't remove existing plaque. Think of them as mouthwash —a supporting player, not the main event. The VOHC seal applies here too.

What doesn't work: raw bones (too hard —they fracture teeth), antlers and hooves (same problem), and any chew that's harder than what you'd be willing to hit your own kneecap with. The rule of thumb from veterinary dentists: if you wouldn't hit your knee with it, your dog shouldn't chew it. Hard chews cause slab fractures of the carnassial teeth —the big upper molars —and those are $1,500–$3,000 to extract, not $500. The cost savings from a cheap recreational bone evaporate the moment your dog cracks a tooth.

🛒 The dental toolkit, in one place: CET Enzymatic Toothpaste (poultry flavor —dogs genuinely like it) and a soft dog toothbrush or finger brush. For VOHC-approved chews: Greenies and OraVet both carry the VOHC seal. Chewy has all three brands and their autoship discount makes sense for something you're buying monthly. A tube of CET toothpaste lasts about 3 months of daily brushing. That's roughly a dime per brushing —against a $1,200 dental extraction, the math isn't close.
