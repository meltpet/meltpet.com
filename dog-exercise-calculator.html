🏃 How Much Exercise Does My Dog Need?

An Australian Shepherd needs 90 to 120 minutes. A French Bulldog needs 20 to 30. The gap between breeds is not a preference — it is the breed. | 2026-10-13 · MeltPet

Last updated: 2026-10-13

Check Your Dog's Daily Exercise

The Exercise Number for Your Breed

Ask how much exercise a dog needs and you will get a range that spans a factor of six. That is not because the advice is vague — it is because the answer really is that different from breed to breed, and the difference is written into what the dog was bred to do. A herding breed that worked sheep for ten hours a day and a companion breed that sat in a lap for ten hours a day are, on the exercise spectrum, barely the same animal.

Here are the working figures for the breeds MeltPet covers in depth. Each one comes from that breed's guide, where you can read the reasoning behind it.

Two things are worth noticing in that table. First, the floor is low and the ceiling is high: the difference between the least and most demanding breed here is six times the time commitment, and it does not shrink if you are busy. Second, several of the entries say "vigorous", and that word is doing real work. Minutes alone do not describe what a working breed needs — a slow amble for 90 minutes is not the same as a run plus training for 90 minutes, and the dog will tell you the difference even if the stopwatch will not.

What Actually Counts as Exercise

The most common mistake is treating the number as minutes of walking. Walking is the foundation, but it is only the base layer — especially for the breeds at the top of that table, whose brains are as demanding as their bodies.

Physically, the useful categories are distance work (walks, hikes, runs), burst work (fetch, flirt pole, tug), and low-impact work (swimming, which suits older dogs and joint issues particularly well). For a Standard Poodle or a Labrador, off-leash running and swimming are close to ideal because the breed was built for retrieving from water and needs the sustained movement that a leash walk does not supply.

Mentally, the account is separate and it is not optional. Puzzle toys, a KONG Extreme stuffed and frozen, scent games, and short training sessions all burn energy in a way that leaves a dog calmer rather than wired. For intelligent working breeds, mental work is not a bonus on top of the physical — it is part of the requirement, and a dog that gets two hours of walking but no thinking is still an under-exercised dog. This is why a Corgi's number is "structured walks plus mental work" rather than a walk alone, and why breed guides for herding dogs keep returning to obedience and scent work as exercise rather than as training.

There is a version of this that surprises owners of high-energy breeds: a sniff-heavy walk can be more tiring than a brisk one. Allowing a dog to stop and process smells engages the brain hard. That does not replace the physical work for a breed that needs to run, but it is a cheap way to add useful load to a day that is short on time — and it is the difference between a walk that drains a dog and a walk that just moves it.

Finally, a word on what does not count. A fenced yard is not exercise. A dog left in a yard paces, patrols, and waits at the door; it does not self-exercise to the level its breed requires. Corgi guides state this bluntly for a reason: "he runs around in the yard" is one of the most common reasons a herding breed ends up under-exercised while the owner believes the opposite.

How to Split It Across the Day

When the number is large — 90 to 120 minutes — the instinct is to wonder where the time will come from. The trick is that almost nothing in that range has to happen in one block, and splitting it is usually better for the dog anyway.

A practical shape for a 60-minute dog, which scales up or down from there:

  • Morning (20–30 min). A real walk at a working pace. This is the anchor and it sets the dog's day up correctly — a dog that gets out early settles for the rest of the morning.
  • Midday (10–15 min). A short outing or a play session. Burst work is efficient here: fetch or tug delivers a lot of physical load in a small window.
  • Evening (20–30 min). The second walk, ideally a different route. New smells are unpaid mental work.
  • Anywhere (5–10 min). A training session or a puzzle feeder. Ten focused minutes of obedience or scent work can leave a dog more settled than ten more minutes of walking.

For a 90-minute-plus breed, this simply becomes three real sessions rather than one long one, and at least one of them should carry real intensity — a run, off-leash time, or a structured sport — rather than being a fourth walk. Australian Shepherd guides put it plainly: a single 30-minute walk is not exercise for that breed, it is a warm-up, and stacking warm-ups does not add up to the workout the dog needs.

Two quality notes that matter more than the schedule. First, vary the route. A familiar path stops providing mental load once it is memorised; a new direction restarts the clock. Second, finish with something the dog has to think about. Ending a walk with a short training session or a scatter-feed rewards the outing and lands the dog in a calmer state, which is what you actually wanted from the exercise in the first place.

If the leash is the limiting factor — the dog pulls, you dread the walk, and the walk gets shorter — the fix is equipment and technique before it is time. A front-clip harness such as the Freedom No-Pull Harness turns pulling into a redirection and makes a 40-minute walk feel like 20, which means it actually happens.

Puppies, Seniors, and Flat-Faced Breeds

The number this calculator gives you is for a healthy adult of a given breed. Three groups need an adjustment, and MeltPet does not apply a percentage to them, because a made-up multiplier would not be honest. Here is what changes instead.

Puppies are not small adults. A puppy's joints are still developing, so the safer pattern is several short walks or play sessions through the day rather than one long one — a point the American Kennel Club makes explicitly in its exercise guidance, noting that a single long walk "can be too hard on your puppy's developing body." The puppy number in the calculator's output is the target for the adult that puppy will become, not a quota to hit now. Let the puppy set the pace, keep sessions short, and ask your vet or breeder what suits your breed's growth timeline.

Senior dogs still need daily movement, adjusted rather than removed. The general pattern is shorter and more frequent, at lower intensity, with swimming as an excellent low-impact option and more attention paid to signs of stiffness or reluctance. The AKC's advice for older dogs is to talk to your vet and to watch the dog's behavior, on the sound reasoning that you know your dog best and are the best judge of what it can comfortably handle. What you do not want to do is stop — inactivity stiffens arthritic joints and accelerates the decline it was meant to avoid.

Flat-faced breeds are a separate case, with a physical limit rather than a motivational one. French Bulldogs, Pugs, and English Bulldogs overheat quickly and can struggle to breathe under exertion, which is why the correct number for a Frenchie is 20 to 30 easy minutes rather than a scaled-down version of a retriever's routine. Short, frequent outings beat long ones, and heat is the enemy — see our guide to summer dog safety for the heat and pavement thresholds that matter. If your flat-faced dog is panting hard, slowing down, or reluctant to continue, that is the ceiling, not a training problem.

Signs Your Dog Is Getting Too Little — or Too Much

You do not need to measure to know whether the number is right. Dogs are unusually honest about this, and the signals split cleanly into two columns.

Too little shows up as behaviour long before it shows up on a scale. Destructive chewing, digging, pacing, excessive barking, attention-seeking, and a dog that escalates rough play are the standard repertoire — and they are usually described by owners as personality problems rather than exercise deficits. Weight gain on an unchanged ration is the physical version of the same signal. For a high-energy breed, the fastest diagnostic is behavioural: if the destruction stops on the weeks you get the full 90 minutes and returns when you do not, you have your answer.

Too much is the less-discussed failure, and it is real. Warning signs include panting that does not recover after rest, limping or stiffness after exercise, reluctance to go out, lying down mid-walk, and sleeping noticeably more than usual. In a puppy or a flat-faced breed these signs carry extra weight, because the damage can be structural rather than just a tired dog. If you see them, reduce duration and intensity and get a vet's view — especially for limping, which is a symptom, not a mood.

The most useful measurement in this whole page is not the calculator's output. It is the comparison between the output and what your dog is actually getting — the gap is the thing worth fixing, and closing part of it is usually enough to change the behaviour at home.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much exercise does my dog need per day?

It depends almost entirely on the breed, which is why a single number does not exist. Across the breeds MeltPet covers, the range runs from 20 to 30 minutes for a French Bulldog up to 90 to 120 minutes of vigorous exercise for an Australian Shepherd. In between: German Shepherds need 90 minutes or more, Labradors and Golden Retrievers need 60 minutes or more, and a Corgi needs a minimum of 60 minutes of structured walks plus mental work. Use the calculator above for your breed, then treat the figure as the target for a healthy adult of that breed.

Does a big yard mean my dog is already exercised?

No — and this is one of the most common reasons high-energy dogs end up under-exercised while their owners believe the opposite. A dog left in a yard paces, patrols, and waits; it does not self-exercise to the level its breed requires. Corgi guides make the point explicitly, distinguishing "real exercise" from "we have a big yard so he runs around." Structured walks plus mental work is the requirement, and a yard does not substitute for it, though it can make burst work like fetch easier to fit in.

Does mental exercise count toward the daily total?

It is not a replacement for physical exercise, but it is part of the requirement for intelligent breeds and it is not optional. Puzzle toys, frozen Kongs, scent games, and short training sessions all consume energy and leave a dog calmer rather than wired. For working and herding breeds, a dog that gets the physical minutes but no thinking is still under-exercised — which is why breed guides list obedience, scent work, and agility as exercise, not just as training.

How much exercise does a puppy need?

A puppy should not be held to the adult number, because its joints are still developing. The safer pattern is several short walks or play sessions spread through the day rather than one long one — the American Kennel Club notes that a single long walk can be too hard on a puppy's developing body. The number this calculator returns is the target for the adult your puppy will become, not a quota for now. Start short and frequent, let the puppy set the pace, and ask your vet or breeder what fits your breed's growth timeline.

Can you over-exercise a dog?

Yes, and the signs are specific: panting that does not recover after rest, limping or stiffness after exercise, reluctance to go out, lying down mid-walk, and sleeping more than usual. Puppies and flat-faced breeds are the most at risk — puppies because their joints are still forming, flat-faced breeds because their breathing limits are physical rather than motivational. If you see limping or persistent stiffness, reduce duration and intensity and get a vet's opinion rather than pushing through.

Why does breed matter more than size?

Because exercise capacity was bred for purpose, not for body weight. A French Bulldog at 25 pounds and a Corgi at 25 pounds are the same size and need wildly different amounts of activity — the Corgi needs 60 minutes of structured work, the Frenchie 20 to 30 easy minutes. The herding and sporting breeds were developed to work long days, and that capacity is still in them; the brachycephalic breeds were developed for company, and their breathing makes long exertion genuinely unsafe. Size tells you how much food to buy. Breed tells you how much the dog needs to move.

What if my dog is a mixed breed?

Use the breed your dog most resembles in energy and build, then adjust by watching the dog rather than the stopwatch. Mixed breeds usually sit between their parent breeds, so if you know both, aim for the middle of the two figures. If you do not know the mix, start at the lower end of the range, see how the dog settles over a week, and increase until the restless behaviours stop. The behavioural feedback loop is more reliable than any estimate for a dog whose ancestry you cannot see.
