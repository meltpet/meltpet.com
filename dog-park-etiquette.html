🐕 Dog Park Etiquette

The unwritten rules. Learned so you don't have to learn them the hard way. | 2026-06-25 · MeltPet · 7 min read

Last updated: 2026-06-29

I Watched a Man Bring His In-Heat Female to the Dog Park. It Went Exactly How You Think.

Dog parks have no orientation. No handbook. You show up, you unclip the leash, and you hope you're not the person everyone's side-eyeing. Most people are the person everyone's side-eyeing at least once.

The man with the in-heat female —three intact males were on her within 90 seconds. A fight broke out. Someone's dog got a torn ear. The man scooped up his dog and left, genuinely confused about what went wrong. "She seemed fine," he said to no one in particular.

Dog parks are one of the few places where dozens of strange dogs with unknown histories and varying training levels mix freely. When it works, it's magical —dogs running in a pack, exhausted and happy. When it doesn't, someone goes to the emergency vet. The difference between those two outcomes is usually etiquette, not luck.

The Stuff Nobody Tells You

Leave the leash on for the first 30 seconds. Don't just fling open the gate and release your dog into the fray. Stand inside the double-gate area. Let your dog scan the park. Is her body language loose and curious? Tail neutral or gently wagging, ears relaxed, approaching the gate calmly? Go in. Is she stiff, tail tucked, barking frantically, or lunging at the gate? She's not ready for the dog park today. Take her on a walk instead. A dog who enters the park already over threshold is a dog who will start a fight within the first five minutes.

Don't bring treats. This is the #1 piece of dog park wisdom and people still do it. A pocket full of liver treats in an enclosed area with 20 dogs is a recipe for a resource-guarding incident. Even dogs who've never guarded anything before will compete over high-value food in a confined space. If you need treats for training, step outside the fence.

If you bring a toy, it now belongs to the park. Same logic as treats. A tennis ball in a dog park is a contested resource. Some dogs will guard it. Others will swallow it. If your dog has a favorite ball, leave it in the car.

Don't stand in a cluster with other humans. When owners huddle together chatting, their dogs cluster around them. This creates a high-density zone where dogs who don't know each other are forced into close proximity. Spread out. Keep moving. The park works better when dogs are flowing, not packed into a corner where the humans are standing.

Your dog doesn't have to greet every dog. If your dog is obsessively following another dog who's clearly trying to get away —ears back, tail tucked, moving behind benches or humans —recall your dog. Not all dogs want to play, and the dog who's fleeing is communicating clearly. A dog who ignores those signals is being rude, even if he's "friendly."

Pick up your dog's poop. This shouldn't need to be said. It always does.

When Not to Go

Don't bring a puppy under 16 weeks who hasn't completed her vaccination series. Parvo lives in soil for months and a dog park is exactly where it concentrates. Wait until two weeks after the final puppy shot.

Don't bring a dog in heat. Ever. There is no exception to this. The pheromones alone can trigger fights between neutered males who've never shown aggression before.

Don't bring a dog who's been in a fight at the same park with the same dogs. Some dogs hold grudges. Some dogs are selective about which other dogs they tolerate. If your dog has a specific enemy at your local park, don't bring him when that dog is there. It's not worth the vet bill.

And if you don't have reliable verbal recall —your dog comes when called, every time, even with distractions —work on that before the dog park. A dog who won't come when called in a dog park is a dog who can't be managed in a dog park. You don't need perfect recall. You need enough recall to get your dog away from a situation before it escalates.

When Your Dog's "Play" Is Actually Bullying — and Nobody Tells You

Most people can't tell the difference between rough play and a problem brewing. The dogs know. The humans are the last to figure it out.

A play bow —front legs down, butt up —means "whatever happens next is play." Dogs who bow repeatedly during a wrestling match are checking in. They're saying: we're still good, right? A dog who stops bowing is a dog who's stopped playing.

Watch for the meta-signals. A sneeze mid-wrestle means "I'm not serious —this is still a game." A shake-off (the full-body shimmy a dog does when wet) means "that was intense, I need a reset." If neither dog offers a reset after a hard round of wrestling, and the intensity keeps climbing with no break, step in before someone escalates. Call your dog over. Give her 30 seconds. If she goes right back into the same intensity, leash up and leave. She's over threshold and won't self-regulate.

The chase that's not mutual is the easiest one to miss. Two dogs running: one in front, one behind. Look at the dog being chased. Is she loose and bouncy, turning back toward the chaser, offering play bows? That's play. Is she running flat-out with her tail tucked, ears pinned, not looking back, and the chaser is silent and focused? That's not play. That's predation. Call your dog off. The dog being chased is terrified.

A dog who pins another dog and stands over them, stiff and still, while the pinned dog freezes —that's not dominance. That's a threat. A dog who mounts other dogs obsessively, ignoring corrections from the mounted dog, is over-aroused, not "dominant." These dogs need to leave the park before someone bites them. The owner scrolling on their phone misses it until the screaming starts.

What to Do When a Fight Starts (Not What Your Instinct Screams)

Your instinct will tell you to grab collars. Don't. Two dogs locked in a fight have no idea where their teeth are going, and your hand in the middle gets bitten —badly. Dog park fights send more owners to the ER than dogs to the vet.

The wheelbarrow method: each owner grabs their dog's hind legs, lifts them off the ground, and walks backward. This destabilizes the dog, breaks their grip, and keeps your face and hands away from the bite zone. Once separated, both dogs go on leash immediately and leave the park —even if they "seem fine" five minutes later. Adrenaline takes 20+ minutes to clear. A dog who goes back in after a fight will fight again within the hour.

If you're alone with no other owner: grab your dog's hind legs. Don't grab the other dog. Don't yell. A loud, sharp sound —an air horn, a whistle, banging a metal bowl against the fence —can startle dogs apart long enough to separate. Some regulars carry a small air horn. It's not paranoid.

After any fight: exchange info with the other owner. You both think it was the other dog's fault. One of you is probably wrong. The vet bill will sort that out. And if your dog has been in two fights at the same park, stop bringing him there. Not because he's a bad dog. Because that park is the wrong environment. Some dogs do better in small playgroups with dogs they know. Some don't need dog parks at all. A dog who walks nicely on leash, has one or two dog friends, and plays fetch in the yard is not deprived. He's just not a dog park dog, and that's fine.
