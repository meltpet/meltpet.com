Flea and Tick Prevention

A monthly dose covers thirty days. The cocoon it is meant to interrupt can wait two hundred and ten. Everything confusing about flea control follows from that mismatch.

Last updated: 2026-11-14

The Short Version: Thirty Weeks, Three Hours, Ten Percent

210 days. 3 to 6 hours. 10.5 per cent. Three numbers, from two guidelines and two veterinary case surveys, and between them they explain almost every flea question people actually ask.

The first is patience. A cat flea pupa, inside its cocoon, can stay dormant for up to 30 weeks — two hundred and ten days — at 11 °C. It is not hibernating in the sense of being asleep and harmless. It is waiting, and it hatches when something walks past: body heat, carbon dioxide, the vibration of a vacuum cleaner. Your monthly product covers thirty of those days. The cocoon does not care which thirty.

The second is speed. For fleas the relevant unit is weeks; for ticks it is hours. The Companion Animal Parasite Council's tick guidance puts it plainly: "some rickettsial infections can be transmitted after as little as 3-6 hours of tick feeding", while the Lyme agent, Borrelia burgdorferi, "can require 24-48 hours of feeding before a host is infected". Which means the protective product has to be on the animal before the walk, not applied afterwards as a repair.

The third is the one that should stop the argument in a multi-pet house. In a review of 286 cats exposed to concentrated permethrin spot-on products — the kind sold for dogs — reported to the Veterinary Poisons Information Service in London, 96.9 per cent were symptomatic and 10.5 per cent died. That is not a rare idiosyncratic reaction. That is roughly one death in ten, in cats whose owners were trying to help them.

The rest of this page is the timeline behind those three numbers, the arithmetic that makes an infestation predictable, and the parts where the official advice genuinely leaves you a choice.

A Monthly Dose Is a Schedule, Not a Cure

The most common misunderstanding about flea products is grammatical. "Treatment" sounds like something that happens to a problem. A monthly parasiticide is something that happens to a calendar — it holds a level of active ingredient on the animal for a defined window, and outside that window it is doing nothing at all.

This is not a marketing distinction. It changes what "it didn't work" means. If a product is applied on the first of the month and the pet picks up a flea on the twenty-eighth, the product still works: the flea dies, and the life cycle stops there. If the next dose is late, the same flea survives, lays eggs within a day, and the arithmetic in the next section starts running.

It also explains why the answer to "how often should I treat" is not a matter of opinion. The interval printed on the pack is the interval over which the manufacturer demonstrated residual efficacy against the target stage. Shortening it is not extra safety; lengthening it is not thrift. It is simply a different schedule, and one with a gap in it.

Four Stages, and Only One of Them Is on Your Pet

Flea control fails when people imagine a flea as an animal that lives on a dog. It is better imagined as a four-stage process, three-quarters of which is furniture.

The adult flea on the animal is the visible one, and it is the only stage most products touch directly. The other three live in the environment: eggs that fall off the host within hours of being laid, maggot-like larvae about half a centimetre long that live in carpet fibre and soil and feed on dried blood in flea dirt, and pupae inside a silk cocoon about half a centimetre long. The whole cycle is described stage by stage in the CAPC's flea guideline, which is where every figure in this table comes from.

Stage Where it lives How long it lasts
Egg Laid on the pet, falls into the environment within a few hours 0.5 mm; hatches in days to weeks depending on temperature
Larva Carpet, bedding, soil, under furniture About 0.5 cm long; feeds on flea dirt, then pupates
Pupa Inside a silk cocoon in carpet, vegetation, bedding 8 to 13 days at 24 °C — or up to 30 weeks at 11 °C
Adult On the pet, which is the only place it can feed Most survive 2 to 3 months; days without a blood meal

Read the last column downward and the practical problem appears. Three of the four stages are somewhere in your house, and two of them — egg and pupa — are built to survive being ignored. An insecticide applied to the dog does nothing to a cocoon under the sofa.

The Arithmetic of One Female Flea

Two rates in the CAPC guideline do most of the work. Egg production begins 20 to 24 hours after a female's first blood meal, and she can lay 40 to 50 eggs a day. Most adults live 2 to 3 months.

Multiply the middle and last figures and you get the shape of the problem: a single female, over a two-month adult life, is responsible for somewhere between 2,400 and 3,000 eggs. Over three months the same arithmetic reaches 3,600 to 4,500.

That figure is an upper bound, not a forecast — it assumes every egg hatches and every larva finds the conditions it needs, which they do not. Newly emerged adults die within one to two weeks in most homes if they never find a host, so a household with no animal in it does eventually clear. But the point of the calculation is not precision. It is that the number of fleas you can see is a very poor indicator of the number you have. Ten visible fleas is not ten fleas; it is ten adults out of a population whose other stages are measured in thousands.

This is also why "I only found two" is not reassurance, and why the trigger for treating an environment is a single sighting rather than an infestation. The founder population of a household infestation is generally small. The generation that follows it is not.

Thirty Weeks in a Cocoon

The pupal stage is where most home flea campaigns die, and the CAPC numbers explain why. At 24 °C and 78 per cent relative humidity, cat fleas begin emerging about 8 days after pupal development starts, and are usually all out by day 13. In temperate climates, 90 to 95 per cent emerge within 21 to 35 days. In subtropical climates it is faster: 96 to 99 per cent within 14 to 28 days. At 11 °C, the cocoon can hold for up to 30 weeks.

Two consequences follow, and both look like paradoxes to an owner.

Cleaning makes it worse before it makes it better. The cocoon does not hatch on a timer. Mechanical pressure, carbon dioxide and increased temperature stimulate emergence — which is to say, the vacuum cleaner, the body heat of a person walking across the room, and the breath of the dog lying on the rug are all hatch signals. A deep clean genuinely does trigger a wave of adults. That wave is the treatment working through the backlog, not failing.

An empty house is not a safe house. Because the cocoon waits rather than dies, a property can sit vacant for months and still produce fleas the week someone moves in. This is the mechanism behind the classic "the new tenant says the flat is infested" story, and it is why environmental control is judged over months rather than days.

The other number worth keeping in view is the one for established infestations: CAPC notes that moderate to severe infestations "may take several months to bring under control". Several months is not a hedge. It is the length of the cocoon's tail.

Why They Came Back (and Why That Is Expected)

If you treat one animal, on schedule, and still see fleas six weeks later, the most likely explanation is not resistance and it is not a bad product. It is that the population you are fighting was never only on that animal.

CAPC's recommendation is unusually blunt about this: to achieve control, "every pet in the home must be treated". The cat that never goes outside, the elderly dog who sleeps downstairs, the rabbit in the spare room — fleas are not species-loyal in the way people assume, and cat fleas are the most common ectoparasite of dogs in North America as well as of cats. An untreated animal in the same house is a reservoir that keeps re-seeding the treated one.

There is a public-health dimension here that rarely makes it into product advertising. The same guideline notes that people in infested environments are often bitten by newly emerging fleas, producing a papular rash that ranges from mild to extensive depending on the number of fleas and how reactive the person is. Treating the pet is also treating the household.

Itching Without Fleas: The Allergy Version

There is a group of animals for whom all of the above applies at a tenth of the flea numbers: the ones with flea allergy dermatitis.

The CAPC guideline defines it precisely — FAD is "caused by hypersensitivity to antigenic material from the salivary glands of fleas". Note what that definition is not about. It is not about how many fleas are present. It is about an immune response to a protein in flea saliva, which means the threshold for a reaction is set by the animal's immune system rather than by the size of the infestation.

The practical consequence is that "I can't find any fleas" is weak evidence in an itchy animal. Cats in particular groom, and grooming removes evidence faster than it removes the cause. A 2020 review in Insects describes FAD as common in both dogs and cats and notes that it has been successfully managed with modern flea products — which is a statement about interrupting exposure, not about killing a visible population.

So for an allergic animal the schedule matters more, not less. If the reaction is to saliva, then the number of fleas required to keep a dermatitis going is not the number you would notice, and a reservoir animal in the same house can sustain that exposure indefinitely while the treated animal looks "clear". This is also why a veterinarian may begin flea control before finding a flea: the diagnosis is often clinical, based on the pattern of itching and hair loss rather than on a flea count.

It is worth being honest about the limits of that reasoning too. Itching has many causes — food, pollen, mites, infection — and flea allergy is one of several explanations rather than the default one. What the flea-specific part of the evidence supports is narrower: in an animal with a flea allergy, exposure has to be interrupted continuously, because the reaction is triggered by a dose of saliva that a monthly schedule is designed to prevent rather than to treat.

Ticks Run on Hours

Everything above is about weeks. Ticks invert the problem, and this is the part of flea-and-tick control where the phrasing "prevention" stops being a scheduling word and becomes a literal one.

The CAPC tick guideline reports that some rickettsial infections — the group that includes Rocky Mountain spotted fever and ehrlichiosis — "can be transmitted within 3-6 hours of tick attachment", while Borrelia burgdorferi, the Lyme agent, "can require 24-48 hours of feeding before a host is infected". That difference is the most useful thing on this page for anyone who walks a dog in long grass. It means "check the dog when you get home" is not equally effective against every disease: it is a good strategy for Lyme and a poor one for the fast-transmitting rickettsiae.

The life cycle is slower than the transmission. A tick larva hatches from the egg within weeks to months depending on conditions, feeds for several days, drops off and moults into an eight-legged nymph, and the nymph then finds another host and feeds for several days to a week. Female hard ticks can expand to as much as 100 times their original weight before detaching — which is why an engorged tick looks like a different animal from the flat one you see on a blade of grass.

Two practical details follow. First, the product has to be present before the tick arrives; a parasiticide applied after the walk cannot undo a transmission that took three hours. Second, "tick season" is a weaker idea than people think. The brown dog tick completes its whole life cycle indoors, in homes and kennels, and is active every month of the year — the reason CAPC's advice on ticks is more absolute than its advice on fleas: "CAPC supports year-round use of tick-control products on pets", on the grounds that reactive or seasonal use lets home infestations establish in the gap.

The guideline lists nine tick species of concern in dogs in North America, including the lone star tick, the American dog tick, the black-legged (deer) tick and the brown dog tick. Nine species with different habitats and different pathogens is the reason no single behavioural trick substitutes for a product: there is no one place to avoid.

The clock How long Who is in control of it
Your product's residual cover 30 days, per the label interval You — this is the only clock you can reset
Flea cocoon in the environment 8 to 13 days warm; up to 210 days cool Temperature, humidity, and whoever next walks past
Tick transmission 3 to 6 hours for some rickettsiae; 24 to 48 for Lyme Nobody — it is over before the dog is home

What the Guidelines Say, and Where They Leave Room

Two organisations' wordings are worth reading side by side, because they are not identical and the difference is instructive.

On fleas, CAPC recommends that "all dogs should be treated year-round and throughout their life with flea control products". On ticks, it "supports year-round use of tick-control products on pets". Both are year-round positions. But the flea guideline then adds a caveat that the tick one does not: "because substantial geographic differences occur in flea prevalence and seasonality, prevention programs should be tailored to needs of the individual pet."

That is not a contradiction; it is a statement about what the evidence supports. Flea pressure genuinely varies by region and by whether there is a heated house and a carpet, so a national guideline can only set a default. Tick guidance is firmer because the brown dog tick removes the seasonal escape hatch: an indoor tick population gives you a winter problem, and a seasonal dosing schedule gives it a gap.

What the guidelines do not say is equally worth knowing. They do not say that a product eliminates the need to manage the environment, and they do not promise a timeline shorter than months for an established infestation. A reasonable summary of the official position is: dose continuously, treat every animal, expect the environment to take months, and adapt the intensity to where you live rather than to the calendar on the wall.

Cats Are Not Small Dogs

This is the section that matters most, and it is the one where the numbers are least ambiguous.

Concentrated permethrin spot-on products — the ones formulated at 45 to 65 per cent permethrin for dogs — are not for cats, and the reason is not caution. In the London poisons service review of 286 cats exposed to them, 96.9 per cent became symptomatic. Increased muscular activity, described as twitching, tremor, muscle fasciculations or convulsions, occurred in 87.8 per cent. The duration is the detail that surprises people: convulsions lasted on average 38.9 hours and tremors 32 hours. Recovery typically took two to three days, and in some cats five to seven. Death occurred in 10.5 per cent of cases.

A survey of Australian veterinarians found the same pattern at larger scale: 750 cases over two years, and 166 deaths. Of those, 39 cats were euthanased because the owners could not pay the anticipated treatment cost — a sentence that connects this page's medical section to its cost section rather directly.

Where the products came from matters too. In that survey, the implicated spot-ons were most commonly bought at supermarkets (146 respondents), then pet shops (43), then veterinary practices (16). The majority were labelled for dogs, and some carried the words "toxic to cats" on the label. People were not being reckless; they were reading a package in an aisle with a product that promised to solve the same problem for less money.

Exposure is not always a direct mistake. Cats can also be poisoned by grooming or resting against a dog that was treated recently — the product transfers. That is the reason a multi-pet household needs a species-appropriate product on each animal rather than one product that covers the house.

Survey Cats Symptomatic Died
VPIS, London (2007) 286 96.9% 10.5%
Australian practitioner survey (2010) 750 Not reported as a rate 166 (22.1%)

If a dog product has been applied to a cat, or a cat has groomed a recently treated dog, treat it as an emergency rather than a wait-and-see. In the London series some cats developed convulsions within two hours of application and others within 24. There is no antidote; treatment is supportive, and the recorded cases that did well were managed in hospital — one published 2022 series of nine cats describes seizures and tremors controlled with benzodiazepines and intravenous lipid emulsion, with all nine discharged within 24 to 54 hours.

For the cat in the house, the practical rule is the boring one: buy the product that says cat on it. If you want a starting point for what those products are, the cat-specific flea and tick range is the right shelf — and nothing from the dog shelf goes on the cat, however small the dog.

A Twelve-Month Plan That Matches the Biology

None of this requires a complex protocol. It requires that your habits line up with the timelines rather than with the seasons.

Dose on a fixed day, not on a feeling. The single highest-value change most households can make is a recurring calendar entry for every animal in the house. Because the cover is 30 days and the cocoon's tail is 210, the failure mode is not a weak product — it is a four-day gap that nobody noticed.

Treat every animal, including the indoor cat. CAPC's guidance is explicit that control requires treating every pet in the home. Cat fleas are the most common ectoparasite of dogs in North America, which means the indoor cat is not outside the problem; she is the part of the reservoir that never leaves the building.

Keep going through winter. This is the instruction people abandon first, and the one with the clearest support. The cocoon waits through the cold months, and the brown dog tick does not observe seasons indoors at all — so a winter gap is a head start you have given the other side.

Expect months, not days, if the house is already infested. CAPC's own phrasing is "several months". Judging a product at week three, during the emergence wave that the vacuuming triggered, is how people end up switching products repeatedly and concluding that nothing works.

Check after exposure, and know what the check is for. A tick search after a walk in long grass is worth doing for every species, and worth doing promptly: at 3 to 6 hours some infections are already transmitted, while Lyme generally needs 24 to 48 hours of attachment. Removal is not a substitute for prevention, and prevention is not a substitute for looking.

If you are choosing a product for a dog, the dog flea and tick preventives shelf is where that decision starts — but which active ingredient suits your dog is a veterinary question, not a shopping one, and it depends on weight, age, health and what the parasites in your area have been exposed to.

What Has No Evidence Behind It

Flea control attracts more folk remedies than almost any other topic in pet care, and the honest position is that the evidence for most of them is absent rather than merely thin. A 2020 review of cat flea control in Insects put it in a single sentence: "To date, no biological, natural, or cultural means have been discovered that mitigate flea infestations."

That is a strong claim, and it is worth reading precisely. It does not say that nothing natural has any insecticidal activity in a laboratory dish — several plant compounds do. It says that nothing in that category has been shown to reduce an infestation in a real household, which is the only outcome an owner cares about.

The same review makes a second point that cuts against a common assumption: resistance to the modern spot-on and oral products has been studied for decades, and pyrethroid resistance in cat fleas is a genuine and growing concern, while the evidence that resistance explains routine product failure remains weak. In practice, when a product "stops working", the more common cause is the one described above — a gap in the schedule, or an untreated animal, or a cocoon population that was always going to take months.

What Prevention Costs, and What Failure Costs

Flea and tick prevention is one of the few recurring pet costs that scales predictably with body weight, because the dose does. In our own monthly cost model, the combined flea, tick and heartworm line runs $15 to $22 for a small dog, $20 to $45 for a medium dog and $30 to $60 for a large one, per month. That figure bundles three products into one line, so treat it as an upper bound for flea and tick cover alone.

The cost of getting it wrong is harder to tabulate, but the veterinary literature gives glimpses. In the Australian survey, 39 of 166 cats died by euthanasia because their owners could not pay the anticipated treatment bill — that is roughly one in four of the deaths in that dataset, caused by the price of care rather than the toxicity itself. And the London series recorded convulsions lasting an average of 38.9 hours, which in a hospital setting is an intensive-care stay, not a consultation.

Set against that, the arithmetic of prevention is unglamorous but clear: a year of monthly cover for a medium dog, at the model's $20 to $45, is $240 to $540. That is the number to compare against an emergency visit, and it is also the number that makes the supermarket shortcut in the cat section so tempting and so expensive.

When This Becomes a Vet Visit

Three situations justify a phone call rather than a forum search.

A cat exposed to a dog product. Symptoms can begin within two hours and include twitching, tremor, unsteadiness, drooling, hypersensitivity to sound and touch, and convulsions. Do not wait to see whether it develops. In the recorded series, 96.9 per cent of exposed cats developed signs.

An engorged tick, or a tick you cannot remove cleanly. Prompt removal matters because of the 3-to-6-hour window for some infections, and because a partially removed tick leaves mouthparts in the skin.

An infestation that has not responded after two full months of correct, uninterrupted, whole-house dosing. At that point the useful questions are diagnostic — is the interval actually being kept, is every animal treated, is the species right — and possibly about local resistance, which is a conversation with someone who knows what is circulating in your area.

This page is a summary of published guidance, not a prescription. Nothing here tells you which product to use on your animal; that decision needs a weight, an age, a health history and a look at the animal in front of you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can fleas survive in a cocoon?

Up to 30 weeks — 210 days — at 11 °C, according to the CAPC flea guideline. Warmth shortens it sharply: at 24 °C and 78 per cent relative humidity, cat fleas begin emerging about eight days after pupal development starts and are usually all out by day 13. In temperate climates, 90 to 95 per cent emerge within 21 to 35 days. The cocoon does not hatch on a timer; mechanical pressure, carbon dioxide and rising temperature trigger it, which is why vacuuming produces a wave of adults.

Why do fleas come back after I treat my pet?

Usually because the population was never only on that pet. Three of the four flea life stages live in the environment, and the pupal stage can wait months for a host. CAPC states that moderate to severe infestations may take several months to bring under control, and that every pet in the home must be treated for control to be achieved. An untreated animal, or a gap in the dosing schedule, restarts the cycle.

How fast can a tick transmit disease?

It depends on the organism. CAPC reports that some rickettsial infections, the group that includes Rocky Mountain spotted fever and ehrlichiosis, can be transmitted within 3 to 6 hours of attachment, while the Lyme agent Borrelia burgdorferi can require 24 to 48 hours of feeding before a host is infected. This is why the preventive product has to be on the animal before exposure rather than applied afterwards.

Can I use dog flea treatment on my cat?

No. Concentrated permethrin spot-ons, the dog-only products at 45 to 65 per cent permethrin, are severely toxic to cats. In 286 feline exposures reported to the Veterinary Poisons Information Service in London, 96.9 per cent of cats became symptomatic and 10.5 per cent died; convulsions lasted an average of 38.9 hours. Cats can also be poisoned by grooming or lying against a dog that was treated recently, so each species needs a product labelled for it.

How many eggs does one female flea lay?

40 to 50 eggs a day, starting 20 to 24 hours after her first blood meal. Most adults survive two to three months, so a single female accounts for roughly 2,400 to 3,000 eggs over a two-month life. That is an upper bound rather than a forecast, since not every egg hatches and not every larva finds a host, but it explains why the number of fleas you can see says very little about the number you have.

Do I need flea treatment in winter?

CAPC recommends year-round flea control for all dogs, and supports year-round tick control, because the cocoon survives cold weather by waiting and because brown dog ticks complete their life cycle indoors and are active every month. Its flea guideline does add that prevention should be tailored to local prevalence and seasonality, so the intensity can vary by region — but the default in the guidance is twelve months, not six.

How long does it take to clear a flea infestation?

Months, not days. CAPC's own wording is that established infestations may take several months to bring under control. The reason is structural: the cocoon's emergence window can run for 210 days at cool temperatures, and the wave of adults that appears after cleaning is triggered by the cleaning. Judging a product at week three, in the middle of that wave, is the most common reason people switch products that were working.
