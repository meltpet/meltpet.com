🇫🇷 French Bulldog Reality Check

$2,000–$8,000 is the entry fee. The vet bills start there. | 2026-06-18 · MeltPet · 6 min read

Last updated: 2026-06-29

Rachel Bought a $4,200 Puppy. Then the Breathing Started.

My neighbor Rachel has a cream-colored Frenchie named Gustav. Ears like satellite dishes. Face like he's mildly offended by everything that has ever happened. I stopped to pet him at the mailbox last fall. Asked how he was doing.

She laughed. Not the good kind.

Gustav was 18 months old. The breeder —a "nice family" in Pennsylvania —charged $4,200. Showed them photos of the parents. Said both were healthy. Gustav's nostrils were so narrow he snored while awake. $3,800 to fix them. Then the soft palate —same problem, different anatomy —another $2,100. His skin allergies started at 6 months. Cytopoint injections every 5 weeks, $85 a pop. And he'd started dragging one back leg. The neurologist was booked three weeks out. IVDD was the working assumption. Surgery: $5,000 on the low end, $12,000 if it was bad.

Rachel adores that dog. Would do it again. But she told me something that stuck: "Nobody said any of this. The breeder said the parents were healthy. I thought that was enough."

For a French Bulldog, "healthy parents" means less than you think.

The Numbers That Actually Matter

I worked in pet food for five years. I've watched French Bulldog owners walk into vet offices with a $3,000 puppy and walk out with a $9,000 estimate. Here's what the breed actually costs, pulled from veterinary teaching hospital data and pet insurance claims —not breeder brochures.

BOAS surgery (nares + palate): $2,500–$5,000. Roughly half of Frenchies have clinically significant airway obstruction. The surgery transforms some dogs. It's also not optional —your dog can't breathe. Here's the thing: you won't always know the severity at 8 weeks. Some puppies compensate. Then they hit adulthood, put on a few pounds, and suddenly every breath is work.

IVDD spinal surgery: $5,000–$12,000. French Bulldogs top the charts for intervertebral disc disease. Discs degenerate, herniate, compress the spinal cord. Saturday your dog is walking. Sunday her back legs don't work. Surgery within 24 hours gives the best odds. After 48 hours, the prognosis tanks. This isn't "let's wait and see." It's a neurological emergency with a five-figure price tag.

Allergies: $600–$1,800 a year, forever. The breed is so prone to environmental allergies that dermatologists barely blink when a Frenchie walks in. Cytopoint shots. Apoquel pills. Prescription food at $90 a bag. Medicated baths. Some dogs need one of these. Some need all of them. Most fall somewhere in between, and none of it is cheap.

Skin folds: free to maintain, expensive to neglect. Wipe the face folds daily with a damp cloth, dry them completely. Miss a few days and yeast moves in. Miss a few weeks and you're at the vet for antibiotics and antifungals. The tail pocket too —if the tail is tightly screwed, stuff gets trapped in there. It's a five-second daily habit that saves you a $300 vet visit. Most people learn this the hard way.

So Why Do People Keep Getting Them?

Because when a Frenchie is healthy —actually comfortable, breathing easily, skin under control —they're one of the most delightful dogs alive. There's a reason they're the #1 AKC-registered breed.

They're funny without trying. Affectionate without being needy. Quiet —they don't bark at the mailman, they don't bark at the wind, they barely bark at all. They need 20, maybe 30 minutes of walking a day. A studio apartment, a suburban house, doesn't matter —they adapt to whatever space you have as long as they're near you. They're small enough not to knock over toddlers but sturdy enough that a toddler won't break them. They greet every stranger like an old friend.

What they are not: athletic, heat-tolerant, cheap to maintain, independent, or good swimmers. Most sink. Their front end is too heavy and their snout is too short —swimming without a life vest is a drowning risk.

A Frenchie is the most expensive low-energy dog on the market. If your budget can absorb the vet bills and you find a breeder who actually screens their dogs, it might be the right fit. If any of those numbers made you flinch, pick a different breed. There are 200 more.

Finding a Breeder Who Doesn't Cut Corners

A French Bulldog breeder should hand you paperwork —not promises —before you hand them a deposit. Specifically:

  • OFA hip scores. The breed ranks poorly on hip dysplasia. Both parents tested, both scores public. You can verify them on the OFA website in 30 seconds. If the breeder won't give you the registration numbers to do that, something's off.
  • OFA patella evaluation. Kneecaps that dislocate. Common in small breeds, and surgery to fix it runs $1,500–$4,000 per knee.
  • Cardiac exam by a board-certified cardiologist. Not "our vet listened and said it sounded fine." An actual echocardiogram with a written report.
  • Spine screening. This one's still catching on, but the breeders who do it —spinal X-rays or CT to check for vertebral malformations —are the ones trying to reduce IVDD in the breed. Those are the breeders you want.
  • DNA panel. Degenerative myelopathy, hyperuricosuria, and whatever else is testable. The tests exist. There's no excuse for not running them.

Meet the mother. See where the puppies live —in the house, handled daily, exposed to vacuum cleaners and doorbells and normal life. Get a contract with a health guarantee and a take-back clause that covers the dog's entire life. Expect a waitlist. Good breeders don't have puppies sitting around waiting for buyers. If someone always has availability, there's a reason.

Ten to twelve years of this. A Frenchie who stays mostly healthy: $25,000–$40,000 lifetime. One who needs BOAS surgery and develops IVDD: easily over $60,000. The purchase price —$2,000 or $8,000, doesn't matter —is the smallest number on this page.

I'm not trying to talk you out of a French Bulldog. I'm trying to make sure you don't end up like Rachel: standing at the mailbox with a dog she loves and a financial hole she didn't see coming. If these numbers don't scare you and you've found a breeder who screens for everything, go get your Frenchie. They're worth it —when you can afford the maintenance.
