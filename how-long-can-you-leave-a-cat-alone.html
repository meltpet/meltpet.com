How Long Can You Leave a Cat Alone?

The honest answer is a range, and only one of the four things that set it is age. Here is the breakdown — and the setup that decides whether it goes well. | 2026-10-15 · MeltPet · 13 min read

Last updated: 2026-10-15

The Honest Answer Is a Range, and One End of It Is 12 Hours

A healthy adult cat can be left alone for a normal working day, and around 12 hours is the practical ceiling that most feline welfare organisations put on a routine absence. A single overnight stretch is usually survivable for a calm adult; past 24 hours, the answer stops being about hours and starts being about people — someone should be going in. Kittens, senior cats, and cats on medication follow much shorter numbers, and a cat with a medical condition may need checking on within hours.

If you searched this question you have already met two answers that contradict each other. One camp says 24 hours is fine as long as the bowls are full. The other says never more than a working day. Both are quoting real guidance, and neither is quoting a clinical trial, because there isn't one. The number you actually need is a function of four things — age, health, how many cats are in the house, and how the house is set up — and the fourth one is the only one you can change before you walk out of the door.

This article gives you the range for each situation, the reasons behind it, and then the part almost every version of this question skips: the setup. Because the difference between a cat who sails through your trip and a cat who is genuinely in trouble is rarely the number of hours. It is whether anyone thought about the water, the litter, the heat, and the one hazard left on the floor.

Read that table as a starting point, not a licence. The honest professional position is that a cat's tolerance for being alone varies more between individuals than it does between categories, and the only reliable instrument you have for measuring yours is what your cat does when you are gone.

Why There Is No Clinical Trial Behind These Numbers

This is worth saying plainly, because most articles on this topic present an hour figure as though it were measured in a laboratory. It was not.

The 12-hour figure is a welfare recommendation, not an experimental result. Cats Protection, the UK's largest cat welfare charity, publishes it as the maximum for a routine absence and is equally clear that it is not a target: an occasional 12 hours is tolerable, regularly leaving your cat for 12 hours is not. The guidance that a kitten should not go more than a few hours is reasoning from feeding frequency and curiosity, not from a study that put kittens in rooms and timed them. The widely repeated "24 hours is fine" is the internet's version of the same advice, with the caveats trimmed off.

What does have data behind it is the thing those hour counts are trying to protect: how cats actually respond to being left. And the data there is more interesting than the hour counts, because it says the premise most people start from — that cats do not really mind — is wrong.

The Four Things That Actually Set Your Number

Hour figures get quoted as if a cat were a cat. In practice, four variables do almost all of the work, and they interact — a young healthy cat in a badly prepared house is worse off than an older cat in a well-prepared one.

  • Age. This is the one everybody thinks of, and it is genuinely the easiest to reason about. A kitten eats several times a day, cannot regulate its own intake, and will investigate anything. An adult cat has a routine and a functioning bladder. A senior cat is a different animal again — see below.
  • Health, including medication. Diabetes, kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, epilepsy, chronic pain, and post-surgical recovery all change the answer to "how long" from hours to "ask your vet". A cat on twice-daily medication cannot be left for a day, whatever her age says.
  • How many cats, and whether they get on. A second cat is company, not cover. Two cats sharing one water bowl and one litter box have less access to essentials than one cat alone, not more. This matters more than owners expect, and it is the reason a multi-cat household is not automatically the easy case.
  • The setup — and this is the one you control. Water in several places and away from food. Food measured out rather than poured into a heap. A box that starts clean and is big enough to stay clean. A room that is not a heat trap in summer. Hazards put away. A way to check on the house from wherever you are. Every one of these is a decision you make in the ten minutes before you leave, and each of them is worth more than an hour of the ceiling.

If you want to know which of the four is failing you, the honest test is not the calendar. It is what you come home to — and the section on warning signs below is built around exactly that.

By Age: What Each Cat Is Actually Built For

Veterinary medicine now sorts cats into life stages rather than a single "adult" bucket, and the categories are worth knowing because they explain why the hour numbers move the way they do. The 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines divide a cat's life into kitten (birth to one year), young adult (one to six), mature adult (seven to ten), and senior (ten and older), with an end-of-life stage that can occur at any age. The practical thresholds below follow that structure.

Mature adult, 7–10 yearsEarly kidney, thyroid, joint and dental change — usually before any visible signWatch for a shift in drinking, appetite, or weight, and shorten the absence if anything has changed.

Two notes that the stage names hide. First, a kitten's ability to cope alone is not only about its stomach — it is about what it has learned. The social window in which a kitten learns that the world is safe closes early, and a kitten who is never left for short, boring, low-drama periods does not learn that you come back. Welfare charities suggest practising five to ten minutes at a time, several times a day, and stretching the gap over weeks rather than jumping from never to a full day. If you have a new kitten, that practice belongs in the first week — see the new kitten checklist for the rest of the setup.

Second, "senior" is not a synonym for "frail", but it is a synonym for "less margin". The conditions that catch older cats early — chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, arthritis — are exactly the conditions that make a missed meal or a missed dose matter more. If your cat is drinking noticeably more than she used to, do not file it under "she's just getting older": read the seven causes of dehydration in cats and see how kidney disease is caught before the creatinine rises. The Myth That Cats Do Not Need You

Everything above this section is about logistics. This section is about the reason the logistics matter, and it is the part of the topic where the science is genuinely better than the folk wisdom.

The folk wisdom says cats are solitary, aloof, and unbothered by your absence — that they tolerate being alone rather than minding it. The research says something different. In a 2019 study in Current Biology, Kristyn Vitale and colleagues put cats and their caregivers through a test developed for human infants: a short period together in an unfamiliar room, a brief separation, then a reunion. Roughly 65% of the cats and kittens were classified as securely attached to their caregiver — a proportion that closely mirrors the rate seen in human infants, and one that stayed stable in the cats who were retested weeks later. Securely attached cats used the person as a safe base: they explored while she was there, showed mild distress when she left, and settled when she came back. That is not the behaviour of an animal for whom you are interchangeable with a bowl of food.

The second piece of evidence is about what happens when the absence is long enough or frequent enough to bite. A 2020 questionnaire study of 223 cats published in PLOS ONE found that 13.45% met at least one of the behavioural criteria the authors used to flag separation-related problems — destructive behaviour, excessive vocalisation, urination or defecation in the wrong place, and signs of low mood or agitation. In that sample, the problems tracked with management rather than with personality: cats with no access to toys and cats with no other animal in the house were significantly more likely to be flagged. The authors are careful about what this does and does not show — it is owner-reported, and it identifies cats with signs consistent with separation-related problems rather than a veterinary diagnosis — but the direction is consistent with the attachment finding, and it mirrors rates reported in dogs.

The practical translation is short. Cats are not indifferent to being alone; they are just quiet about it. A dog who is distressed tells the neighbours. A cat who is distressed may over-groom a patch of her belly, stop eating, or start urinating beside the door — and you may only find out when you get home. Which is why the setup ahead of you matters more than the number of hours on the calendar.

Water Is Where Most Setups Fail First

Water is the resource people get wrong most often, for a simple reason: it looks solved. There is a bowl. There has always been a bowl. The problem is that the bowl is small, it sits next to the food, and nobody refilled it at hour nine.

The working number in feline medicine is 40 to 60 millilitres of water per kilogram of body weight per day, which for a 5 kg cat is roughly 200 to 300 ml — somewhere around a large mug, every day. Where your cat sits inside that band is decided mostly by diet: a cat on wet food takes much of her water from her food and lands at the lower end, while a cat on nothing but dry kibble is at the higher end and is doing all of it from the bowl. If you are leaving a dry-fed cat alone all day, you are leaving her the whole of her daily requirement in a container you will not be watching. The cat water intake calculator will give you your own cat's number in millilitres, including how much of it her food supplies.

Three changes fix most of this, and all three are free:

  • More than one container, in more than one place. A single bowl that gets knocked, fouled, or simply has the cat sitting in it takes the day's water with it. Three bowls in three rooms is the cheap version of a safety net.
  • Move the water away from the food. Cats are routinely reluctant to drink next to their food, which is one reason bowls placed side by side go untouched for a whole absence.
  • Choose the container deliberately. Wide and shallow beats tall and narrow, because cats dislike having their whiskers pressed against the sides, and stainless steel or ceramic beats scratched plastic, which holds odours and bacterial biofilm.

A pet fountain genuinely helps with one part of this. Cats instinctively prefer moving water, and many drink more from a fountain than from a still bowl — which is the entire point when you are trying to get water into a dry-fed cat who will be alone. A stainless steel fountain is also easier to clean than a plastic one and less prone to biofilm. Be honest about the size of the effect, though: a fountain raises intake, it does not remove the need for a second water source, and it is a device that can fail while you are out — so the bowls stay out either way.

One habit worth keeping even when you are not going anywhere: cats mask dehydration well, and the earliest measurable sign is weight. A drop of about 5% of body weight is roughly where mild dehydration begins — on a 5 kg cat, that is only 250 g. If you already weigh your cat weekly, a trip away is a natural moment to take a reading before and after; if you have never done it, the at-home dehydration test covers the checks that do not need a scale.

Food: Portions, Feeders, and How They Fail

Food looks even simpler than water — pour out a day's worth, leave it, done — and it is where the second most common mistake lives: leaving too much.

Wet food spoils. It should not sit out for a whole absence in any quantity your cat will not clear within a few hours, so a wet-fed cat left alone for a working day needs either a timed feeder, a person, or a schedule that does not put a fresh portion down as you leave. Dry food keeps far longer, but it is not immortal: it goes stale, its fats oxidise, and welfare guidance is to replace it at least every 12 hours rather than leaving the same portion out indefinitely. Free-pouring a mountain of kibble to be safe is self-defeating in a different way — it is the fastest route to an overweight cat, and for a cat on a prescription or portioned diet, it undoes the plan.

So the workable version is: measure the day's food, split it if you can, and use the tools for what they are good at. A timed feeder that drops a measured portion mid-day keeps a dry-fed cat's routine intact, and it is genuinely useful. It is also a machine, and machines fail — which is why the standard advice is to set it up and test it for several days before the trip, and to leave backup food in a separate bowl anyway. The failure mode is not that the feeder jams. It is that the feeder jams at 9 a.m. on the first day and nobody finds out for three days. If your cat is on a measured diet and you have never worked out her daily calories, the cat calorie calculator will give you the number to portion against.

Two finer points that only matter when you are away. First, in a multi-cat house, free-feeding hides illness — you cannot tell who is eating what. If you are going away, measure, so that a cat who stops eating is visible when you return. Second, cats who eat quickly can vomit a large portion straight back up, which wastes the meal and looks alarming when you get home; if that has happened before, a puzzle feeder is a better answer than a bigger bowl. And if you come home to vomiting that is new, the colour of the vomit is the first thing to check before you decide how urgent it is.

The Litter Box Maths for an Empty House

A litter box is a cleaning problem at home and a hygiene problem when you are away, because a cat who is alone has no option to use the second box you did not put down.

The widely used rule of thumb is one box per cat, plus one. That figure comes from the Ohio State University Indoor Pet Initiative as practical guidance rather than a controlled result, and the peer-reviewed AAFP and ISFM house-soiling guidelines give the principle underneath it rather than the formula: every cat needs a choice of each key resource, including at least two toileting areas, in separate locations and away from food and water. For a trip, the operational version is simpler than the arithmetic. Start with every box clean, place them in different rooms rather than side by side, and scoop daily if anyone is coming in. Two boxes next to each other are read by many cats as one box.

The detail that catches people out is depth and cleanliness. Welfare guidance is to keep the litter at roughly 3 cm deep, to scoop waste out at least twice a day, and to change the whole tray at least once a week — numbers that matter when the box has to last a whole day unattended. A box that is too shallow gets scratched through; a box that is too small forces the cat to stand in what she has just done. If you are going to change anything about the box before a trip, change it well before the trip: cats are suspicious of sudden changes to their toilet, and a new litter introduced on the morning you leave is a good way to come home to a puddle on the bath mat. If the box has been repeatedly refused, peeing outside the litter box works through the causes in the order that fixes them, and the litter comparison shows what the cats in that test would and would not accept.

The Eleven-Point Setup Before You Walk Out

This is the checklist the hour counts are standing in for. Ten minutes with it is worth more than any debate about whether the ceiling is 12 hours or 24.

  1. Water in three places, at least one away from the food. Refill every container to the top, not partway.
  2. Food measured for the time you will be gone — split into two or more servings if you can, and not free-poured into a heap.
  3. Test any automatic feeder or fountain for several days first, and leave backup food and water in a separate container in case it stops.
  4. Every litter box clean when you leave, in separate rooms, litter about 3 cm deep.
  5. One hiding place and one high perch the cat can reach, because being able to get up and away is how cats handle stress.
  6. Toys out — and nothing with string, ribbon, or elastic attached. A bored cat will play with whatever is available, including the cord on the blinds.
  7. Hazards put away: cables and chargers, human food and medicines, cleaning products, and any plant on the toxic list. Check the floor as well as the counter — a cat reaches what you can see.
  8. Windows and doors secure, but not newly sealed if your cat normally goes out. Suddenly cutting a cat off from her outdoor territory is a stressor in its own right.
  9. Temperature sorted. A room that is comfortable for you at breakfast can be an oven by three o'clock; leave a window on a secure catch or a fan, and never leave a cat shut in a car-adjacent space like a conservatory in summer.
  10. Some way to see the house from where you are — a camera, a neighbour's phone number, or a friend who will look in. Remote monitoring does not replace a visit, but it turns three days of ignorance into an hour of noticing.
  11. A note for whoever might need it: your vet's details, the nearest emergency clinic, your cat's food and medication, and how to reach you. If you are gone longer than a day, someone should hold a copy of this even if nobody is expected to visit.

If you do one thing on this list and skip the rest, make it the water. If you do two, make the second the litter box.

What You Check When You Walk Back In

The absence is not over when you open the door; it is over when you have checked the four things that tell you how it went. Do this before you do anything else, especially if you were away longer than a day.

  • Water and food levels. How much was actually drunk and eaten is the single most useful piece of information you have. A cat who barely touched the water over a long absence is a reason to look closely, not to shrug.
  • The litter box. Volume and number of clumps tells you whether she was urinating normally while you were gone. A box that is barely used is a signal; so is a box with many small clumps.
  • How she looks and moves. Greeting you loudly and following you around is normal after a separation. Hiding, not coming when called, moving stiffly, or sitting hunched is not.
  • Her coat and skin. A new bald patch, particularly on the belly or the inside of the thighs, is the classic footprint of stress grooming — and it is easy to miss for weeks because you are looking at her face.

Some of what you find means "call the vet today", and it is worth knowing the list before you need it rather than after:

  • A male cat straining, squatting repeatedly, or producing little or no urine. This is a urethral obstruction, and it is a same-day emergency, not a wait-and-see. Do not assume he is better because he has gone quiet — that can mean he is getting worse.
  • Not eating for more than about a day, or not eating at all since you left. Cats who stop eating can become seriously ill quickly, and prolonged refusal to eat carries its own liver risk.
  • Repeated vomiting or diarrhoea, particularly if it continues after you are back and she has settled.
  • Laboured or open-mouthed breathing, collapse, weakness, or a seizure. Emergency, now.
  • Any sudden change in an older cat — new vocalisation at night, new litter box accidents, new hiding. In older cats, a new behaviour is a medical question first and a behavioural one second.

None of these is made more likely by a single day alone in a well-prepared house. They are made more likely by a long absence nobody checked on — which is the argument for the next section, not for never leaving.

Past 24 Hours You Need a Person, Not a Gadget

There is a clean line in every credible source on this topic, and it is not about hours. It is about people. Once an absence runs past roughly a day, the question stops being "how long can she cope" and becomes "who is going to lay eyes on her".

The reason is not that a cat cannot survive two days with enough food and water. It is that nobody can tell whether she is fine, and the things that go wrong while you are away — a feeder that jams, a water bowl that tips, a urinary blockage, a sudden illness — go wrong invisibly and get worse in the dark. A person going in once a day turns a three-day blind spot into a series of twelve-hour windows, in which almost everything is still fixable. Automatic feeders, fountains, and cameras are genuinely useful, and every one of them is a tool for the gaps between visits, not a substitute for the visits themselves.

If you are arranging cover, here is what the person actually needs, and it is more than a key:

  • Food, in measured portions, with the schedule written down — including which cat gets which bowl if there is more than one.
  • Medication, with doses and times, and what to do if a dose is missed.
  • Your vet's name and number, and the nearest out-of-hours clinic, before anything goes wrong rather than during.
  • What "normal" looks like for your cat — how much she usually eats, drinks, and uses the box — so an abnormality is visible to someone who does not live with her.
  • Instructions to actually look at the cat, not just refill bowls. A visit that is only logistics still misses the point.

Two things worth deciding before you book anything. A second cat is not cover. Companionship helps — the questionnaire study above found that cats with no other animal in the house were more likely to show separation-related signs — but two cats alone together still cannot check each other's water or notice a blockage, and cats who merely tolerate each other can become less tolerant when resources get tight. Boarding is the last resort, not the first. Cats bond to territory, and moving a cat to an unfamiliar building with unfamiliar smells is a stressor you are choosing, while a sitter coming into her own home is the option that changes least. For most cats, and almost all anxious ones, in-home care is the kinder arrangement.

If you cannot arrange a visit for a longer trip, that is the situation where boarding is genuinely better than a house left running on autopilot. It is not a failure to plan; it is the right answer to a different constraint.

If Your Cat Is the One Who Struggles

Some cats are genuinely fine for a working day and fall apart over a weekend. The signs are consistent enough to watch for, and they are easy to misread as bad behaviour when you get home.

The behaviours that the feline separation literature flags are: destructive behaviour, usually aimed at doors, blinds, or whatever is nearest the exit; excessive vocalisation, often reported as howling that starts shortly after you leave; urinating or defecating outside the box, frequently on items that carry your scent or beside the door you went through; and the quieter pair of over-grooming, which shows up as bald patches, and loss of appetite, which shows up as food left in the bowl. A cat may show only one of these, and the picture only becomes clear if you can see what happens after the door closes — which is the one genuinely good use of a home camera.

What helps is unglamorous and takes weeks rather than days. The core of it is teaching the cat that departures are boring: short, low-key exits and returns with no emotional ceremony, repeated and gradually lengthened, so that the door stops predicting a long lonely stretch. Second, give the cat a job for the hours you are gone — food in a puzzle feeder, a window perch onto something interesting, a rotation of toys rather than a heap of them — because a cat with nothing to do will find something to do. The questionnaire data above is a reminder that this is not decoration: cats in that sample with no access to toys were significantly more likely to show separation-related signs.

Third, lower the baseline while the retraining does its work. Pheromone products are the common veterinary suggestion here, and a Feliway diffuser is the version most vets name. Be clear about what you are buying: it is not a cure, it does not work for every cat, and it works best as one part of a plan that also includes the exits, the enrichment, and — if the signs are severe or persistent — a conversation with your vet, because genuine separation-related distress in cats is a recognised problem and not a personality trait to be endured. If your cat follows you room to room, why cats follow you everywhere covers where ordinary attachment ends and something worth acting on begins.

The Takeaway

How long you can leave a cat alone is not one number, and it was never going to be. It is a healthy adult cat and a working day, about 12 hours as the routine ceiling, and much less for kittens, seniors, and anyone on medication. Past a day, it stops being a question about your cat at all and becomes a question about people, because a person who looks in once a day catches almost everything while it is still fixable, and a house full of helpful machines catches almost nothing.

What tips a trip from fine to risky is almost never the number of hours. It is a water bowl that ran out, a box that should have been clean, a feeder that stopped, or a hazard nobody scanned the floor for. Those are all decisions you make before you leave, and they are the reason this article puts eleven of them in a list rather than a single figure at the top.

And the last thing to carry with you is the correction that makes the rest of it make sense: cats are not indifferent to being alone, they are quiet about it. Roughly two thirds of them are securely attached to the person in the house, and when the absence is long enough, a measurable minority show it in ways that only appear once the door closes. Prepare the house like she will notice, and then go.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I leave my cat alone overnight?

Usually yes, in the sense that most healthy adult cats will be fine, and a single overnight absence is within the range that welfare guidance treats as occasionally acceptable. It stops being reasonable when the cat is a kitten, a senior, on medication, or unwell. If the overnight turns into a habit — most nights, most weeks — it is no longer occasional, and the guidance that puts a routine ceiling at about 12 hours is aimed exactly at that pattern.

Can I leave my cat alone for 24 hours?

Some healthy adult cats cope with 24 hours, and it is the figure the internet quotes most often. It is also the point at which credible welfare and veterinary sources stop describing the absence as a solo decision: past roughly a day, the recommendation is that someone checks in on the cat once a day, because the risks that matter over that window — a blocked urethra, a failed feeder, an untasted water bowl — are the ones nobody notices without eyes on the cat. Treat 24 hours as the ceiling, not the target.

How long can a kitten be left alone?

A few hours at most for a kitten under four months, and not overnight. Kittens that young are eating several small meals a day, have limited bladder control, and treat every surface in the house as something to climb. From four months to a year, a short day with a check-in is reasonable once the house has been kitten-proofed. Building the tolerance slowly — starting with five to ten minutes, several times a day — teaches the kitten that you come back, which is a lesson worth learning before the first long absence rather than during it.

Do cats get lonely when you leave them?

Sometimes, and less visibly than dogs. In a 2019 study in Current Biology, about 65% of cats and kittens were classed as securely attached to their caregiver, using the same test that is run with human infants. In a 2020 questionnaire study of 223 cats published in PLOS ONE, 13.45% of the cats met at least one behavioural criterion for a separation-related problem, with destructive behaviour and excessive vocalisation the most common. So the honest answer is that many cats are attached to us, a minority show measurable distress at being left, and the ones who do tend to do it quietly.

Should I get a pet sitter for my cat?

If you will be away for more than about a day, yes — at least one visit a day, from someone who will look at the cat and not only the bowls. Daytime absences within a working day do not need cover in a healthy adult cat. A professional sitter, a neighbour, or a friend all work, provided they have your vet's number, your cat's food and medication details, and a clear description of what this particular cat looks like when she is well.

Are automatic feeders and water fountains enough?

They are useful, and they are not cover. A feeder that fails silently and a fountain that stops circulating both do their damage while nobody is watching, which is why the standard advice is to test any device for several days before you rely on it and to leave backup food and water in separate containers regardless. Over a working day they add real value. Over an overnight or a weekend, a device plus nobody is a setup where the first sign of trouble is the thing you find when you get home.

How do I know if my cat has separation anxiety?

You look for behaviour that happens only when you are gone: destructive scratching around doors and exits, vocalising that starts shortly after you leave, urinating or defecating outside the box on items that smell of you, over-grooming that produces bald patches, and eating less while you are away. A camera is the single most useful tool, because none of this is visible while you are in the house. Rule out medical causes first with your vet, since a bladder problem can look identical to a stress problem, and then work on unemotional, gradually lengthened departures plus enrichment.
