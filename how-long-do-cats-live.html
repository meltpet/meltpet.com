How Long Do Cats Live?

Every website answers this with the same range, and the range is not a measurement. The largest feline life table ever published answers it with a curve instead — and the curve says something more useful than "12 to 18 years". | 2026-10-27 · MeltPet · 15 min read

Last updated: 2026-10-27

The Short Version: Three Numbers, Not One

A kitten born into the UK companion-cat population can expect 11.74 years. A cat that has already reached its tenth birthday can expect 5.46 more, which puts its expected total near 15.5. And a cat that sits one kilogram away from the median weight of its breed and sex — in either direction — is associated with about 0.2 fewer years.

Those three sentences contain the whole answer, and they are not contradictory. They are three different questions. The first is what a population produces. The second is what an individual cat, conditional on having already survived, should expect. The third is the only one of the three that can be changed by anything you do tomorrow.

Almost every article on this subject answers the first question with a range — twelve to eighteen years, or twelve to fifteen, or "indoor cats live to seventeen" — and then stops. That range is not a finding from any study. It is closer to a folk average, repeated until it looks like a fact, and it hides the two things that actually matter: that a cat's remaining life expectancy depends heavily on how old it already is, and that the spread inside the population is enormous compared with the difference between any two averages.

This page works from one primary source, the 2024 life tables for UK companion cats built from the VetCompass clinical-record network, and from one contrasting study, a 3,108-cat necropsy series from a US teaching hospital. Between them they cover most of what is actually known. Where a number comes from arithmetic on their published tables rather than from a sentence in the paper, it is labelled as such, because the difference between a measurement and a derivation is exactly what goes wrong in this topic.

One thing to settle before reading on. Everything below describes populations and associations. Nothing in it predicts your cat. A cat with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy at four is not protected by a breed average of ten years, and a cat at body condition 4 with good teeth at fourteen is not doomed by a population figure of eleven. The numbers are for planning and for knowing which levers exist — not for prognosis.

Where "12 to 18 Years" Actually Comes From

The range is everywhere, so it is worth asking what it measures. It is not the result of a study that followed cats from birth to death and reported a spread of outcomes. It behaves much more like an average of averages: the midpoint of what veterinary practices see in adult patients, widened by the observation that some cats reach their late teens.

Two mechanisms inflate it.

First, it describes cats that are already patients. A figure drawn from the age at death of cats seen in practice excludes the kittens that never made it to a first appointment, the young cats killed on roads and never recorded as a death, and the cats whose owners never sought care at all. Every one of those omissions pushes the number up.

Second, it quietly changes what is being measured. There is a difference between life expectancy at birth — the average total lifespan of a whole cohort — and the average age at death of the cats you happen to observe. Those are different statistics, and the second is always higher when early deaths go unrecorded. A phrase like "cats live 12 to 18 years" usually blends the second statistic with a handful of memorable old cats.

None of that makes the folklore malicious. It makes it unfalsifiable, which is worse: no observation about your cat can contradict it, so it cannot guide a decision. A life table can, because a life table is a set of numbers you can check.

The practical cost of the folklore is not that it is too high or too low. It is that it arrives with no structure. A person told "12 to 18" has no way to convert that into a plan for a ten-year-old cat, no idea which health problems start when, and no sense of which risks are front-loaded into young adulthood and which are back-loaded into the last two years. Those three pieces of structure are what the rest of this page supplies.

The Best Number We Have, and What It Measures

In 2024, a team at the Royal Veterinary College and National Chung Hsing University published the first life tables for the UK companion-cat population. They used the VetCompass network of primary-care practices — anonymised clinical records, not survey responses — and built the tables from 7,936 confirmed deaths in cats under primary veterinary care in 2019.

The headline result: life expectancy at age 0 was 11.74 years (95% confidence interval 11.61 to 11.87).

That is a lower number than most readers expect, and it is worth being precise about why. Life expectancy at age 0 answers a specific question: if you took a newborn kitten entering this population and averaged the total lifespan across all the ways a cat's life can go, what would you get? To answer it you need to know how many cats die in each year of life, which is what a life table is for. It is built from three columns, and understanding them makes every other number on this page usable:

  • l(x) — how many of the original cohort are still alive at exact age x. The study starts with 7,936 and counts down.
  • q(x) — the probability that a cat alive at age x dies before its next birthday. This is the column that tells you where risk sits.
  • e(x) — the expected number of additional years, at age x. This is the column that applies to a living cat, and it is the one nobody quotes.

The confidence interval is tight — a fifth of a year either side — because the sample is large. That tightness is worth noticing for a different reason: it means the 11.74 figure is not statistical noise. If it disagrees with the number on your favourite website, the disagreement is about method, not about sampling error.

The same paper also produced the numbers that make the rest of this page possible: separate tables for females and males, for crossbred and purebred cats, a breed table for the twelve breeds with at least fifteen recorded deaths, and two regression models — one for dying before three years of age, one for how long a cat lived if it died after nine months.

Why the Population Number Looks Too Low

Three things hold 11.74 down, and none of them is a defect.

Kitten mortality is real and it is front-loaded. In the life table, the probability of dying in the first year was 0.039 and in the second year 0.047 — slightly higher, because kittens that survive the first weeks still face the traffic and infectious-disease window of a young animal finding its feet. Three point nine percent sounds small, and in a single year it is. Compounded across a whole population born together, it removes a meaningful slice of total years, because every early death subtracts decades rather than years.

Most recorded deaths involved a decision. Of the 7,936 deaths in the study, 6,642 (83.7 percent) were by euthanasia, 987 (12.4 percent) were unassisted, and 307 (3.9 percent) had no method recorded. A pet population's lifespan is therefore partly a record of when owners and veterinarians chose to stop, which is a fact about medicine and money as much as about biology. It is also why the study could show that euthanased cats had a higher life expectancy at age 0 — 12.09 years against 9.81 for unassisted deaths — than cats that died without intervention. Access to end-of-life care and long life travel together in these records.

It is one country, one year, one care system. The authors compare their result with life tables from the USA (11.18 years) and Japan (4.2 years) and note that the spread is largely methodological and demographic rather than biological. A number is only meaningful alongside the population it was measured in.

The honest summary is that 11.74 is not "how long cats live" in the way a person means it. It is what a population of cats produces, including the ones that died at eight months. If your cat is currently asleep on a chair and aged six, that population figure has almost nothing left to tell you — which is the subject of the next section.

The Number That Actually Applies to Your Cat

This is the part almost every article omits, and it is one subtraction.

Life expectancy at age 0 answers a question about kittens. But a cat that is alive today at age 10 has already survived the years in which the population loses its members fastest in proportional terms. It is no longer a random draw from the cohort; it is a survivor. The life table handles this directly, because it recomputes expectancy at every age:

e(x) values are from Table 1 of the published life table. The implied total is our arithmetic — current age plus e(x) — and it describes the cohort average, not any individual cat.

Look at what happened between the first row and the fifth. The population figure is 11.74. The figure for a cat that has already reached 10 is 15.46. That is a gap of 3.7 years created entirely by survivorship — and it is the reason two people can both be quoting "the science" and disagree by four years without either of them being wrong.

There is a second consequence, less obvious and more useful. The gap between e(0) and e(10) is not spread evenly. Most of it is created in the first three years, where q(x) peaks at 0.047, and it is created by a small number of causes: trauma, infectious disease, congenital problems, and the accidents that young animals have. By the time a cat is eight, the annual risk of death is still under 5 percent. The danger of being a cat is concentrated early, then plateaus, then climbs again after about fourteen.

The practical version: if your cat is between three and nine years old, the population average is not a useful thing to carry around. The relevant questions are about what accumulates silently in that plateau — weight, teeth, kidneys, blood pressure — because that is the window in which the levers still move.

What Share of Cats Reach 10, 15 and 20

Averages hide the shape. The share of a cohort still alive at a given age does not, and it can be read straight off the l(x) column: how many of the original 7,936 cats are still alive at each age.

Each share is the l(x) entry divided by 7,936 — arithmetic on the published table, not a figure stated in the paper.

Two things follow.

Reaching ten is the norm, not the achievement. Nearly two thirds of cats get there. That is a useful corrective to the way owners talk about a ten-year-old as elderly: by the standards of the population, that cat has done the ordinary thing. Reaching fifteen is different — about one in three — and reaching twenty is uncommon enough that roughly one cat in twenty-four does it.

The last five years are where the distribution thins fastest. Between 10 and 15, the cohort loses about 45 percent of its remaining members. Between 15 and 20 it loses another 88 percent of what was left. This is why an average is such a poor tool for an old cat: the same number is being asked to describe a 12-year-old with a good body condition and a 12-year-old in renal failure.

If you want the single most honest sentence about cat lifespan, it is this: most cats reach ten, about a third reach fifteen, and the median is somewhere in the low teens — which is a distribution, not a promise. A median in that region is also why the "12 to 18" range survives: it brackets the middle of the distribution loosely enough to feel right.

The Shape of the Risk: Why Year Nine Matters

The q(x) column is the least quoted and most decision-relevant part of the table. It gives the probability that a cat alive at a given age dies before its next birthday — the annual hazard, not the lifetime one.

The paper's own summary of this column is precise and worth keeping: annual mortality "increased with age from year interval 3 to 4", and the probability "did not exceed 0.05 before year 9". That is a measurable threshold, and it is the one place where a life table tells you something a caring owner can act on: for the first eight years, the annual risk of death is under one in twenty. After that it roughly doubles about every four years — 0.069 at ten, 0.131 at thirteen, 0.212 at fifteen, 0.341 at seventeen.

What changes at that boundary is not that cats suddenly age. It is that the diseases which have been accumulating without symptoms become the things that kill. Kidney disease, dental disease, hyperthyroidism, heart disease and cancer all have long silent phases, and the arithmetic of annual hazard is really the arithmetic of how long a silent phase can run before it becomes the recorded cause of death.

That is why the useful intervention for a nine-year-old cat is not a supplement. It is a baseline: bloodwork, blood pressure, a urine sample and a weight, taken while the cat still looks well, so that the next year's numbers have something to be compared against. A creatinine of 2.1 mg/dL means one thing next to a two-year history of 1.4 and something quite different on its own.

The Four Levers That Have a Published Coefficient

Most advice about longevity is unfalsifiable. This study is unusual because it fitted a model to cats that died after nine months of age and produced actual estimates in years for four factors. The model's baseline — a female, neutered, crossbred cat sitting exactly at the median adult weight for its breed and sex — comes out at 12.68 years.

Body-weight deviation−0.02 years per 100 g (−0.03 to −0.01)Lifespan fell for every 100 g of increase or decrease from the breed-and-sex median

Read as arithmetic, that gives you a way to compare factors honestly. Being male and entire and purebred, at exactly median weight, is 12.68 minus 1.20 minus 1.07 minus 1.27 — about 9.1 years in the model. Those are additive estimates from a linear model fitted to population records, not a sentence about any particular cat, but they are the right order of magnitude and they rank the factors for you.

Two cautions. The estimates describe cats in the UK primary-care population in 2019, and a linear model applied far outside the observed range will mislead. And every one of these is an association in clinical records: nobody randomised kittens to be entire. The mechanism behind the neuter effect is not mysterious — roaming, fighting and traffic — but the number is still an association.

Weight: The Only Lever You Touch Every Day

The body-weight coefficient is the smallest number in that table and the most interesting, because it is the only one that responds to anything you do.

Take the estimate literally for a moment. Every 100 grams away from the median for your cat's breed and sex is associated with 0.02 fewer years. Scale it up: one kilogram is ten of those steps, so about 0.2 years per kilogram; three kilograms is about 0.6 years. For a cat whose breed-and-sex median is 4.5 kg and who weighs 7.5 kg, that is roughly three kilograms of deviation and a sixth of a year of expected lifespan — on top of everything else being overweight does.

Two features of that number are easy to miss and both matter.

It is symmetric. The model uses the absolute deviation, so being two kilograms under the median counts the same as being two kilograms over. That is not a licence to dismiss overweight as harmless; it reflects that a cat below its median weight may be losing muscle to an untreated disease, and the coefficient cannot tell the difference between a lean athlete and a cat with cancer. Both show up as "not at the median".

It is a population estimate on top of a much larger effect. The weight coefficient is the lifespan penalty of the deviation itself. It is not the cost of what the extra fat does — diabetes, joint disease, hepatic lipidosis risk if the cat stops eating. A separate study of 1,457 cats in 27 practices followed for four and a half years found obese cats were 3.9 times as likely to develop diabetes mellitus, 4.9 times as likely to need veterinary care for lameness, and 2.3 times as likely to develop non-allergic skin disease. Those are the mechanisms behind the lost years, and they are why the weight lever is worth more than its coefficient suggests.

What makes weight different from the other three factors is that it is measurable at home, weekly, in grams, and it responds within weeks. If you want to track it properly rather than by eye, a digital kitchen scale that reads in grams will do for kittens and small adults weighed in a box; for most adults the veterinary scale at a quarterly visit is the better instrument, because a bathroom scale cannot resolve the 200-gram changes that matter.

The protocol for actually changing the number is its own subject and it is not simply "feed less" — a cat cannot crash-diet the way a dog can, because the liver sets a floor. That is covered in the feline weight-loss plan, which is the correct next page if the weight deviation is upward.

Sex and Neuter Status: The Biggest Early Effect

The later-life model gives male sex a cost of 1.20 years and neutering a gain of 1.07. Those are modest. The early-life model is where the size of the neuter effect shows up.

For death before three years of age, the study fitted a separate logistic model. Entire cats had 4.29 times the odds of dying before three (95% CI 3.72 to 4.95) compared with neutered cats, and purebred cats had 1.83 times the odds (1.50 to 2.23) compared with crossbreds. A four-fold odds ratio on early death is a different scale of effect from a one-year adjustment at the end of life, and it is consistent with the mechanism: entire cats roam further, fight more, and meet cars more often.

It also explains why the sex gap behaves the way it does. Females had a life expectancy at age 0 of 12.51 years against 11.18 for males — a gap of 1.33 years — but the paper reports that the gap narrowed with age and was no longer significantly different by the 15 to 16 year interval. If the male disadvantage were a fundamental difference in ageing, the gap would persist. Instead it fades, which is what you would expect if the disadvantage lives in the young-adult years and is manufactured by behaviour and access to risk.

The practical reading is not "neuter your cat and gain a year". It is that the first three years carry a hazard that is heavily behavioural, and that most of what a young cat dies of is not disease. If your cat is under three and goes outside, that is the window where the numbers are worst — and it is also the window in which the choice is easiest to make.

How to read it without over-reading it.

The ranking is not a breed ranking of hardiness. The Sphynx figure, 6.68 years, comes from 18 deaths and carries a confidence interval running from 4.53 to 8.83. The paper also notes that the Sphynx had the narrowest range of recorded lifespans of any breed in the table — 0.13 to 14.67 years — which is another way of saying the estimate rests on a thin, homogeneous sample. A number with a two-sided interval that wide is a signal to look at breed-specific health problems, not a prediction about a kitten.

Population association versus individual risk. Crossbred cats as a group outlived purebreds by about 1.48 years at age 0, and the later-life model put being purebred at −1.27 years. But "crossbred" is not one thing; it is every cat without papers, spanning the entire range of care, and the table's own range for crossbreds is 0.00 to 26.69 years.

Your breed guide is the better page for the mechanism. A number tells you that British cats in this dataset averaged 9.58 years; it does not tell you that the breed's cobby build makes extra weight easy to miss until it is two or three pounds. For the mechanism, the breed pages are more use than the table — the British Shorthair guide, Maine Coon guide, Ragdoll guide, Bengal guide and Sphynx guide each cover their own.

Indoor, Outdoor, and the Study That Disagrees

The most repeated claim in this subject is that indoor cats live to fifteen and outdoor cats live to three. It is worth looking at what the evidence actually shows, because it is neither that clean nor that dramatic.

A 2022 study in PLOS ONE examined 3,108 cats that had a post-mortem examination at a US veterinary teaching hospital between 1989 and 2019. Housing was recorded for 2,284 of them. The median age at death was 9.43 years for indoor-only cats, 9.82 for indoor-outdoor cats and 7.25 for outdoor-only cats. Those differences were statistically significant, and the outdoor-only group was the one driving them: outdoor cats died younger than indoor-only cats, and also younger than indoor-outdoor cats. There was no significant difference between indoor-only and indoor-outdoor cats.

Two further details from that study keep the finding honest.

Housing did not survive the multivariable model. Once sex, neuter status and viral infection status were included, environmental status was not a significant predictor of age at death. FeLV positivity and being entire were. Housing is entangled with all of those: entire cats are more likely to be outdoor cats, and outdoor cats are more likely to be FeLV positive. The clean story — "outside kills cats" — is really "outside is where the young entire cats meet the risks that kill them".

The categories are coarse. The study counted a cat with balcony access, or one that had escaped briefly, as indoor-only; and it counted a cat with access to a barn as outdoor-only. A cat that spends ten supervised minutes in an enclosed garden and a cat that roams two miles a night are both "indoor-outdoor". Grouping them together hides most of the actual risk difference.

The honest synthesis: unrestricted outdoor access increases exposure to a set of known causes of premature death — vehicle trauma, fights and bite abscesses, FeLV and FIV, poisoning, getting lost — and the effect shows up most strongly in young cats. What it does not do is turn every outdoor cat into a three-year cat. The "outdoor cats live 2 to 5 years" figure that circulates widely has no primary source behind it; the best available comparison, on the best data available, is a gap of roughly two years in median age at death, concentrated in cats whose outdoor access is unrestricted.

If your cat is indoors, the trade is not free: indoor life removes the trauma risk and substitutes a different set of problems, most of them related to inactivity and environment. That is where enrichment stops being a nice-to-have. A cat that cannot hunt needs something to do with its day, and the cheapest version of that is a puzzle feeder that makes the food take ten minutes instead of ninety seconds.

Why Two Good Studies Give 9 and 12

The two studies above disagree by about two and a half years on the central number: a median age at death of 9.07 years in the US necropsy series, against a life expectancy at age 0 of 11.74 in the UK primary-care tables. Neither is wrong, and the gap is instructive.

They measured different things. One is a mean total lifespan for a cohort at birth; the other is a median age at death in a group of cats that died. Those are not the same statistic, and a median will sit lower than a mean when there is a long right tail of very old cats.

They counted different cats. The US series is cats that had a necropsy at a teaching hospital — a population filtered toward the diagnostically difficult, the referred and the deceased-after-illness. It was 84.2 percent mixed breed and drawn from one institution over three decades. The UK tables are primary-care records from a national network, which is closer to the population a pet owner actually belongs to.

The US series tells you what cats die of. Cancer was the most common pathophysiologic cause of death at 35.81 percent of cases, and renal histological abnormalities were present in 62.84 percent of cats while being the primary cause of death in only 13.06 percent. That second pair of numbers is one of the most useful in the literature: kidneys look bad in most old cats, but they are what killed one in eight. Finding a problem is not the same as finding the cause.

The lesson generalises beyond cats. When two credible sources give you different lifespans, the useful question is not which one is right but which population you belong to. If your cat is a pet with primary veterinary care, the 11.74-style figure is the relevant family of estimate. If you are reading a pathology series, you are reading about the distribution of causes, not the length of life.

Life Stages: The Official Cutoffs

If a single number is the wrong tool, the right tool is a set of stages with defined boundaries — which the profession does have. The 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines replaced the older six-stage scheme with four age-based stages plus a fifth that is not age-specific.

StageAgeWhat changes in the guidelines' framing
KittenBirth to 1 yearVaccination series, parasite control, growth nutrition, early behaviour and handling

Mature adult7 to 10 yearsScreening for age-related disease begins in earnest, nutrition adjusts to changing metabolism
SeniorOver 10 yearsEarly detection becomes the point of the visit; more frequent examination and diagnostics
End of lifeNot age-specificHandled in separate end-of-life guidance, because it can arrive at any age

Two observations about this scheme.

It is coarser than the biology. A ten-year-old Maine Coon and a ten-year-old Burmese are both "senior" on this table, and their expected remaining years are not the same. The stages are a framework for what to do at a visit, not a prognosis.

It puts the boundary in the right place for practical purposes. A cat is "senior" for a long time — from ten onwards, which in the life table is nearly two thirds of the population and, on average, another five and a half years. The stage label is not a warning that the end is near. It is a change of method: from screening occasionally to screening on a schedule, and from treating what is presented to looking for what is not yet visible.

If you want to convert a cat's age into human-equivalent terms for a family conversation, that is a different calculation with its own assumptions, handled in the pet age calculator. The conversion is a communication device, not a lifespan estimate, and mixing the two is how people end up believing a nine-year-old cat is "sixty-three in human years" and therefore near the end.

What Actually Adds Years

Stripping the numbers down to what a person can do, there are six things with real support, in rough order of how much they matter.

1. Keep the weight at the median. This is the only factor in the model you can change by next month. Weigh monthly, feed in grams, and treat treats as part of the ration rather than as something that happens outside it. The coefficient is 0.2 years per kilogram, and the disease associations behind it are much larger than the coefficient. If the number is going up, the protocol is in the weight-loss plan.

2. Manage outdoor access rather than idealising either choice. The evidence supports restricting unrestricted roaming, especially before age three, when the hazard is highest and when the outdoor risk is mostly trauma. A catio, a harness, or simply keeping the cat in after dark are all real risk reductions; none of them requires turning an outdoor cat into an indoor one.

3. Neuter before the roaming years. The 4.29-fold odds of dying before three for entire cats is the largest single effect in the study, and it is behavioural in mechanism. This is a decision with a deadline rather than a preference.

4. Get a baseline at nine, not at fourteen. Annual hazard passes 5 percent in the tenth year. Bloodwork, blood pressure, a urine sample and a weight at nine gives you a reference point for everything that follows. Cats hide renal disease until two thirds of function is gone, so the first abnormal result is often the first evidence — which is why the comparison to a previous year is worth more than the result itself. The dehydration pages explain the early physical signs that go with this: the at-home checks and why cats dehydrate so easily.

5. Treat the mouth. Dental disease is the most common chronic problem in cats and one of the most ignored, in part because cats eat through pain. It matters twice: directly, because infected gums are a source of chronic inflammation and occasional bacteraemia, and indirectly, because a cat with a painful mouth eats less, loses muscle and drifts below its median weight — which the coefficient above treats as sympathetically as being overweight. The overview is in the cat dental care guide.

6. Watch water and urine. Kidney disease is the classic slow disease of old cats: 62.84 percent of the cats in the necropsy series had renal histological abnormalities, and it was the recorded cause of death in 13.06 percent. It is also the one where early detection changes the timeline most, because the interventions — water intake, phosphate, blood pressure, protein — all work better earlier. The prevention overview is here, and the intake arithmetic is in the water calculator.

What is not on the list is worth naming too. There is no evidence that a specific supplement extends feline lifespan; there is no study showing that grain-free food, raw food or a particular water source adds years; and the hair-and-teeth folk remedies are folk remedies. The six items above are unglamorous, which is probably why the market for the alternatives is so large.

The Oldest Cat on Record, and What Outliers Mean

The oldest cat ever verified is Creme Puff, born on 3 August 1967 and died on 6 August 2005 in Austin, Texas — 38 years and 3 days, according to Guinness World Records. Her owner had also kept the previous record holder, which is either a coincidence or a hint about husbandry, and the reported diet included dry food, broccoli, eggs, turkey and a small amount of red wine every second day.

That last detail is a good illustration of why outliers make bad advice. A single cat reaching 38 tells you that the feline body can, in principle, run for nearly four decades. It tells you nothing about which of the thousand things that cat experienced mattered, because there is no comparison group. In the UK life table the longest recorded lifespan in the dataset was 26.69 years for a crossbred cat, and about 4 percent of the cohort was alive at 20 — so even the realistic tail of the distribution is far below the record.

The useful way to hold both facts is this: the ceiling of the species is high enough that a well-managed cat has a real chance of a long life, and the distribution is wide enough that no individual cat's outcome is determined by an average. Creme Puff is not a target and 11.74 is not a verdict. They are the two ends of the same description.

When "Old" Is a Diagnosis, and When It Is Not

The most common error in the last third of a cat's life is attributing treatable disease to age. The second most common is the opposite: treating every change as a crisis.

These are the changes that should not be filed under "just old":

  • Weight loss with a normal appetite. Hyperthyroidism, diabetes, chronic enteropathy and cancer all present this way. A cat that is eating and shrinking needs bloodwork, not a richer food.
  • Drinking and urinating more. Kidney disease, diabetes and hyperthyroidism are the three common answers, and all three are treatable. This is also the change owners most often explain away because the litter tray got heavier gradually.
  • Muscle loss along the spine. A bony spine with a sagging belly is muscle wasting, not thinness, and it changes the weight conversation entirely — the target is not the same for a cat losing muscle.
  • New vocalisation at night, or pacing. In an older cat this can be pain, hypertension, hyperthyroidism or cognitive change. It is a symptom, not a personality.
  • Sudden appetite loss in a cat carrying extra weight. This is the one to act on same-day rather than this week, because a cat that stops eating mobilises fat into its liver faster than the liver can process it. That is the single most dangerous acute scenario in feline medicine, and it is covered in detail in the weight page.
  • Vomiting that changes character. Frequency, colour or timing — the colour guide is a reasonable first pass on how urgent it is: what the colour tells you.

And here is the counterweight. A fourteen-year-old cat that sleeps more, jumps less, and takes longer to get up is not necessarily sick. Those are the ordinary mechanics of an old animal, and the point of a baseline at nine is that you can tell the ordinary slope from the new deviation. A cat that is slower but eating, drinking, grooming, using the tray and interacting is a cat that is ageing. A cat that has changed in one of the six ways above is a cat that needs a diagnosis.

The financial side of this is not a footnote either. The last two years of a cat's life are usually the most expensive, and the arithmetic of whether insurance was worth it turns entirely on when it was bought. That comparison is in the insurance analysis; the short version is that policies priced on a young cat's risk stop being priced that way at exactly the point you need them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average lifespan of a cat?

The largest published estimate is 11.74 years, the life expectancy at age 0 for UK companion cats in a life table built from 7,936 recorded deaths. That is a population average for cats born into that population, and it includes kittens that died in their first year. For a cat that has already reached a given age, use the conditional figure instead: a cat aged 10 has 5.46 more years on average, which puts its expected total at about 15.5.

Do indoor cats live longer than outdoor cats?

In a 3,108-cat necropsy study, outdoor-only cats had a median age at death of 7.25 years against 9.43 for indoor-only cats and 9.82 for cats with both indoor and outdoor access. Outdoor-only cats died younger than either other group, but indoor-only and indoor-outdoor cats were not significantly different. Note that in that study's multivariable model, housing was not a significant predictor once sex, neuter status and infection status were accounted for.

How long does a cat live after it turns 10?

About 5.46 more years on average, so an expected total near 15.5 rather than 11.7. The life table recomputes expectancy at every age: 8.81 more years at age 5, 5.46 at age 10, 4.24 at age 12 and 2.72 at age 15. A cat that has survived to 10 is not an average cat any more, because the cats that died young are no longer in its comparison group.

Do female cats live longer than male cats?

Yes, by about 1.2 to 1.3 years. Life expectancy at age 0 was 12.51 years for females and 11.18 for males, and in the later-life model male sex cost 1.20 years. The gap shrinks with age and was no longer statistically significant by the 15 to 16 year interval, which makes sense: the disadvantage is concentrated in the young-adult years, where roaming and trauma do the damage.

Do purebred cats live shorter lives?

On average, yes. Crossbred cats had a life expectancy at age 0 of 11.89 years against 10.41 for purebreds, and in the later-life model being purebred cost 1.27 years. The breed table spans 14.42 years for Burmese to 6.68 for Sphynx, but several of those rows rest on 15 to 20 deaths, so the ranking is a clue rather than a prediction for an individual cat.

Does neutering extend a cat's life?

The association is real and large. Entire cats had 4.29 times the odds of dying before three years of age compared with neutered cats, and among cats that died after nine months, being neutered was associated with 1.07 more years of lifespan. Both numbers come from the same UK life-table study, and both are associations in clinical records rather than results from a randomised trial.

What is the oldest cat ever recorded?

Creme Puff, born 3 August 1967 and died 6 August 2005 in Austin, Texas, at 38 years and 3 days, according to Guinness World Records. In the UK life table the longest recorded lifespan in the dataset was 26.69 years for a crossbred cat, and only about 4 percent of the cohort was still alive at 20, so records like Creme Puff sit far outside the distribution rather than at its edge.
