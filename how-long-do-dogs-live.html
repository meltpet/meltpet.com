How Long Do Dogs Live?

The UK's largest life-table study puts the average at 11.23 years. The eight-year gap between breeds is the useful part — and it does not follow size the way the internet says it does.

Last updated: 2026-11-08

The Short Version: Three Numbers, Not One

11.23 years. 4.53 to 12.72. 1.8 years. Almost every article on this question answers with a single figure and stops. The three above are the same answer told properly, because each one measures something different.

The first is the population average: life expectancy at birth for dogs under primary veterinary care in the United Kingdom, built from 30,563 dogs that died between January 2016 and July 2020. This is the best number anyone has for "how long does a dog live" in the abstract. Note the word average. It is not the typical age at which dogs die in your neighbourhood, and it is not a prediction for the animal asleep on your floor.

The second is the spread. In that same table, the longest-lived breed sits at 12.72 years and the shortest at 4.53. An eight-year gap between breeds is not noise, and it is far wider than the gap between males and females, or between a dog fed carefully and one fed by the bowl. If you want one sentence from this page to remember, it is that breed does more of the work than almost anything else in the dog's control.

The third is the only figure here that responds to something you do every day. In a two-decade feeding study of Labrador Retrievers, dogs fed 25 per cent less than their paired littermates, on the same diet, lived a median 1.8 years longer. That is the size of the prize available from body condition — smaller than the breed gap, larger than most supplements people buy, and considerably better evidenced.

Everything below is the arithmetic behind those three numbers, the reasons to distrust each of them slightly, and the part of the answer that applies to a dog who is already eight.

Where "10 to 13 Years" Comes From

Ask a vet how long a dog lives and you will usually hear a range: ten to thirteen, twelve to fifteen, "depends on the breed". Nobody is being evasive. That range is a verbal average with the confidence interval removed, and it comes from the same kind of data, compressed until it fits in a consultation.

The compression is where the useful information goes. "10 to 13 years" is roughly where the middle of the distribution sits once every breed is poured into one bucket. It is accurate in the way that "the average European is 1.7 metres tall" is accurate: true of the group, useless for predicting the person in front of you, and completely silent about the fact that the group contains both a Jack Russell Terrier and a French Bulldog.

The other reason the generic figure feels too high is selection. People quote the age at which dogs they knew died. Those dogs are survivors. A dog that died at three from a road accident, a congenital heart defect or a splenic mass contributes nothing to the anecdote, and a lot to the life table. The population number is lower than the folklore number precisely because the population number counts the dogs that did not make it.

So the honest answer to "how long do dogs live" is a distribution, not a number — and the only way to narrow it is to ask a second question: which dog?

What 11.23 Years Actually Measures

The study behind the figure is a life table, which is a demographer's tool rather than a veterinarian's one. It starts from a group of deaths and works backwards to answer a question about the living: if a dog were born into this population today, how many years would it be expected to live, given the death rates observed in each year of age?

That phrasing matters, because it means the number describes a population assembled from deaths that happened in a specific window, not a cohort of puppies followed from birth. The study's own accounting is worth reading: 32,390 candidate records were screened, and 30,563 confirmed deaths (94.4 per cent) went into the analysis. Of those, 47.7 per cent were female, 57.4 per cent were neutered, 78.4 per cent were purebred, 21.3 per cent were crossbred, and 0.3 per cent had no recorded breed.

Three consequences follow, and they are the three things most articles skip.

It is a UK primary-care population. These are dogs registered at veterinary practices that contribute to a surveillance network, not dogs on a breeder's premises, not dogs in a shelter, not dogs that never saw a vet. Referral hospitals see sicker animals; breeding kennels see a different population again. Every one of those settings would produce a different table.

It is period, not cohort. The dogs died between 2016 and 2020. A puppy born this year is being born into a population with different surgery rates, different breeding practices, different food, and a different distribution of flat-faced breeds. Life expectancy tables are always a snapshot of the recent past, never a forecast.

It counts deaths, not causes. A dog killed by a car at two and a dog euthanised at fourteen for cancer enter the same column at different ages. The table does not care why, and it cannot tell you what will happen to your dog. It can only tell you how fast the cohort emptied out as it aged — which, it turns out, is enough to be genuinely useful.

If you want the same exercise for the other species, the cat life-table guide runs the numbers for cats, which behave quite differently — and the age calculator converts a dog's age into the human-year equivalent if that is the framing you find easier to hold.

Two rows are worth pausing on before we go further. The crossbred average — 11.82 years, on 6,511 dogs, the largest single group in the table — sits above the Labrador Retriever and well above several pedigree breeds. Those two rows are the reason the next three sections exist. The first candidate explanation, that smaller means longer-lived, does not survive contact with the table.

Why That Table Is Not a Ranking

Before using any row to reassure or alarm yourself, note what the column of dog counts is doing. The American Bulldog estimate rests on 129 dogs and the Beagle on 172; the crossbred average rests on 6,511. A confidence interval of 7.16 to 8.33 for a breed with 129 dogs is telling you that the true average could be almost anywhere in that range. Comparing a 129-dog breed with a 2,500-dog breed to two decimal places is not precision, it is decoration.

Breed labels are also messier than they look. They come from clinical records, which means they reflect what the owner or the practice wrote down. "Husky" in a UK practice record may mean a Siberian Husky, an Alaskan Malamute cross, or something the receptionist guessed. "Crossbred" is a single bucket containing every mixed dog in the country, from a Cavalier-Poodle to a farm collie. Those labels are good enough for population work and not good enough for a claim about your dog's bloodline.

And eighteen breeds is eighteen breeds. The table is not a sample of all breeds, ranked. It is the list of breeds with enough deaths in the dataset to build a life table. A breed that is absent is not necessarily long-lived or short-lived; it is simply too thinly represented to say anything about. If your breed is not here, the honest answer is that nobody has published a life table for it from this population.

Finally, a breed average is not a bloodline, and a life table is not a prognosis. Within any of these rows there are lines that die young of a single inherited condition and lines that reach fifteen. The row tells you where the middle of a distribution sits. It does not tell you where your dog sits in it.

That said, the differences in this table are large enough to be real, and the pattern in them is not random. Which brings us to the part of the literature that is genuinely strange.

The Size Paradox: Large Dogs Age Faster

Across mammal species, bigger usually means longer-lived. Elephants outlive mice by decades, whales outlive dolphins, and body mass is one of the better single predictors of lifespan in comparative biology. Dogs invert it. Within this one species, the bigger animals tend to die younger, and the effect is strong enough that a 2013 analysis in The American Naturalist took it as its starting point.

That study looked at death records from 74 breeds and asked a sharper question than "do big dogs die young": at which stage of life is the cost paid? A large dog could die earlier because it suffers higher mortality in its first year, because it starts ageing sooner, because its baseline risk is elevated throughout, or because it deteriorates faster once deterioration begins. Those are four different mechanisms, and they imply four different things about what you would see in a vet's clinic.

The answer, in their analysis, was the last one. There was no clear relationship between body size and the age at which senescence began. The baseline hazard was only slightly higher in large dogs. What tracked body size closely was the rate of ageing — how quickly mortality risk climbs with each year. In the authors' summary, large dogs die young mainly because they age quickly.

Put plainly: a Great Dane and a Jack Russell may both be healthy at six. But the Dane's risk of dying in the next twelve months rises much faster from that point than the terrier's does, and the gap compounds. This is why the size effect shows up so dramatically in the breed-level literature and so weakly in any single dog's chart.

The mechanism is still argued about — growth rate, insulin-like growth factor signalling, and the simple fact that more cells dividing for longer gives cancer more chances all have support. Nobody has a lever you can pull. Which is why the size finding is interesting rather than actionable.

But Size Does Not Order This Table

Here is the part that should make you suspicious of every "small dogs live longer" article you have read. Take the two smallest breeds in the table. The Chihuahua lives an average of 7.91 years. The Jack Russell Terrier, a dog of maybe seven kilos, lives 12.72. Both are small. They are 4.8 years apart — more than half the entire spread of the table.

Now take the large ones. The Labrador Retriever, at around thirty kilos, averages 11.77 years. The German Shepherd, a heavier dog, averages 10.16. The Boxer averages 10.04. The Labrador outlives the Chihuahua by nearly four years while weighing ten times as much.

So the size effect is real across 74 breeds and invisible as an ordering principle across 18. Both statements are true, and holding them together is the whole skill. Size is a population-level tendency that emerges when you average over a hundred breeds. At the level of an individual breed, the specific diseases that breed carries dominate the arithmetic completely. A Chihuahua's lifespan is not set by its mass; it is set by what Chihuahuas die of.

Which raises the obvious question: if the short-lived breeds in this table are not the big ones, what do they have in common?

Flat Faces, Not Big Bodies

Look at the bottom of the table again. French Bulldog 4.53. English Bulldog 7.39. Pug 7.65. American Bulldog 7.79. The four shortest-lived breeds in a list of nineteen are, with one exception, the flat-faced ones — and the study says so directly, noting that many brachycephalic breeds (dogs with a short, flat face) had relatively short life expectancy at birth, with the French Bulldog shortest at 4.53 years, 2.86 years below the English Bulldog at 7.39.

That 2.86-year gap between the two bulldogs is worth sitting with. These are closely related breeds, similar in build and in the shape of the skull, and one of them averages under five years in this dataset. Whatever is driving the French Bulldog number is not "being a bulldog" in general and it is certainly not "being large".

The clinical explanation is not mysterious, and it is the same list that makes these breeds expensive to insure: airway obstruction that worsens with heat and exertion, difficulty giving birth naturally, spinal malformation, skin folds that trap moisture, and eyes that sit proud of the skull. None of that is a lifespan mechanism in itself. It is a set of recurring ways for a young or middle-aged dog to die.

If you are considering one of these breeds, the French Bulldog guide works through the airway and spinal numbers, and the breed cancer-risk ranking covers the other major cause of death in the middle years.

One caution about reading this as a verdict on flat-faced dogs. The confidence interval for the French Bulldog runs from 4.14 to 5.01, and it rests on 232 dogs. That is a real signal — the interval does not come close to the next breed up — but it is a signal about a population of dogs bred and kept in the UK in the 2010s, many of them under primary care for exactly the conditions that shorten life. A well-bred Frenchie in a cool climate with a good airway is not the average of that row.

Sex: A Small, Stubborn Difference

Female dogs in this dataset averaged 11.41 years at birth (95 per cent confidence interval 11.35 to 11.47) against 11.07 for males (11.01 to 11.13). The gap is 0.34 years — about four months — and because the sample is large, the intervals do not overlap. It is a real difference in the population.

It is also small enough to be irrelevant to any individual decision. Four months is not a reason to choose one puppy over another, and it is dwarfed by the breed spread and by body condition. It matters for a different reason: it tells you the dataset is behaving the way a good dataset should, picking up small consistent effects rather than only the loud ones.

What this page deliberately does not do is convert that into advice about neutering. The same study builds separate tables by neuter status, and the spay and neuter timing guide works through that literature properly — including the fact that the age at which surgery happens changes the answer for joints in some breeds and not others. Borrowing a lifespan number from a table of associations to justify a surgical decision would be exactly the kind of overreach this site exists to avoid.

If Your Dog Is Already Eight, the Number Is Different

This is the calculation almost nobody publishes, and the one most owners actually want. Life expectancy at birth answers "how long does a dog live". Life expectancy at age answers "how long does a dog who has already got this far live" — and because it is conditioned on survival, it is always longer than the naive subtraction.

An eight-year-old dog in this dataset has an expected 4.61 further years. Subtract eight from 11.23 and you get 3.23. The two numbers differ because the dogs that would have died at two, at four, at six are no longer in the denominator. Your dog has already cleared those hurdles. That is not a statistical trick; it is the same reason a 70-year-old human has a longer remaining life expectancy than a newborn's life expectancy minus seventy.

Read the last row and you will see why vets are cautious about the word "old". A fourteen-year-old dog has, on average, another year and a half. That is not a rounding error at the end of life; it is a substantial period of time in which a lot of good decisions can still be made — about pain management, about weight, about what counts as a good week.

And read the top row against the breed table: these are population figures. A fourteen-year-old Jack Russell has better odds than a twelve-year-old French Bulldog, because the table above averages over both. Use the population number to plan. Use the breed row to adjust your expectations.

Two features stand out. The first is how flat the first four years are: the chance of dying between birth and one, and between two and three, is essentially identical at around 1.7 per cent. Whatever kills dogs in early life — congenital defects, accidents, infections — is not concentrated in the first year the way it is in humans, and the curve does not start climbing until middle age.

The second is the bend. Between six and seven the annual risk is 3.3 per cent; between seven and eight it is 4.7; between eight and nine it is 6.9. From that point it roughly doubles every three years. This is the age at which an annual check stops being a formality, and the reason "senior" screening protocols tend to start at seven rather than at ten. If you want a single practical takeaway from the entire life table, it is that the seven-to-nine window is where the curve changes slope, and where a baseline blood panel is worth more than it will be at any later point.

The One Lever With a Published Number

Almost every claim about extending a dog's life is a claim about a supplement, a diet type, or a routine, supported by a mechanism and no outcome data. There is one exception, and it is unusually well documented — because it was run as a controlled feeding study rather than observed after the fact.

Forty-eight Labrador Retrievers, paired at six weeks by sex and weight within each of seven litters, were assigned at random within each pair to either control feeding or 25 per cent diet restriction. Feeding began at eight weeks. Both groups ate the same diet; only the quantity differed. The dogs were followed for life.

The result, reported across two papers two decades apart: the food-restricted dogs lived a median 1.8 years longer, with delayed onset of late-life disease, particularly osteoarthritis. Long-term restriction did not harm skeletal maturation, structure or metabolism — a concern the design was built to test. And the mechanism was not mysterious: body fat above 25 per cent was associated with increasing insulin resistance, and that insulin resistance independently predicted both lifespan and chronic disease.

There is a second finding in the same study that gets quoted less often and is arguably more useful. Even within the control group, high static fat mass and declining lean mass predicted death, most strongly in the year before it happened. In other words, the number on the scale was not just a risk factor for a shorter life; it was a leading indicator of the last year of it.

Three honest caveats. It was one breed, one diet, and a laboratory-managed population of 48 dogs — small by epidemiological standards, even if the follow-up is exceptional. "25 per cent restriction" meant keeping the dogs lean, not starving them; the restricted dogs still grew normally and lived to a median age in the teens. And the finding is about body composition, not about any particular food. There is no evidence in this study that grain-free, raw, or boutique diets do anything similar, and it would be a misreading to conclude that they do.

What follows practically is unglamorous and cheap. Weigh the dog monthly rather than eyeballing it — a kitchen scale for the bowl is more accurate than a scoop, and a gram scale removes the guesswork from portions. Score body condition rather than reading a number, because the weight calculator needs a condition score to mean anything. If your dog is carrying extra weight, the target is not a dramatic diet; it is a slow, measured return to rib coverage you can feel with light pressure.

What This Data Cannot Tell You

A page that gave you eighteen numbers and no limitations would be selling certainty it does not have. Here is the list, in order of how much it should change your reading.

It cannot tell you when your dog will die. Nothing can. The life table describes the rate at which a cohort empties; it does not assign a date to an individual. A dog in the bottom breed row can reach sixteen, and a dog in the top row can die at four. Twenty-three dogs in this cohort reached twenty or more years of age, which is roughly one in 1,300 — rare, real, and not something you can plan around.

It cannot separate correlation from cause. The breed differences are descriptive. That a breed has a short life table does not identify what to do about it, and it does not mean the breed is "unhealthy" as a category — it means the dogs in that row died at those ages, for reasons the table does not record.

It cannot generalise outside its population. UK primary care, deaths between 2016 and 2020. Dogs in the United States, in southern Europe, in shelters, or in breeding kennels are different populations with different risks, and their life tables would differ.

It cannot rank your breed if your breed is not in it. Eighteen breeds plus a crossbred pool. Everything else is unmeasured here, and absence from the table is not a compliment.

And it cannot answer the question people actually care about, which is not "how long do dogs live" but "how long will this dog live, and what should I be watching for". That question needs the breed-specific disease profile, a body condition score, and a vet who knows the animal — which is why the useful part of this page is the shape of the risk curve, not the headline average.

How to Use This Without Frightening Yourself

The most common failure mode after reading a lifespan article is to treat the average as a deadline. It is not, and the arithmetic of the table is the antidote: an eight-year-old dog's expected remaining life went up relative to the naive subtraction, because surviving is itself information.

What the numbers are genuinely good for is timing and money. Three uses, in order of how much they tend to matter.

Insurance and savings decisions. Premiums are priced on exactly this kind of table, which is why they climb steeply in the seven-to-ten window and why "is insurance worth it" has a different answer at two than at nine. The cost-benefit analysis runs the arithmetic; the short version is that the years in which cover is most likely to pay are also the years in which it costs most. If you do decide to compare, start from actual pet insurance quotes rather than from an annual average.

Senior screening. The bend in the mortality curve at seven to nine is the argument for baseline bloodwork, blood pressure, and a proper dental examination before there is a problem. It is cheaper to establish what normal looks like for your dog at seven than to interpret an abnormal result at eleven with nothing to compare it against.

Budgeting the last two years. Most lifetime cost estimates assume an even spread, and the spending is not even. The monthly cost calculator gives you the running rate, and the hidden costs guide covers the irregular ones; together they are a better basis for a sinking fund than a single annual figure.

And the last use is the least quantitative. Knowing that a Labrador averages 11.77 years and a French Bulldog 4.53 does not tell you anything about your own dog's future. It does tell you that the eight years between those rows is mostly not something an owner controls — which is a reason to spend your effort on the part that is, and to be sceptical of anyone selling the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average lifespan of a dog?

In the largest published life table, covering 30,563 dogs under UK primary veterinary care that died between 2016 and 2020, life expectancy at birth was 11.23 years. Female dogs averaged 11.41 and males 11.07. The figure is a population average across all breeds, so it is lower than the "twelve to fifteen years" people usually quote, because it includes the dogs that died young.

Do small dogs live longer than big dogs?

On average across many breeds, yes — and dogs are an exception among mammals, where larger species usually live longer. An analysis of death records from 74 breeds found that the driving factor was the rate of ageing: large dogs' mortality risk climbs faster with each year, rather than their ageing starting earlier. But size does not order an 18-breed table. The Chihuahua averages 7.91 years and the Jack Russell Terrier 12.72, and the Labrador Retriever at 11.77 outlives both the Chihuahua and the German Shepherd.

Which dog breed lives the longest?

In this dataset the Jack Russell Terrier, at 12.72 years, followed by the Yorkshire Terrier at 12.54 and the Border Collie at 12.10. Read that as "longest-lived of the eighteen breeds with enough records to build a table", not as a ranking of all breeds — a breed that is absent from the table is unmeasured rather than unlucky.

Which breed has the shortest lifespan?

The French Bulldog, at 4.53 years — 2.86 years below the English Bulldog at 7.39, on 232 dogs. The four shortest-lived breeds in the table are the French Bulldog, English Bulldog, Pug and American Bulldog, which is a pattern about flat faces and body shape rather than about size.

How long will my dog live if it is already eight?

An eight-year-old dog in this population has an expected 4.61 further years, not the 3.23 you get by subtracting eight from 11.23. Life expectancy at an age is conditioned on having survived to that age, so it is always longer than the naive subtraction. At ten the figure is 3.28 years, at twelve 2.23, and at fourteen 1.46.

Do female dogs live longer than male dogs?

In this dataset, yes: 11.41 years against 11.07, a difference of about four months, with confidence intervals that do not overlap. It is a real population difference and far too small to matter in choosing a dog — the breed spread is more than twenty times larger.

Can I extend my dog's life?

One intervention has a published effect size. In 48 Labrador Retrievers fed the same diet, with one dog of each pair given 25 per cent less food from eight weeks of age, the restricted dogs lived a median 1.8 years longer, with later onset of late-life disease, especially osteoarthritis. Body fat above 25 per cent was associated with insulin resistance that independently predicted lifespan. Keeping a dog lean is the best-evidenced lever available. There is no comparable outcome evidence for supplements, grain-free diets, or raw feeding.
