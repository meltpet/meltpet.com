How Often Should You Change Cat Litter?

This is three questions wearing one coat: how often you scoop, how often you replace the whole bed, and how often the box itself has to go. The published guidance answers them at three different scales — and disagrees with itself on two of them. | 2026-10-23 · MeltPet · 14 min read

Last updated: 2026-10-23

The Short Version, Before the Evidence

Scoop at least once a day, and twice a day if you can manage it. Replace the entire bed of litter every one to four weeks — weekly for non-clumping litter, every two to four weeks for clumping litter in a single-cat home, and at the shorter end of both ranges as cats are added. Wash and dry the box completely at every full change, never refill a damp box, and use nothing scented. There is one override that beats every schedule below: if you can smell the box from the doorway of the room, the interval is already wrong regardless of what the calendar says.

That paragraph is the answer most people came for, and it is defensible against every source cited at the bottom of this page. But it hides something important, which is that "how often should I change the litter" is not one question. It is three, measured in hours, weeks and years, and the confusion in most published advice comes from answering them all with a single number.

So this page separates them first, then shows you where the guidance disagrees and how to pick a side. If your cat has a urinary history, or you are pregnant, or you have three cats and one box, jump to the section that matches — those cases override the general schedule rather than sit inside it.

TaskHow oftenWhat it actually removes
ScoopAt least once a day; twice is the targetThis morning's deposit. Stops the box from becoming a place the cat avoids.
Top upWhenever the bed drops below depthNothing. It maintains the depth so digging still works.
Full change + washEvery 1–4 weeks, by litter type and cat countThe ammonia and bacteria bound into the litter itself, plus the film on the plastic.

Everything below is detail on those four rows. The reason they are worth separating is that people who fail at litter box maintenance almost never fail at all four. They scoop faithfully and never change the litter, or they change the litter weekly and let the box itself go for six years, or they do both perfectly and refill while the plastic is still wet.

Three Clocks, Not One Number

Think of litter maintenance as three clocks running at wildly different speeds. The scoop clock runs in hours. The change clock runs in weeks. The box clock runs in years. A single "how often" answer cannot serve all three, which is why the question keeps getting asked.

The scoop clock: hours

A cat urinates roughly two to four times a day and defecates once, give or take, depending on diet and age. That means the box is receiving fresh waste several times in every 24-hour window, and the useful unit is not "the week" but "before the next deposit lands on top of the last one". Scooping once a day clears a day's accumulation. Scooping twice a day means the box is never more than about half a day from clean, which is the state a fastidious cat will accept without negotiating.

Here is the arithmetic on what that costs you, because it is smaller than people assume. Twice a day is 730 scoops a year. At roughly 45 seconds each — walk over, scoop, bag it, walk back — that is about nine hours of your life per year per box. It is not nothing. It is also less time than most people spend looking for the cat's carrier before a vet appointment.

The change clock: weeks

This is the clock everyone argues about. Clumping litter binds urine into removable masses, so the bed stays workable for weeks as long as you keep taking the masses out. Non-clumping litter absorbs urine throughout and cannot be separated from it, so the bed is degrading from the first use and the whole thing has to go far more often. That is the entire mechanical reason the two formats carry different intervals, and it is not a matter of brand quality.

The box clock: years

Plastic scratches. Every scoop drags grit across the base, and those micro-scratches are where bacteria settle and where odour survives a wash. This is why a box can smell faintly wrong the day after a thorough clean while the litter is brand new. There is no published interval that is reliable here — a box used by one gentle cat may last years, and a box scraped daily by a vigorous digger may not. The usable test is behavioural: if the box still carries odour immediately after a full wash and dry, the box is the problem and no schedule will fix it.

The geometry worth knowing

One number makes the whole routine easier to think about. A 24 × 18 inch tray filled to 2 inches holds 864 cubic inches of litter — exactly half a cubic foot. That is one full change. Fill the same tray to 3 inches and it is three quarters of a cubic foot, which is why the "fill it deeper" instinct quietly increases how much litter you buy without improving the box. Nothing about depth is free.

Where the Guidance Disagrees

Most articles on this subject quote one authority and present it as settled. It is not settled, and the disagreement is instructive rather than confusing — once you know who each number was written for. Here are the five sources this page relies on, with what each one actually specifies.

The two live disagreements are the full-change interval (weekly versus two to four weeks) and the litter depth (1 to 2 inches versus 3 inches). Neither is a mistake. Both resolve once you ask what population the number was written for.

Why one source says weekly and another says four weeks

The AVMA brochure is not general litter box advice. It is a client handout about feline lower urinary tract disease, developed with the Cornell Feline Health Center, and its recommendations sit in a section titled "what can I do at home to prevent future occurrences". The audience is households where a cat has already had a urinary episode — a population in which a clean box is part of reducing recurrence, and in which the cost of changing litter too often is trivially small next to another episode.

The two-to-four-week figure comes from a clinical review aimed at the general population of healthy cats on clumping litter. Different population, different interval, and both can be right at once. The practical consequence is a decision rule rather than a number:

  • Use the weekly end if the cat has any urinary history, if there are two or more cats sharing, if the tray is on the small side, or if the litter is non-clumping.
  • Use the two-to-four-week end if it is one healthy adult cat, in a properly sized tray, on clumping litter, with daily scooping.
  • Ignore both and change it sooner if the box smells at arm's length. The calendar is a fallback, not the authority.

Why one source says 1 to 2 inches and another says 3

This one is more interesting, because 3 inches is double the top of Cornell's range, and both are describing what cats prefer. The likely explanation is that they are answering slightly different questions. Cornell's figure describes the bed cats choose when given options — deep enough to dig and cover, shallow enough that the surface is not a mound. The three-inch figure comes from a clinical protocol for a box that has to survive two to four weeks between full changes, where extra depth is doing mechanical work: keeping urine away from the plastic base so clumps do not weld themselves to the bottom.

That gives a workable reconciliation. Treat 1.25 inches as the floor, because it comes from the guideline with the most explicit substrate specification, and treat 1.5 to 2.5 inches as the normal working range for a standard tray. Go deeper — up to about 3 inches — only when the box is large and the interval is long, and accept that you are buying more litter to solve a mechanical problem rather than a preference one. Do not go below the floor to save litter. Urine that reaches the plastic is how a box ends up smelling at the base even when the surface looks clean.

Why Timing Is Behaviour, Not Just Hygiene

There is a temptation to file this under housekeeping, as though the only stakes were smell. The behavioural stakes are larger, and the clinical literature is unusually direct about it.

Cornell's house-soiling guidance opens by stating that house soiling is the most common behaviour problem reported by cat owners — the single most frequently raised complaint, ahead of aggression and scratching. Its first instruction, when a cat is showing signs of box trouble, is not to buy anything or move anything. It is to first be sure the box is clean, with the observation that some cats refuse to use a box containing any urine or faeces whatsoever. That is the sharpest version of the point: there are cats for whom one un-scooped deposit is disqualifying, not merely unpleasant.

A clinical review in Today's Veterinary Practice goes further and puts cleanliness above every design decision. Its summary is that the biggest influence on litter box preference appears to be cleanliness — above substrate, above covered versus uncovered, above location. That is a strong claim and worth reading carefully: it does not say a dirty box is the most common cause of house soiling, only that cleanliness is the variable that moves preference most. But it is also the cheapest variable in the entire system to change, and the only one you can adjust tonight without buying anything.

The box-size study is a useful illustration of how these factors interact. When researchers gave 74 cats in 43 households two trays of different sizes and logged every deposit for 28 days, the larger tray received 5,031 deposits against 3,239 in the regular tray — a clear preference. But the authors were careful to note that other factors, including box cleanliness and location, may have a compounding influence on the choice. In other words, even the cleanest study of box size could not fully separate size from how clean each box stayed.

What this means practically is that the schedule is not a hygiene preference you are entitled to have opinions about. It is a variable your cat is scoring you on continuously, and the feedback arrives as behaviour: hesitation at the box, perching on the rim, eliminating beside it rather than in it. If any of that is happening, the interval is the first thing to change — before the litter brand, before the box style, before the pheromone diffuser.

How Many Boxes Changes the Answer

The single most repeated rule in feline husbandry is that a household needs one litter box per cat, plus one extra. It appears, in almost identical wording, in the AVMA's client guidance, in CDC's healthy-pets material, in Cornell's advice, and in the AAFP/ISFM house-soiling guidelines. When four organisations with different mandates converge on the same sentence, it is not a marketing line.

The number matters here because it changes what your maintenance interval is actually doing. Waste output is fixed by the cats; box count only decides how much surface area it is spread across. Add boxes and each individual box stays usable longer, which lets you keep the longer end of the change interval. Remove boxes and the same waste concentrates into a smaller volume, which shortens it. A three-cat household with three boxes is not the same maintenance problem as a three-cat household with five, even though the cats are identical.

There is a second rule attached to the first that is easier to get wrong: the boxes have to be in different places. Three trays lined up in the same laundry room function, from the cat's point of view, as one large box — and if a cat is guarding the doorway, all three become inaccessible at once. Cornell's phrasing is multiple boxes in multiple locations; the clinical review puts it more bluntly, that all boxes in the same location are similar to one big box, which is not ideal. The point of the extra box is redundancy of route, not redundancy of tray.

One honest trade-off: more boxes means more litter to change, not less. Each box still has to be emptied on its own schedule. What you buy with the extra trays is a cat that always has a clean option, not a lighter chore. If you were hoping the fifth box would reduce the work, the fifth box is not for you — it is for the cat who is currently being ambushed at the only one.

Litter Depth: The Spec Nobody Agrees On

Depth is the part of litter maintenance people treat as personal taste, and it is where most boxes are quietly wrong. Too little litter and the cat cannot bury, urine reaches the plastic, and the base of the box starts to hold odour no matter how often you scoop. Too much and you are paying for volume that does nothing, the bed becomes a mound the cat has to climb, and clumps break apart under their own weight instead of lifting cleanly.

The published numbers, gathered in one place: the AAFP/ISFM house-soiling guidelines specify fine, unscented clumping litter at a depth of at least 1.25 inches (3 cm). Cornell describes what cats prefer as about 1 to 2 inches. The clinical review in Today's Veterinary Practice specifies filling to a depth of at least 3 inches when refilling after a full wash. Those are the three anchors, and the spread between them is roughly a factor of two.

DepthWhat it is good forWhat goes wrong
Under 1.25 in (3 cm)Nothing. This is below every published floor.Urine hits plastic, clumps weld to the base, odour survives scooping
1.5–2.5 inStandard trays; the range most cats choose when offered optionsNeeds topping up every few days as clumps are removed
About 3 inLarge trays and long change intervals, where depth protects the baseUses noticeably more litter; a small tray becomes a mound

The practical instruction that follows: match depth to the tray rather than to a fixed number. A large under-bed storage tub at 2.5 inches holds a proper bed without becoming a hill. A standard commercial tray at 2.5 inches is a mound the cat has to wade into. And a box that gets a full change every two weeks needs more depth than one that gets changed weekly, because the litter is carrying the interval.

Box size belongs in the same paragraph, because depth and footprint fail together. The AAFP guidelines recommend a rectangular box 1.5 times the length of the cat from nose to base of tail, and in the study they cite, adult cats preferred a 34 × 15 inch (86 × 39 cm) tray over a smaller one. The clinical review gives a minimum of 22 × 17 inches. If your cat hangs over the edge, that is not a scooping problem and no schedule will fix it — the tray is simply too small, which is why storage tubs and concrete mixing trays keep appearing in veterinary advice as legitimate litter boxes.

A Full Change, Done Properly

Frequency is only half the job. How the change is done decides whether the box is actually reset or just re-covered, and one step in this sequence is skipped more than any other.

  1. Empty it completely, into a sealed bag. Tip it slowly — dumping a full tray fast throws a dust cloud that is unpleasant for you and worse for a cat with any respiratory sensitivity. Do this step outside or near a bin, not over the carpet you will then have to clean.
  2. Rinse with hot water before scrubbing. Hot water loosens the crust that forms where urine met plastic. Scrubbing a dry box just moves that crust around.
  3. Wash with a mild, unscented detergent and a dedicated brush. The brush matters: a brush that also cleans the kitchen sink is how you transfer whatever was in the sink. Keep one for the box and nothing else.
  4. Rinse until the water runs clear and stops smelling of soap. Residue is the most common reason a freshly cleaned box gets refused. A cat's nose is doing the quality control here, and "a faint lemon scent" reads to a cat as a warning, not as clean.
  5. Dry it completely — air dry or paper towel, not a quick wipe. This is the step that gets skipped. Fresh litter poured into a damp box clumps against the base immediately, sticks to the plastic, and starts the next cycle already compromised. Dry takes ten minutes in air and about one minute with a towel. The towel is the better trade.
  6. Refill to depth, and resist the urge to overfill. Depth by tray size, per the section above. Then leave it alone for a day before judging it.

What not to use, and why it matters more than it sounds: bleach, ammonia-based cleaners, and anything citrus or heavily perfumed. Ammonia is the compound you are trying to remove, so cleaning with it is a strange kind of counterproductive; bleach residue on a surface a cat walks on and then grooms off is a straightforward hazard; and citrus and perfume are reliably aversive to cats, which means a box that smells "clean" to you can read as contaminated to the animal that has to stand in it.

There is also a failure mode that has nothing to do with the wash: topping up instead of changing. Topping up maintains depth, so the box looks and scoops normally while the bed underneath accumulates the fines, dust and ammonia that clumping cannot bind. This is why some people can honestly say they scoop twice a day and still have a box that smells. The scoop clock was being honoured; the change clock was not running at all.

Toxoplasmosis: The One Human-Health Reason Frequency Matters

For most of this page, changing the litter more often is about the cat and the smell. There is exactly one case where it is a human health instruction, and it is the reason the CDC has a specific position on how often a litter box should be cleaned rather than leaving it to preference.

The mechanism is the whole argument. When an infected cat sheds Toxoplasma gondii oocysts in faeces, those oocysts are not immediately infectious. The CDC states that the parasite becomes infective one to five days after it is passed. That window is the entire basis of the recommendation: remove the waste daily and the oocysts are gone before they become capable of infecting anyone. Let it sit for a week and you have given them all the time they need. The same guidance notes that after infection a cat can shed for up to about two weeks.

The CDC's practical position is unusually calm about this, and worth quoting in substance rather than paraphrasing into alarm: you do not need to give up your cat if you are pregnant or planning to become pregnant. What you do is hand the scoop to someone else where possible; if that is not possible, wear disposable gloves, wash your hands thoroughly afterwards, and change the box daily rather than weekly. Immunocompromised people are given the same advice, for the same reason.

Two pieces of context that keep this proportional. First, the CDC lists undercooked meat as a major route of human infection, alongside contaminated soil, water and unwashed produce — the litter box is one exposure route among several, not the dominant one, which is why gardening gloves and a meat thermometer appear in the same list of precautions. Second, an indoor cat fed commercial food is a low-risk cat; the risk profile rises with hunting and raw meat feeding. If you are pregnant and want one thing to change, it is the daily scoop plus gloves, not the cat.

Matching the Schedule to the Litter

The change interval is a property of the substrate before it is a property of your habits. Clumping litter can be separated from its waste, so the bed lasts. Everything else absorbs, and absorption has a ceiling. Here is the working table, built from the guidance above and adjusted for format rather than brand.

Two rules sit on top of the table. The first is that a format change is not the moment to also shorten the interval — if you are switching litter, do it gradually, mixing new into old, and keep everything else steady so you can tell what the cat is reacting to. Cornell makes the same point about brand switches: do it gradually, adding small amounts of the new litter to the old.

If you are choosing a substrate from scratch, or your cat is currently refusing a box, the lowest-risk start is an unscented fine clumping clay, because it is the format cats are most likely to accept and the one that tolerates the longest interval. Dr. Elsey's Ultra is the unscented clumping clay we use as the default in this situation; whatever you pick, check that it is unscented before anything else, because scent is the one variable that reliably makes a box aversive.

The second is that self-cleaning boxes do not remove the change clock. An automatic rake handles the scoop clock, which is genuinely useful for a household that is out of the house all day, but the litter bed still saturates and still has to be replaced on roughly the same schedule as a manual box of the same format. Treat automation as a way of buying scooping reliability, not as a way of buying the full change.

Kittens, Seniors, and Cats With a Diagnosis

The general schedule assumes a healthy adult cat in the middle of its life. Four groups sit outside that assumption, and for each of them the interval shortens for a concrete reason rather than out of caution.

Kittens

Kittens eliminate more often relative to their size and have less warning between the urge and the event, which means a box that would be acceptable to an adult is a box they will miss. They also need low sides — Cornell's point about young kittens needing boxes with low sides is practical rather than aesthetic; a kitten who has to climb into the tray will eventually choose the floor. Scoop more often than you would for an adult, and expect to change the litter more frequently simply because the volume of waste per unit of litter is higher in a small tray.

Senior cats

Arthritis is common in older cats and rarely shows as an obvious limp, which makes the litter box one of the first places it becomes visible. A cat with sore hips needs a low entry point and a route that does not involve stairs. The box may also need to be duplicated — one per floor — because a senior cat who cannot face the stairs will find a corner instead, and that is usually read as a behaviour problem when it is a mobility one. If your older cat's box habits have changed, the box setup and a veterinary visit are both part of the answer.

Cats with kidney disease, diabetes or hyperthyroidism

All three increase urine volume, and increased volume overwhelms the litter's capacity faster than behaviour does. If your cat has any of these diagnoses, the change clock effectively resets — you are no longer maintaining a bed for normal output. This is also why the box is a diagnostic instrument: a cat whose litter consumption suddenly doubles is telling you something has changed physiologically. Our guide to kidney disease in cats covers the water side of that picture, and the water intake calculator puts a number on how much your cat should be drinking.

Cats with a urinary history

This is the group the AVMA brochure was written for, and their instruction is the strictest in this entire article: waste removed twice a day, litter changed weekly or more often as needed. If your cat has had feline lower urinary tract disease, treat the AVMA numbers as your floor rather than your target. A cat who associates the box with pain is also a cat who will start avoiding it, and avoidance is much harder to reverse than it is to prevent.

Adding a cat does not add a fixed amount of maintenance; it multiplies the pressure on shared resources. Two caveats. First, these are per-box intervals, not per-household — five boxes all take their own two-week clock, which is why multi-cat litter maintenance is genuinely more expensive rather than just more annoying. Second, if the boxes are clustered in one room, the effective number of boxes is closer to one and the interval above is too generous. Spread them, and the table holds.

There is one more multi-cat effect that is easy to miss: resource competition changes behaviour before it changes hygiene. A cat who is being blocked from a box will often not fight about it — she will simply start eliminating somewhere uncontested. If you have added a cat recently and the litter box routine has started to fail, the box count and placement are the first thing to audit, and our guide to introducing a new cat covers the territory side of that in more depth.

The Signs You Are Already Too Late

Cats rarely complain about a box in a way that is easy to read. These are the observable signals, roughly in order of how late you are.

  • You can smell it from the doorway. The most reliable single indicator, and the one that requires no interpretation. If it registers before you enter the room, the interval has already failed — regardless of how recently you scooped.
  • The cat perches on the rim. Balancing on the edge rather than standing in the litter is a cat minimising contact with the contents. It is also, in a small tray, a sign the footprint is too tight.
  • She shakes her paws on the way out. Some shaking is normal after burying; shaking repeatedly as she leaves suggests she does not want the litter on her.
  • Deposits appear right beside the box. A cat who uses the floor next to the tray, rather than a distant corner, is often still trying to use the box — she just cannot find a usable spot in it.
  • She scrapes the empty walls or the floor before going. Digging at nothing usually means there is not enough depth to dig in.
  • Clumps are welded to the bottom of the tray. That means urine is reaching plastic, which means depth is short or the interval is long, or both.
  • The litter clouds when you pour it. Dust is what remains when the fine fraction has broken down. Fresh clumping litter should pour, not bloom.
  • She waits by the box and looks at you. Uncommon but unambiguous in the cats who do it — some cats do ask for the box to be serviced rather than use it as it is.

If you recognise two or more of these, change the litter and wash the box today rather than waiting for the next scheduled change. Then watch whether the behaviour resets. A cat who goes back to normal use once the box is genuinely clean has told you exactly what the problem was.

The Over-Cleaning Myth, and What Is Real

There is a piece of folklore on this subject that needs retiring: that cleaning the box too thoroughly removes the cat's own scent marks and makes her reluctant to use it or inclined to mark elsewhere. It gets repeated in forums and occasionally in product copy, and it is not supported by the clinical guidance. Cornell's advice, the AVMA's and the behavioural reviews all point in the same direction — toward more frequent cleaning, not less — and none of them qualifies the recommendation to protect a residual scent layer.

The kernel of truth underneath the folklore is about how you clean, not how often. There are real ways to overdo a litter box, and they all involve what you put on it:

  • Harsh chemistry. Bleach, ammonia-based cleaners and strong disinfectants leave residue on a surface the cat's paws and coat contact constantly. This is a genuine aversion risk and a genuine ingestion risk.
  • Anything scented. Citrus, pine and perfume all read as contamination to a cat. A box that smells like a cleaning product is not a clean box from the perspective of the animal that has to stand in it.
  • Refilling damp. Even with a perfectly rinsed box, moisture in the base causes immediate clumping against the plastic and shortens the next interval.
  • Changing everything at once. A brand-new box, a new litter and a new location in the same week is three changes the cat cannot attribute. Change one thing and give it a fortnight.

So the honest position is this: clean thoroughly, rinse completely, dry fully, and use nothing that smells of anything. Those four rules capture everything the folklore was reaching for, without giving you permission to scoop less.

The Ten-Minute Weekly Reset

Most people who let the box slide do not lack motivation; they lack a routine that survives a bad week. The fix is to separate the daily task from the weekly one so completely that the daily task can be done badly on a bad day without the whole system degrading.

The daily version, about a minute. Scoop into a bag, tie it, bin it. Top up the bed to depth. If you do nothing else, do this — it is the one task that keeps the box in the category a cat will accept. A slotted metal scoop is worth the upgrade over the plastic one that ships with most trays, because it cuts through a clump instead of smearing it and it does not hold odour the way scratched plastic does; a plain stainless scoop is all you need, and the wider head is easier on a big tray.

The weekly version, about ten minutes. This is the reset that buys you the rest of the week:

  1. Empty the tray completely into a sealed bag, slowly.
  2. Rinse with hot water.
  3. Wash with mild unscented detergent and the dedicated box brush.
  4. Rinse until the water runs clear and carries no soap smell.
  5. Dry fully — towel is faster than air, and air is fine if you start the job earlier.
  6. Check the base by hand for scratches and for any odour that survived the wash.
  7. Refill to depth, matched to the tray size.
  8. Wipe the floor under and around the tray, and empty the tracking mat.
  9. Sweep or vacuum the immediate area — tracked litter is what makes a clean house smell like a litter box.

If a weekly full change sounds excessive for a single cat on clumping litter, remember that the published range runs from weekly to four-weekly, and that weekly sits at the end the AVMA specifies for cats with a urinary history. Doing it weekly is not over-maintenance; it is simply choosing the conservative end of a legitimate range. Doing it every four weeks is also legitimate — for one healthy cat, in a properly sized tray, with faithful daily scooping.

When Cleaning Stops Being the Answer

Everything on this page assumes the problem is maintenance. Sometimes it is not, and the symptoms of the two overlap enough that it is worth knowing where the boundary sits.

Cleaning is the right first move because it is free, immediate, and eliminates the most common variable. But cleaning is not a diagnosis, and a cat who continues to avoid the box after a genuinely clean reset needs a veterinary assessment rather than a stricter routine. Cornell's guidance is explicit that any medical condition interfering with normal urination or defecation can produce box problems — urinary tract inflammation makes urinating painful and more frequent, and cats readily associate the box with that pain. Kidney disease, hyperthyroidism and diabetes all increase volume and urgency; digestive problems make defecation painful.

Some signs are not maintenance questions at all and should not wait for a litter change to be evaluated. Straining to urinate, producing small amounts frequently, crying while urinating, blood in the urine, or repeated unproductive attempts in a male cat are all urinary signs — and in a male cat, an inability to pass urine is an emergency rather than a same-week appointment, because a urethral obstruction can become fatal quickly. Our guide to cat peeing outside the litter box walks through the diagnostic order for that whole picture, starting with the emergency check.

The useful mental model is that litter maintenance is the control variable, not the explanation. You control the box completely, so you should always set it right first — and then, if the behaviour persists in a box that is objectively clean, sized correctly, placed well and in sufficient number, you have eliminated the environmental explanation and earned a vet visit. That is what the schedule is for. Not to solve the problem, but to make sure the problem you are left with is the real one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should you change cat litter?

For one healthy adult cat on clumping litter, every two to four weeks with daily scooping. Non-clumping litter needs a full change weekly or sooner because urine is absorbed throughout and cannot be separated out. The AVMA's client guidance for cats with a urinary history specifies litter changed once a week, or more often as needed, which is the conservative end of the same range. As cats are added, move toward the shorter interval: two cats on clumping litter do better at one to two weeks, and three or more at roughly weekly.

Is it OK to just top up the litter instead of changing it?

No, and this is the most common way a box fails while appearing to be maintained. Topping up restores depth, so the box looks and scoops normally, but the bed underneath accumulates the fines, dust and ammonia that clumping cannot bind to a removable mass. The result is a box that gets scooped twice a day and still smells. Topping up is a maintenance action between full changes, not a replacement for them.

How often should you scoop a litter box?

At least once a day, and twice a day is the target. The AVMA's client guidance specifies waste ideally removed twice a day. Twice daily matters most in three situations: a cat with a urinary history, a box shared by more than one cat, and any cat who shows signs of hesitation at the box. Cornell's guidance notes that some cats refuse to use a box containing any urine or faeces at all, which is the clearest possible argument for not leaving a deposit in place for a full day.

Can I use bleach to clean the litter box?

It is not recommended. Bleach leaves residue on a surface the cat's paws and coat contact constantly, which is both an ingestion risk and a reliable way to make the box aversive, and the smell reads as contamination rather than cleanliness to an animal with a far more sensitive nose than yours. The clinical guidance points to mild soap and warm water instead. The same applies to ammonia-based cleaners, citrus products and anything perfumed.

Does having two cats double how often I change the litter?

Effectively yes, and possibly a little more than double. Waste output doubles, and the standard rule of one box per cat plus one extra means you are also maintaining more boxes. A two-cat household with three boxes should be changing clumping litter roughly every one to two weeks rather than every two to four. If those boxes are all in one room, treat the household as having closer to one box, and the interval shortens further.

Is it safe to change the litter box while pregnant?

Yes, with precautions, and the CDC is explicit that you do not need to give up your cat. Toxoplasma oocysts become infectious one to five days after being shed, so daily removal is the key measure — it clears them before that window closes. Have someone else handle the box where possible; if not, wear disposable gloves and wash your hands thoroughly afterwards. Keep the cat indoors and fed commercial food, and treat undercooked meat and unwashed produce as the more significant exposure routes they are.

Why does my cat hang over the edge of the litter box?

Usually because the tray is too small rather than because the litter is dirty, though both produce the same posture. The AAFP guidelines recommend a rectangular box 1.5 times the length of the cat from nose to base of tail, and in the study they cite, adult cats preferred a 34 by 15 inch tray over a smaller one. Most commercial trays are shorter than that. A storage tub or concrete mixing tray with a low entry cutout is a legitimate and inexpensive replacement, and the low cutout matters for kittens, seniors and cats with joint pain.
