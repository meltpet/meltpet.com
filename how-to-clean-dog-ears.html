👂 How to Clean a Dog's Ears

Without getting bit. Without hurting the dog. Without making things worse. | 2026-06-22 · MeltPet · 4 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-14

The First Time I Cleaned a Dog's Ears, I Used Q-Tips. Don't.

I was 19, working at a grooming salon, and a Cocker Spaniel named Duchess came in with ears that smelled like a wet basement. The groomer handed me a bottle of ear cleaner and a handful of cotton balls and said "figure it out." I did not figure it out. I used Q-tips because that's what I used on my own ears. I poked around in the canal trying to get every speck of brown gunk. Duchess shook her head violently mid-Q-tip and I nearly perforated her eardrum.

The groomer took over. Showed me how to do it properly. I've since cleaned hundreds of dog ears without incident. Here's what I learned: ear cleaning is simple. Ear cleaning wrong is dangerous. The margin between the two is about three millimeters and a few basic rules.

Before You Touch the Ear: Infection or Just Wax?

Clean ears smell like nothing, or very faintly like warm skin. Infected ears smell like yeast (think: bread dough, but wrong) or rot. If the smell hits you when you lift the ear flap —not when you're nose-to-canal, but just lifting the flap from a foot away —that's infection until proven otherwise.

Look at the color. Normal wax is light brown and thin. Dark brown, black, yellow, or green discharge are not wax. Red, swollen ear flaps are not normal. If you touch the ear and the dog flinches, yelps, or pulls away hard, that's pain. Pain means inflammation. Inflammation means something is wrong. Do not put anything in this ear. Take the dog to the vet. If the eardrum is ruptured and cleaning solution gets into the middle ear, you're looking at vestibular damage —the dog loses balance, can't walk straight, might lose hearing in that ear. It's not worth guessing.

If the ears are just waxy —light brown, minimal smell, the dog doesn't mind you handling them —proceed.

The Actual Steps

Get the right stuff. A veterinary ear cleaner with a drying agent. Epi-Otic is the standard. Virbac makes a good one. The bottle will say "otic cleanser" or "ear cleaning solution." Do not use hydrogen peroxide. Do not use rubbing alcohol. Do not use witch hazel, tea tree oil, vinegar, or anything from a Pinterest post. Dog ear tissue is delicate and highly vascular. Irritating it sets you up for an actual infection.

Do this somewhere easy to clean. Bathroom. Laundry room. Outside. When you put liquid in a dog's ear, the dog will shake her head. Ear cleaner mixed with wax will spray in a three-foot radius. You want that to hit tile, not your couch.

Lift the ear flap. Hold it straight up. This straightens the ear canal. Fill the canal with solution —don't drip it, fill it. You want enough liquid to reach the base of the canal. The dog will not like this part. That's okay. Hold the ear flap and the base of the ear gently but firmly so she doesn't shake yet.

Massage the base of the ear. Right where the ear meets the skull. Use your thumb and forefinger. Squish the cartilage. You should hear a wet squelching sound. Do this for 20–30 seconds. This is what breaks up the wax and debris —the cleaning solution, plus the mechanical action of the massage. The dog will lean into your hand if you're doing it right. It feels good. Like scratching an itch they can't reach.

Let go and stand back. The dog will shake. Let her. This is how she clears the loosened debris from the canal. It's going to splatter. You already planned for this.

Wipe the outer ear. Cotton ball or gauze wrapped around your finger. Wipe the ridges of the outer ear —all those folds and crevices in the pinna. Wipe the opening of the canal. Do not go deeper than your first knuckle. The ear canal in a dog is L-shaped —it goes down and then turns inward. You cannot reach the eardrum with your finger on most dogs, but you can pack debris deeper and create a plug if you push stuff into the bend. Wipe what you can see, leave what you can't.

Treats. Lots of treats. Every ear cleaning ends with high-value rewards. The goal is for the dog to associate ear handling with good things happening. If the dog fights you, you need to desensitize more slowly —a few days of just lifting the ear flap and giving treats, then touching the ear opening and giving treats, then introducing the bottle without squeezing it. Some dogs need a week of desensitization before the first actual cleaning. Don't force it. A traumatized dog will fight ear cleanings for life.

🛒 What to buy: Epi-Otic Advanced (Virbac) is the ear cleaner most vets stock —it has a drying agent that helps prevent the moisture buildup that leads to yeast infections. One bottle lasts a year of monthly cleanings for one dog. You'll also need a bag of cotton balls —not Q-tips, not cotton rounds, just standard cotton balls that you can wrap around your finger. Both available on Amazon for under $15 total. If your dog has recurring ear infections, ask your vet about a medicated flush —those are prescription, not over-the-counter.

How Often, By Ear Type

Floppy-eared dogs —Goldens, Labs, Cockers, Bassets, Poodles and Doodles —need cleaning every 1–2 weeks. The ear flap traps moisture, blocks airflow, and creates a warm dark environment that yeast loves. These are the dogs who account for 80% of the ear infections in a vet's practice.

Prick-eared dogs —Huskies, German Shepherds, Corgis —get better airflow. Once a month is fine unless they're visibly dirty or they swim a lot.

Swimmers of any ear type: clean the ears the same day they swim. Water in the ear canal is the #1 trigger for otitis externa. Drying the ears after swimming prevents more infections than treating them after they start.

If you're cleaning every 1–2 weeks and the ears still smell bad or look dirty within a few days, the dog likely has an underlying allergy or chronic infection that needs veterinary management. Cleaning more often won't fix the root cause.
