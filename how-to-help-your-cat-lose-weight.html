How to Help an Overweight Cat Lose Weight

Sixty-one percent of American cats are overweight or obese, and most of their owners think they are fine. This is the protocol that actually moves the number — and the one organ that decides how fast you are allowed to go. | 2026-10-25 · MeltPet · 16 min read

Last updated: 2026-10-25

The Short Version: Five Decisions, Not a Diet

Work out a target weight from the cat's body condition, then feed 0.8 times the resting energy requirement for that target weight — not for the weight the cat is now. Aim to lose 0.5 to 1 percent of body weight per week, treat 2 percent as a stop sign, and weigh weekly on the same scale. Weigh the food in grams instead of scooping it, and audit the treat pipeline before you change anything else. Finally, decide what maintenance looks like before the first gram comes off, because the regain is what decides whether the twelve months you just spent meant anything.

That is the whole method. Everything below is the reasoning, the numbers behind it, and the five places where households that follow the method still fail.

The reason this page leads with arithmetic rather than encouragement is that feline weight loss is not a willpower problem. The cat does not buy the food. Almost every failure is one of three things: nobody defined a target weight, so there was nothing to aim at; nobody measured the food, so the deficit was imaginary; or the plan was never recalculated as the cat got smaller, so the deficit quietly closed itself at about week eight.

There is also one constraint that dogs and humans do not share, and it sets the speed limit for everything else: a cat that stops eating — for any reason — starts mobilising fat into its liver faster than the liver can process it. That condition, hepatic lipidosis, is the reason the safe rate for cats is slower than for dogs, and the reason a diet that makes your cat refuse food is more dangerous than the weight it was meant to fix.

One thing to settle before you start. If your cat scores 8 or 9 out of 9 on body condition, has another diagnosed condition, is a senior with muscle loss, or has lost weight without being on a diet, this is a veterinary conversation first and a feeding plan second. A blood panel before you begin is cheap compared with a year of dieting that was never going to work because the thyroid was driving it.

Is Your Cat Actually Overweight?

The 2022 US prevalence survey put 61 percent of cats at overweight or obese — 28 percent in the overweight band and 33 percent in the obese band — with only 38 percent at an ideal body condition. The same survey found that 28 percent of owners of cats scoring 6 to 9 out of 9 described their cat as normal, ideal or thin before they were told the score.

So the first step is not a diet. It is agreeing that there is a problem, using something more reliable than a glance.

The four hands-on checks

Ribs. Rest your palms flat on the chest, no pressing. An ideal cat feels like the back of your hand — ribs under a thin layer of skin. An overweight cat feels like the heel of your hand. An obese cat feels like a padded cushion with something hard buried in it.

Waist from above. Look down on your cat while it stands. There should be an hourglass narrowing behind the rib cage. A straight line is overweight; a bulge on either side of the spine is obese.

Tuck from the side. At the same standing view, the belly line should rise from the rib cage toward the hind legs. A hanging belly with no tuck is the pattern to flag.

The fat pad. Run a hand along the spine and over the hips. There should be a thin covering, not a ridge you have to push through to find bone.

Two things get mistaken for fat and are not. The primordial pouch — the loose skin hanging under the belly — is normal anatomy in most cats and is not a body condition score. And a long-haired cat can look twice its weight; judge by hands, then confirm on a scale.

That last column contains the most useful arithmetic on this page. If a score of 8 to 9 corresponds to roughly 30 percent above ideal body weight, then a first estimate of the target is simply current weight divided by 1.3. A 16 lb cat at score 8 has an estimated ideal of about 12.3 lb. It is an estimate, not a verdict — the score is what you are actually managing — but it gives you a number to compute calories from instead of guessing, and it is the number a veterinarian will refine at the next visit.

For a cat in the 6–7 band the correction is smaller, and the honest answer is that no single percentage is defensible. Work backwards instead: pick the highest weight at which the ribs are still palpable under light pressure, feed for that, and let the weekly scale tell you whether the estimate was right.

Why a Cat Cannot Diet Like a Dog

Everything else in this article is arithmetic. This section is the reason the arithmetic has a floor.

When a cat stops eating, fat leaves its storage sites and arrives at the liver faster than the liver can oxidise or export it. The fat accumulates inside the hepatocytes, the organ stops working, and the cat becomes jaundiced, reclusive and — characteristically — afraid of its own food bowl. Cornell's clinical description is blunt about the mechanism and the stakes: hepatic lipidosis is "almost always accompanied by or preceded by the onset of anorexia", and in more than 90 percent of cases it is a secondary consequence of something else, with obesity heading the list of underlying conditions. Their clinical faculty put it more simply: the incidence "seems much higher in obese cats".

That combination — an overweight cat, a period of not eating, a liver that cannot keep up — is why a cat is not a small dog with a smaller bowl. Two consequences follow directly.

The fear is real, but it is aimed at the wrong number

Search for how to diet a cat and you will find a lot of advice never to cut calories by more than 25 percent. The number is not wrong in spirit, but the evidence behind the danger zone is narrower and more specific than the folk version suggests.

A review of the feline hepatic lipidosis literature lays out the actual experimental record. In one study, obese cats fed 60 percent of their calculated maintenance energy requirement for 14 weeks did not accumulate liver lipid. In another, cats fed 50 percent of maintenance for 29 days developed no clinical signs, no characteristic biochemical changes and no liver morphology consistent with the disease. When intake was dropped to 25 percent of maintenance, lipid did accumulate.

Read those together and the picture is clearer than the folklore. A 20 percent deficit — roughly what a 0.8 times resting-energy plan produces — sits a long way above the threshold the studies were probing. What the studies do not license is the thing that actually causes most cases in practice: the cat that stops eating altogether, because the diet food was unpalatable, the household changed, another cat arrived, or the bowl got moved next to the vacuum cleaner.

The operational rule. A plan is safe when the cat keeps eating it. A plan is dangerous the moment the cat refuses it, regardless of how modest the calorie target looked on paper. If your cat goes 48 hours without eating anything, that is a same-day veterinary call, not a wait-and-see week — and that rule applies with extra force to a cat that is already overweight.

Two more differences that matter

Protein is not optional. Cats are obligate carnivores, and the weight they lose on a poorly built diet is not all fat. A study in cats found that increasing dietary protein during weight loss promoted fat loss while reducing the loss of lean body mass. Practically: when you cut calories, cut fat and carbohydrate density, not protein density. This is the single strongest argument against diluting a maintenance food with filler to make it stretch.

Muscle loss is silent on a scale. A cat can hit its target weight and come out weaker, because some of what it lost was muscle. That is why the clinical protocols monitor a muscle condition score alongside body condition at every weigh-in, and why a falling muscle score is listed as a reason to slow the rate of loss rather than celebrate the number. Nor is this a cosmetic project: the largest UK life table associates every kilogram away from a cat’s breed-and-sex median weight with about 0.2 fewer years, which is the arithmetic behind the lifespan figures.

The Arithmetic: Feed for the Target, Not the Current

Here is the calculation, and then the reason most people get it almost right.

Resting energy requirement — the calories a cat burns doing nothing but existing — scales with metabolic body size:

RER (kcal/day) = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75

Multiply by an activity factor to get daily energy requirement. A sedentary indoor cat sits around 1.2. For weight loss, the working factor is 0.8. The exponent is not arbitrary: it is Kleiber's law, the observation that metabolic rate scales to the three-quarter power of body mass across mammals, which is why a cat needs more calories per pound than a dog and a dog more than a horse. Our cat calorie calculator runs this arithmetic for you, including the per-day food conversion.

The worked example that changes everything

Take a 16 lb cat at body condition score 8. The estimate of ideal weight is 16 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 12.3 lb, or about 5.6 kg.

Feed for that: 70 × 5.60.75 ≈ 249 kcal is the resting requirement at the target weight, and 249 × 0.8 ≈ 200 kcal per day.

Now do it the way most people do it — 0.8 times the resting requirement at the cat's current 16 lb (7.26 kg): 70 × 7.260.75 ≈ 309 kcal, times 0.8 ≈ 247 kcal per day.

Both numbers produce weight loss. The second one produces weight loss that slows to nothing. Here is why: as the cat drops from 16 lb toward 12, the resting requirement for its current weight falls with it. At 13 lb the same formula gives about 208 kcal, and 0.8 of that is 167 — which is below the 247 you were feeding. In other words, a plan anchored to current weight has a moving target built into it, and the deficit closes itself. A plan anchored to target weight has a fixed endpoint: when the cat reaches 12.3 lb, 200 kcal is the maintenance for that weight, and you already know what to feed forever.

The other legitimate method

If you have a measured baseline — you know exactly how many calories the cat was eating to hold its current weight — you can simply cut that by 10 to 20 percent and adjust from there. Veterinary weight-management protocols use the same move at plateaus: reduce intake by about 10 percent when the loss stalls. It is a perfectly good approach. It just requires the baseline to be real, which is what the next four sections are about.

Whatever number you land on, treat it as a hypothesis with a two-week expiry date. The clinical follow-up interval is a weigh-in every two to four weeks, precisely because the calculation is an estimate and the scale is the correction.

How Fast Is Safe

The 2014 AAHA weight management guidelines set the target rate for cats at 0.5 to 2 percent of body weight per week, with the same guideline listing a falling muscle condition score, a rate above 2 percent, or a comorbid condition as reasons to slow down. AAHA's own client-facing material tightens the message for cat owners to "no more than 1 to 2 percent" and names hepatic lipidosis as the reason.

Read those two together and you get a practical band rather than a single figure: 1 percent a week is the target, 2 percent is the ceiling, and anything above the ceiling is a stop sign. The lower half of the guideline range exists for a reason — cats on over-the-counter diets, cats with other conditions, and cats whose muscle score is slipping should be at the slow end, because the risk being managed is nutrient deficiency and lean mass loss, not just liver fat.

Those numbers are why a kitchen scale beats a bathroom scale for this job. A 16 lb cat at 1 percent a week is losing about 2.6 ounces — 74 grams — a week. A human bathroom scale cannot see that clearly, and the day-to-day noise from a full stomach, a full bladder and a litter tray visit is bigger than the signal. A baby scale or a luggage scale with a carrier resolves it.

Two things that look like failure and are not

Week one is mostly water and gut contents. It is normal to see a fast drop in the first seven to ten days and then a much slower line. Judge the plan on weeks three onward.

The rate slows as the cat shrinks, because the percentage is of a smaller number. This is arithmetic, not metabolic collapse. Going from 16 lb to 12 lb is a 25 percent reduction; at 1 percent of current weight per week it takes roughly 29 weeks — about seven months. At 2 percent it takes about 14 weeks. Neither number is a sign that something is wrong; both are the guideline working exactly as written.

What is a sign of something wrong: a cat at 3 percent a week or more, a cat that is losing weight while eating its full ration, or a cat whose ribs start to show before its waist does. All three go to the vet, not to a stricter diet.

The Scale Problem

You cannot run a measurement loop without a measurement. Three practical rules make the weekly weigh-in reliable enough to act on.

Weigh the cat, not yourself holding the cat. A human scale plus subtraction carries an error bigger than the weekly loss. A baby scale, or a luggage scale clipped to a carrier weighed empty then with the cat in it, resolves to a tenth of a pound or better.

Same scale, same time, same conditions. First thing in the morning, before breakfast, after the tray. Record the number in one place — a note on the fridge beats an app you will stop opening.

Monthly at the vet, weekly at home. The clinical protocol is a weigh-in every two to four weeks, and a clinic scale is more accurate than anything you own. Use it as the audit; use your home number for the week-to-week decisions.

The most useful habit is a two-week rolling average rather than a weekly verdict. One week's 0.2 lb that turns out to be a meal is not information. Two weeks of flat line is.

Where the Calories Actually Hide

If a cat is not losing weight on a measured ration, the ration is not what the cat is eating. In practice the extra calories come through a short list of doors, and it is worth walking the house and closing them before you cut the ration again.

The treat budget is smaller than anyone expects

AAHA's client guidance puts the daily treat allowance for an 8 lb cat at ideal body condition at about 20 calories. That is the whole budget. Against it, the numbers used in our calorie calculator are instructive: ten small soft treats come to roughly 20 calories, two dental chews to roughly 30, and a single tablespoon of wet food used as a topper to about 15.

Treat energy varies by brand and size, so read the packet rather than trusting these figures — but the order of magnitude is the point. Two dental chews are not "a couple of biscuits"; they are one and a half days of the treat allowance, and on a 200 kcal plan they are a 15 percent overshoot.

Then walk the house. The other doors are: a second cat's bowl (below), a dog's bowl, a partner or child who feeds "because she asked", a neighbour who does the same, milk or cream, hairball gel, pill pockets, and the water bowl being next to the food bowl so the cat grazes all evening. None of these are dramatic. All of them are invisible to a person who believes the cat is on 200 calories a day.

Free-feeding is the structural problem. A bowl that is never empty cannot be measured, and it defeats every calculation on this page. If you take one action before reading further, take this one: stop free-feeding and move to measured meals — two, three or four a day, whichever the household can actually sustain.

Weigh the Food, Not the Cup

A measuring cup is a volume instrument being used to deliver an energy target, and the conversion between them depends on the shape of the kibble, the size of the pieces, and how densely the scoop is packed. Two people scooping "half a cup" from the same bag can be 20 percent apart without either of them doing anything wrong.

The fix costs about the price of a coffee and removes an entire class of error. A digital kitchen scale that reads to a gram turns the target into a mass:

grams per day = daily kcal ÷ (kcal per kg of food ÷ 1000)

On a 3,600 kcal/kg dry food, a 200 kcal target is 200 ÷ 3.6 ≈ 56 grams a day. On a 4,200 kcal/kg food it is 48 grams. That difference — eight grams, about a sixth of the ration — is the size of error that produces a stall, and it is invisible on a cup. A basic digital kitchen scale is the only piece of equipment this plan actually requires.

Two habits that make it stick

Weigh the day's ration once, in the morning. Put it in a container. Feed from the container all day. When the container is empty, the cat is done — and nobody has to do arithmetic at seven in the evening.

Weigh wet food too, if you are mixing. "Three ounces" on a can is a volume claim made at packing. If wet food is carrying half the calories, it needs the same treatment as the dry.

One caution that belongs here rather than in a footnote: if you are weighing, you will discover the label's feeding guide and your calculation disagree. They usually do, because the guide is written for an intact, active cat. Follow the arithmetic, then let the scale tell you whether to adjust by 10 percent in two weeks.

Wet, Dry, and the Protein Question

Three variables matter here and they are usually argued about separately: water, protein, and volume.

Water. The best-controlled evidence on the water question comes from a crossover study in cats fed two diets identical in nutrient profile on a dry-matter basis — one canned, one freeze-dried to a low water content. Cats on the high-water diet ate significantly less energy, about 1,053 kJ a day against 1,414 kJ a day, roughly 252 against 338 kcal, and lost body weight while body composition stayed the same. In preference tests they ate more of the wet food, which is the part that matters for compliance: the food that fills the stomach was also the food they liked.

The caveats are real and worth knowing. The study used 16 intact male cats, not obese household cats; each diet period was three weeks; and the two diets were the same food with water added or removed, which is not the same as comparing a supermarket kibble with a supermarket pâté. What it supports is narrow and useful: bulk water reduces energy intake without reducing palatability. What it does not support is free-feeding wet food and assuming the water does the work.

Protein. In cats, higher dietary protein during weight loss has been shown to promote fat loss while reducing the loss of lean body mass. This is the mechanism behind the standard advice to choose a high-protein, low-carbohydrate weight-management food rather than simply a smaller amount of the old one. It also explains why "just feed less of the current food" eventually shows up as a cat that reaches its target weight with less muscle than it started with — visible as a dropping muscle condition score, and on the cat as a bonier spine with the same waist.

Volume. Fibre is a legitimate satiety tool and appears in clinical troubleshooting for begging. The trap is using fibre to stretch a maintenance food: you dilute protein at the same time, which is the wrong trade for a cat. If you need bulk, use a diet built for it rather than adding filler.

A practical arrangement that satisfies all three: measured wet food as the base of each meal, dry food weighed in grams as the remainder, and the carbohydrate and calorie split checked once so you know what proportion of the ration is doing which job. Then stop changing it. Consistency is worth more than optimisation once the numbers are in range.

Multi-Cat Households: The Actual Blocker

In a two-cat house, the diet is usually not for the cat that is eating the food. It is for the cat that finishes first, and the overweight cat is almost always the slower, larger one.

This is a logistics problem with four workable solutions, in increasing order of cost:

Separate rooms at mealtimes. Feed in two closed rooms for fifteen minutes, then remove what is left. It is free, it works, and it fails only when the household is not home at the same time every day.

Elevated or gated feeding. A baby gate with a cat-sized gap, or a shelf the slimmer cat can reach and the heavier one will not attempt, works for some pairs. It is unreliable for athletic cats and unkind to arthritic ones.

Timed feeders. Useful for portion control and for the cat that wakes the house at four in the morning, less useful for preventing theft unless each cat has its own.

Microchip-reading feeders. The lid opens only for the cat registered to it. This is the only solution that works while nobody is home, and it is the one clinical weight-management protocols name specifically for multi-pet households. It is also the most expensive, and it does not solve the problem of a determined cat entering behind another one — which is why it is worth watching the first week rather than trusting the box.

Whatever you choose, weigh both cats. The slim cat is the canary: if it is losing weight on a plan designed for the other one, food is moving in the wrong direction and you have found the leak without having to guess.

One more household note that belongs in this section: a single cat with a bowl down all day is not "grazing naturally", it is a cat with no meal structure and no way to measure anything. Structured meals also fix the 4 a.m. problem, because a cat that expects food at set times stops campaigning for it at random ones.

Exercise a Cat Will Actually Do

Be honest about the arithmetic: exercise is a small lever on the calorie balance and a large lever on muscle, mood and behaviour. An extra ten minutes of chasing a wand toy does not offset a bowl of kibble. It does keep the cat from spending the diet asleep, and it is the part of the plan the cat enjoys.

AAHA's client guidance suggests at least two play sessions of ten to fifteen minutes a day. The way to get a cat to accept them is to borrow the shape of a hunt rather than the shape of a workout:

Stalk, chase, pounce, catch, eat. Move the toy away from the cat, let it stalk, let it almost catch it, then let it catch it. The catch is not optional. A session that never ends in a capture is a session that ends in frustration, which is the main argument against the laser pointer — there is nothing to catch, so the sequence never closes. If you use one, finish on a physical toy the cat can bite.

Feed after play. Ending the sequence with a measured meal is how the hunting circuit closes in a real cat. It also makes a meal out of the smallest ration of the day, which is the one the cat is most likely to complain about.

Make the food work for itself. Scatter a portion of the dry ration across a hard floor, or put it in a puzzle feeder. The cat spends longer eating, eats less at once, and gets a small amount of activity. A simple puzzle feeder is the cheapest enrichment purchase in this whole plan.

Use the vertical space you already have. Cats are built to climb, and climbing is real load-bearing work for a heavier cat. The AAFP and ISFM environmental needs guidelines treat this as a core requirement rather than a luxury: resources that are spread out, at different heights and in different rooms, produce a more active animal. If your cat has never had a shelf, the first jump onto it is a training session of its own — and for an obese or arthritic cat, it is also a fall risk, so put it low and add a step.

The Plateau, and What to Do With It

Almost every successful diet has at least one plateau, and most have several. The clinical approach is not to panic and not to halve the ration. It is a short, ordered checklist.

First, check the arithmetic. If you have been feeding the same grams for three months, the deficit has closed by itself as the cat got lighter. Recompute the resting requirement at the current weight and see whether the number on the bowl still represents a deficit at all. This is the single most common cause and it requires no willpower from anyone.

Second, audit the pipeline. Two weeks of honest weighing of the cat and the food will usually find the extra treats, the second bowl, or the neighbour. Do this before cutting calories, because cutting while an unmeasured leak persists just makes the cat hungry and the owner guilty.

Third, if the cat is genuinely adherent, cut about 10 percent. That is the standard move in weight-management protocols, and it is deliberately small: 10 percent of 200 kcal is 20 kcal, which at the guideline rate is several weeks of progress. Cutting more than that to force the number down is how muscle gets lost and how cats stop eating.

Fourth, know your floor. The plan's target is 0.8 times the resting requirement at goal weight, which is where you started. Going meaningfully below that without veterinary supervision is where the liver risk and the nutrient-deficiency risk stop being theoretical. If you are adherent, being measured, and still not losing at that level, the answer is a veterinary assessment — not a smaller bowl.

Fifth, give it four weeks. Two weigh-ins. A fortnight of flat line in a cat that is otherwise well is not a plateau, it is noise, and the response to noise is to change nothing.

When to Stop Dieting and Call the Vet

This page describes a plan for a healthy overweight cat. It is not a plan for a cat that is losing weight for a reason. The distinction matters, because the most expensive mistake in this whole area is dieting a cat whose weight is a symptom.

Before you start, if any of these are true: the cat scores 8 or 9, has a diagnosed condition, takes medication, is over ten years old, has lost weight recently without a diet, or is drinking and urinating more than usual. Those are not contraindications to weight loss; they are reasons for bloodwork first. An untreated hyperthyroid cat will lose weight no matter what you feed it, and a diabetic cat needs insulin before it needs a smaller bowl.

Seniors, Muscle Loss, and the Cat That Is Not Fat

Body condition score measures fat. It says nothing about muscle, and in older cats the two come apart.

That is what a muscle condition score is for. Run your hands over the spine, the shoulder blades and the hip bones: a cat with good muscle has a slight covering over each, and a cat losing muscle has ridges you can count. The score has been validated against body composition measurement in cats, which is why clinical protocols now record it next to body condition at every visit rather than treating it as an optional extra.

Three situations follow from this, and they all point away from restriction:

The cat that is thin but weak. A senior cat at body condition 4 with obvious spine ridges is not a candidate for a calorie cut; it is a candidate for a protein-dense diet and a veterinary work-up. Ageing cats often need more protein density, not less.

The cat that has stopped grooming and stopped jumping. That is often pain, not laziness. An arthritic cat cannot exercise its way out of obesity while the joint hurts, and pain control is what makes the activity half of the plan possible. Weight loss still helps the joint — it just has to start with the food, gently.

The cat with kidney disease. Weight loss in a cat with chronic kidney disease is a symptom to be investigated, not a goal to be pursued. If your cat is in that group, the relevant reading is kidney disease prevention, and the plan belongs to your vet.

The general rule for the second half of a cat's life: judge the trend in both scores. Fat coming off with muscle holding is success. Fat coming off with muscle going is a plan that needs changing, even if the scale looks perfect.

Holding the Loss

The end of a diet is not the end of the plan. It is the point where the plan changes from a deficit to a maintenance calculation — and where most of the work of the previous year is either protected or given back.

Do not just stop. When the cat reaches target, it is eating 0.8 times the resting requirement at that weight. Maintenance for the same cat is roughly 1.2 times the resting requirement — so the transition is an increase of about 50 percent from the diet ration, delivered in steps rather than in one jump. Adding it back in 5 to 10 percent increments, with a weigh-in between each, lets you find the actual maintenance without overshooting. Many cats end up needing less than the formula predicts.

Be careful what you switch to. If the diet was a purpose-built low-calorie food, moving to a standard maintenance food at the same volume is a calorie increase dressed up as a like-for-like swap. Compare the kcal per kilogram, not the bag size, and recalculate the grams.

Keep weighing. Monthly, forever, on the same scale. A cat that regains two pounds has undone most of a year; the only early warning is a number you are still writing down.

Expect the campaign to be long. A 16 lb cat going to 12 lb at 1 percent a week is a seven-month project, and the last two pounds take as long as the first two. Owners who expect six weeks and get twenty-eight weeks are the ones who quit at week nine. The rate is set by the liver, not by motivation.

One last thing, because it is the part nobody says: the cat will ask for food, and it will ask in the most persuasive way it knows. The plan is not to out-argue it. The plan is to make the measured ration large in volume, split across more meals, delivered after play, and then to stop negotiating. A cat that is fed four small meals a day is a cat that has less to campaign about than one fed twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should an overweight cat eat to lose weight?

Feed 0.8 times the resting energy requirement (70 times body weight in kilograms to the power 0.75) calculated at the target weight, not the current one. For a cat whose target is 12 lb (5.4 kg), that is 249 times 0.8, about 200 kcal a day. Feeding 0.8 times the requirement at the current weight gives a larger number that produces initial loss and then stalls, because the deficit shrinks as the cat does. Use the target-weight figure, then adjust by 10 percent after a two-week weigh-in if nothing is moving.

How fast can a cat safely lose weight?

The 2014 AAHA guidelines give 0.5 to 2 percent of body weight per week for cats, and AAHA's client guidance tightens that to no more than 1 to 2 percent. In practice, aim for 1 percent and treat 2 percent as a ceiling. For a 16 lb cat, 1 percent is about 2.6 ounces a week. Faster loss buys a shorter timeline at the cost of lean muscle and, in the worst case, hepatic lipidosis — which is why a falling muscle condition score is treated as a reason to slow down rather than speed up.

Can I put my cat on a diet without seeing a vet?

For a healthy cat at body condition 6 or 7 with no other diagnoses, a measured plan at the guideline rate is reasonable and is what this page describes. See a vet first if the cat scores 8 or 9, is over ten, has any diagnosed condition or medication, is losing weight without a diet, or is drinking and urinating more than usual. Those cases need bloodwork because the weight may be a symptom rather than a cause. A single pre-diet panel is inexpensive and it rules out the scenario where a year of dieting was never going to work.

Why is my cat not losing weight when I have cut the food down?

Three causes account for almost all of it. The ration is not measured — cups vary by 20 percent between people and between kibble shapes, so weigh in grams. Something unmeasured is arriving — treats, a second cat's bowl, a dog's bowl, a partner, a neighbour. Or the plan was never recalculated, so the deficit closed itself as the cat got lighter. Work through them in that order before cutting anything further, and give each change two weigh-ins before judging it.

Is wet food better than dry for weight loss?

The water helps, but it is not magic. In a controlled crossover study with diets identical on a dry-matter basis, cats on the high-water version ate about 252 kcal a day against 338 on the low-water version and lost body weight, while preferring the wet food in preference tests. That is a real effect and it improves compliance because the cat feels full. It does not mean wet food can be free-fed: a can left down all day is the same measurement problem as a bowl of kibble left down all day.

Will dieting give my cat fatty liver?

Not at the guideline rate. The experimental record shows obese cats fed 60 percent of maintenance for 14 weeks did not accumulate liver lipid, and cats fed 50 percent for 29 days developed no signs of the disease; accumulation appeared at 25 percent of maintenance. The real-world trigger is not a modest deficit — it is the cat that stops eating entirely, for any reason. So the safety rule is behavioural rather than numerical: if your cat refuses food for 48 hours, that is a same-day vet call regardless of what the calorie target says.

Does my cat need a prescription weight-loss diet?

Not always, but it is the tool with the most evidence behind it, and survey data suggests only about one in five owners of overweight pets have tried one. The case for a purpose-built diet is that it delivers a calorie deficit while keeping protein density high and micronutrients adequate — the two things that go wrong when you simply feed less of a maintenance food. Over-the-counter options can work if you read the kcal per kilogram and keep the protein up, but the narrower the ration gets, the more a properly formulated diet matters.
