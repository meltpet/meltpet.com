🐱 How to Introduce a New Cat — Without Starting a War

The scent-first method that prevents hissing, hiding, and litter box wars. A realistic day-by-day timeline. | 2026-08-11 · MeltPet · 8 min read

Last updated: 2026-08-11

The Worst Way to Introduce Cats (Everyone Does It)

You bring the new cat home in a carrier. You set the carrier down in the living room, open the door, and let the resident cat investigate. Two seconds later there's hissing, a blur of fur, and the new cat is wedged behind the refrigerator for the next six hours.

This is the "let them work it out" method, and it's exactly backwards. Cats are territorial by nature —the single most important thing about them for this task. Dumping a stranger into a resident cat's territory isn't an introduction, it's an invasion. The result isn't friendship; it's a stressed resident cat, a traumatized newcomer, and sometimes a vet bill.

The method that actually works is scent-first: keep the cats physically separated, let them get used to each other's smell through doors and swapped bedding, and only bring them face-to-face once the scent says "familiar." It takes days, not hours. Here's exactly how to do it.

Before the New Cat Arrives: Set Up the Safe Room

The single most important prep step happens before the carrier comes through the door.

Pick a room the resident cat doesn't treasure —a spare bedroom, a bathroom, a home office —and make it the new cat's room. It needs:

  • Food and water in separate bowls, placed away from the door
  • A litter box at the opposite end of the room from the food (cats won't eat near their toilet)
  • Hiding spots: a cardboard box with a blanket, or the carrier itself with the door open and a towel over it. A new cat's default instinct is to hide; give it somewhere safe to do that
  • A scratching post, because scratching spreads scent and marks territory —which is what you want the new cat to do in its room

Pro move: plug a Feliway pheromone diffuser in the safe room a few days before the new cat arrives, and one in the main living area. Feliway releases a synthetic version of the facial pheromone cats use to mark safe territory —it's an assist, not a fix, but owners who use it during introductions consistently report less hissing and faster settling. It's cheap insurance for a stressful process.

Step 1 (Days 1–2): The Safe Room, Zero Contact

Bring the new cat straight into the safe room. Close the door. Do not let the resident cat in.

This sounds harsh, but it's the kindest thing you can do. The new cat gets to decompress in a small, quiet space that smells only of itself (and you). The resident cat gets to process the smell of a stranger at a safe distance.

Your job during these two days:

  • Visit the new cat regularly —sit on the floor, talk quietly, offer treats. Don't force contact if it hides. Let the cat come to you
  • Feed both cats near their sides of the closed door, at the same time. This builds a positive association: the smell of the other cat = food time
  • Watch for the new cat eating. Most cats eat within 24–48 hours once they feel safe. If it won't eat after two full days, call your vet —stress can suppress appetite enough to cause real problems

Step 2 (Days 2–4): Scent Swapping

Now you're going to let them trade smells without ever meeting.

  • Swap bedding: take a blanket the new cat has slept on and put it where the resident cat sleeps, and vice versa. Do this daily
  • Hand towels: rub a towel on the new cat's cheeks (scent glands), then leave it in the resident cat's area. Repeat in reverse
  • Praise and treat: whenever a cat sniffs the other's scent calmly —no hissing, no growling —reward it immediately with a treat. You're teaching "that smell = good things"

Some hissing at the swapped bedding is normal and fine. Hissing is communication, not a failure. What you're watching for is the trend: if day 4 is calmer than day 2, you're on track. If the resident cat is spending hours growling at the door, slow down and spend more days on this step.

Step 3 (Days 3–6): Visual Contact, Separated

Now they get to see each other —but not touch.

The cleanest way is a door crack or a baby gate. Prop the safe room door open just a few inches with a doorstop, or set up a baby gate (cats can jump most gates, so use one tall enough, or just use the door crack method —it's more reliable).

  • Do this during meal times, when both cats are focused on food
  • Keep sessions short: 5–15 minutes, a few times a day
  • Feed treats to both cats the entire time they can see each other
  • If either cat hisses, that's OK —let it happen, keep the food coming. End the session if it escalates to growling + puffed fur + flattened ears

The goal of this step isn't peace; it's tolerated presence. Cats that eat calmly while seeing each other are learning that the other cat is part of the scenery, not a threat.

Step 4 (Days 5–8): Short Supervised Meetings

Time for the real thing —but short, supervised, and with escape routes.

  • Let the new cat out of the safe room while you're home and watching
  • Keep the safe room door open so it can retreat at any time
  • Have a toy or treat ready to redirect any tense moment
  • Start with 15–20 minutes, once or twice a day

Expect hissing, growling, maybe a swat. All of that is normal. The line you don't cross is actual fighting —fur flying, claws out, chasing that doesn't stop. If that happens, separate them immediately and go back to Step 3 for a few more days. Cats don't resolve conflict by fighting; they avoid each other and hold grudges. A fight now can set the relationship back weeks.

Step 5 (Days 7–14): Full Access, Keep Monitoring

Once meetings have been calm for a few days running —tolerated presence, no fights —let them share the house full-time.

You're not done yet, but the hard part is over. Keep an eye on:

  • Food guarding: feed in separate spots for the first week. Two bowls far apart is fine; one bowl is asking for trouble
  • Litter boxes: the rule is one box per cat, plus one extra. Three boxes for two cats, in different rooms, is the peacekeeping standard. A cat that suddenly stops using the box is a stress signal —check the health and stress signals and go back a step
  • Sleeping arrangements: let them figure it out. Cats that end up napping near each other (even if not touching) have made it

Some pairs never become cuddly, and that's fine. Tolerated coexistence —each cat living its life, occasionally touching noses —counts as success. Most cat pairs take 1–2 weeks to reach that point; stubborn pairs can take a month. The cats set the pace.

When It's Not Going Well: Troubleshooting

  • The resident cat won't stop growling at the door. It needs more time on scent swapping. Stop the visual sessions, spend 2–3 extra days on bedding swaps and door-feeding.
  • The new cat won't come out of hiding. It needs more time and less pressure. Visit quietly, leave treats near its spot, don't force it. If it's been more than two days without eating, call the vet.
  • They fought. Separate, check for injuries (puncture wounds need a vet —they infect fast), and drop back to Step 3. One fight isn't the end; a pattern of fights means the introduction is moving too fast.
  • Litter box accidents. Add boxes, move them to quieter spots, and make sure they're scooped daily. A dirty or threatened box is the top cause of out-of-box peeing during introductions.

If you're four weeks in and still seeing regular fights or constant hiding, it's worth a call to a veterinary behaviorist. That's not failure —cat introductions are genuinely hard, and some pairs need professional help. The money you spend on a behaviorist is nothing compared to a lifetime of two miserable cats.

What This Actually Costs (Beyond the Cat)

Before you bring the second cat home, run the numbers —because the second cat isn't half the cost of the first, it's nearly the full cost:

  • Double the food, litter, and vet baseline —two cats, two of everything
  • Initial setup: carrier, bowls, litter box, scratcher, toys for the new cat —$150–400
  • Optional but recommended: Feliway diffuser setup (~$60 for starter kit with refill), a taller cat tree so each cat has vertical territory

The honest version of this whole article is one sentence: go slow, use scent, and let the cats set the pace. Do that, and in a few weeks you'll have two cats that tolerate each other's existence —and occasionally, if you're lucky, two cats curled up on the same couch. That's the reward for a week of patience.
