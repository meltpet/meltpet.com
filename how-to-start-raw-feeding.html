🦴 How to Start Raw Feeding

A straight-talk beginner's guide —the 80/10/5/5 math, a safe transition, and the mistakes that land dogs at the ER. | 2026-09-10 · MeltPet · 11 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-10

The Honest Summary, Up Front

Raw feeding isn't "just meat," and it isn't automatically healthier than a good kibble. If you feed it correctly —80% muscle meat, 10% edible bone, 5% liver, 5% other secreting organ, plus a short list of supplements —it's a nutritionally complete diet most dogs thrive on. If you feed it as a chicken breast over rice, you're slowly creating a deficiency. A 30 lb adult dog eats roughly 340 grams of raw per day (about 2.5% of body weight). That's 272g muscle, 34g bone, 17g liver, 17g other organ — every single day, not on average. If that math sounds like more work than you signed up for, feed a high-quality kibble and stop reading. This guide is for people who've already decided to try raw and want to do it without getting their dog (or themselves) sick.

Why I Won't Tell You Raw Is "Natural" or "Species-Appropriate"

I spent five years writing dog food labels for a mid-size manufacturer —the job I wrote about in my dry vs wet vs raw comparison. In that time I watched marketing teams slap "ancestral" and "biologically appropriate" on bags of extruded kibble without blinking. Those words don't describe a diet. They describe a feeling. Raw feeding has its own version of this language, and it's just as unhelpful. "Natural" doesn't mean balanced. A wolf's diet isn't a domestic dog's ideal —dogs have been eating our leftovers and, later, our kibble for 15,000 years, and their digestion has adapted.

What raw feeding genuinely has going for it is control and simplicity of ingredients: no rendered mystery meals, no extrusion that destroys nutrients and requires re-spraying them on afterward, no need for carbohydrate to hold the pellet together. Most owners report smaller, firmer stools and better coats within weeks. What it costs you is responsibility: you become the formulator. Get the ratio wrong and the dog pays for it months later in ways that are hard to trace back to dinner.

80/10/5/5 Isn't a Rule. It's a Nutrient Map.

People treat the "prey model" ratio like a fad diet. It isn't. Each slice of that ratio exists because a specific nutrient lives in a specific tissue:

The 80% muscle meat —chicken thigh, beef chuck, pork shoulder, heart, gizzard —provides protein, B vitamins, and most of the phosphorus. "Muscle meat" includes meaty organs like heart and gizzard, which many feeders count toward this bucket. It does not include liver.

The 10% edible bone —chicken necks, wings, backs, soft ribs —is the calcium source. Bone is where the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio lives, and that ratio is the single most dangerous number in raw feeding. Too little bone and you get calcium deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism —rubber bones in puppies, osteoporosis in adults. Too much and you get constipation and, over time, mineral imbalances. The 10% figure exists because whole-prey analysis shows a prey animal is about 10% bone by weight. It's anatomy, not orthodoxy.

The 5% liver —vitamin A, copper, and iron. Liver is so concentrated that going over ~5-7% causes vitamin A toxicity over months. Going under causes deficiency. Both are real, and both are slow.

The 5% other secreting organ —kidney, spleen, pancreas, brain —provides things muscle and liver don't have enough of. This is the bucket most beginners skip, and it's the one that quietly matters for long-term mineral balance.

This is also why "I feed my dog raw chicken breast and broccoli" isn't raw feeding —it's an unbalanced homemade diet with extra steps. If you only remember one sentence from this guide: a raw diet is a ratio, not an ingredient list.

A Worked Example: 340g a Day for a 30 lb Dog

Here's what a complete day looks like for a 30 lb (13.6 kg) adult, neutered dog at maintenance. The starting point for daily amount is 2–3% of body weight —use 2.5% as your default and adjust by body condition, not by appetite.

On top of the tissue, a balanced home-prepared raw diet needs a handful of supplements —vitamin E, omega-3 (fish oil), zinc, manganese, and iodine most commonly. Cats additionally require taurine every single day; a cat on raw without taurine supplementation is a heart failure waiting to happen. ⚖️ Bone is the guardrail. If you drift the bone percentage by more than about 2 points in either direction, have the recipe reviewed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist before you feed it long-term. Getting Ca:P right matters more than anything else in this guide.

The Transition: 7 Days, Not 7 Hours

A dog who has eaten kibble for years has a gut microbiome tuned to starch. Dumping raw into that system on day one produces what raw feeders euphemistically call "the cleanse" —and what vets call diarrhea. Transition over about a week, watching stool daily:

  • Days 1–2: 75% old food, 25% raw, mixed in the same bowl.
  • Days 3–4: 50/50. Stool should start getting smaller and firmer.
  • Days 5–6: 25% old food, 75% raw.
  • Day 7: 100% raw. Give the changeover two weeks before judging stool quality.

Puppies and seniors can take longer —stretch the same steps over 10–14 days. If stool turns liquid, step back one stage for two days, then advance again more slowly. A little loose stool during transition is normal. Bloody stool, vomiting, or lethargy is not —stop, and call your vet. The same "slow wins" logic applies if you later switch proteins: many dogs handle beef fine but react to the sudden arrival of a novel protein like lamb or venison.

Four Mistakes That Land New Raw Feeders at the Vet

Over the years I've watched the same four errors repeat in every raw feeding group. None of them are exotic. All of them are avoidable.

1. Treating muscle meat as the whole diet. The classic: chicken thighs and nothing else, because "that's what they'd eat in the wild." It isn't —a wild canid eats the organs and bone too. Deficiency from a meat-only diet shows up as lethargy and a dull coat at first, and as bone disease or heart problems later.

2. Cooking the bones or feeding weight-bearing bones. Cooked bone splinters —that's a perforated intestine and a $3,000 surgery. Weight-bearing bones from large animals (beef femurs) are hard enough to crack teeth. Edible bone means soft, flexible, raw poultry bone sized to the dog, or bone ground into the mix.

3. Ignoring the supplement list because it feels unnatural. "Wild dogs don't take fish oil." True, and wild dogs also don't live to 13 on a fixed indoor routine. Modern muscle meat from the supermarket has less omega-3, less vitamin E, and less iodine than prey animals did. The supplements exist to close those gaps. Skip them and you're feeding a diet that looks complete and isn't.

4. Freezing, thawing, and refreezing carelessly. Freezing raw meat for at least three weeks at or below -18°C kills parasite risks (especially relevant for pork and wild game). But thawing on the counter, refreezing partially-used packs, or leaving a bowl out for hours turns a safe product into a bacterial incubator. Thaw in the fridge, portion before freezing, and pick up uneaten food after 20–30 minutes.

And one non-food mistake: not budgeting for it. Raw is usually the most expensive way to feed —in our food-format cost table for a 50 lb dog, commercial raw or fresh runs $200–$450/month versus $50–$100 for premium kibble. Owners who don't plan for that quietly drift back to kibble after two months, which is fine —but the dog just went through two gut resets for nothing. The Bacteria Question, Handled Honestly

Raw meat carries Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria. Here's the uncomfortable truth the raw feeding community doesn't lead with: your dog will probably handle these fine, but you and your family are the vulnerable ones. Dogs have short, highly acidic digestive tracts built for this. Humans don't. Infected dogs shed the bacteria in their saliva and feces for days, which is how it reaches the toddler who kisses the dog's face or the parent who doesn't wash after picking up the yard.

If anyone in your home is under five, over 65, pregnant, or immunocompromised, raw feeding is a household-health decision, not just a nutrition decision —many vets will advise against it outright, and that advice is defensible. If you proceed anyway, the hygiene protocol is non-negotiable: buy from reputable suppliers, freeze for three weeks, thaw in the fridge, wash every surface and bowl the raw touches with hot soapy water, wash your hands after feeding and after handling stool, and don't let the dog lick your face or the baby's face.

Gear You'll Want Before Day One

The good news: raw feeding gear is cheap compared to the food. Before you buy anything, get a kitchen scale accurate to 1g —the 80/10/5/5 split is gram math, and eyeballing a 17g slice of liver is how imbalances start. A dedicated grinder matters if you're feeding whole ground mixes or feeding a small dog (grinding bone into the mix is the safe way to give tiny dogs bone). Portion the week's food into individual meal containers on prep day and freeze what you won't use in 48 hours. That's the entire setup. Everything else on the raw-feeding influencer shelf is optional.

What About Cats?

The ratio above is the same for cats —with two differences that matter. Cats are obligate carnivores, so the "can I just feed kibble instead?" escape hatch is smaller; and cats must get supplemental taurine on any home-prepared diet, raw or cooked, because cooking and freezing degrade it and muscle meat alone doesn't provide enough. If you're feeding a cat, our raw food calculator forces the taurine line and handles the feline calorie math. Frequently Asked Questions

Can puppies eat raw food?

Yes, but the margin for error is much smaller. Growing puppies need a precisely balanced calcium-to-phosphorus ratio; get the bone percentage wrong and you can cause permanent skeletal deformity. If you want to raise a puppy on raw, use a commercial AAFCO-complete raw diet or have a board-certified veterinary nutritionist formulate the home-prepared recipe. Do not wing a DIY puppy raw diet —our puppy checklist covers the non-food parts of week one, but the food itself deserves a professional.

Can I mix raw food and kibble in the same meal?

Many dogs tolerate it fine, but feeding them in the same bowl is the one thing raw advocates will argue with you about —raw digests faster than kibble, and the two move through the gut at different speeds. There's no strong evidence that mixing is dangerous for a healthy dog, but separating meals (kibble at breakfast, raw at dinner) sidesteps the debate entirely and makes any digestive upset easier to diagnose.

Will raw bones choke my dog or break his teeth?

Edible raw bones —chicken necks, wings, backs —are soft and flexible enough that most dogs chew and swallow them safely. Cooked bones are the danger: heat makes them brittle and splintery, so never feed cooked bones. Recreational weight-bearing bones (beef femurs) are hard enough to fracture a carnassial tooth, so size them generously and supervise. When in doubt, grind the bone into the mix instead of feeding it whole.
