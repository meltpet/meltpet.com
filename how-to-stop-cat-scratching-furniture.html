How to Stop a Cat Scratching Furniture

It is not spite, it cannot be trained away, and one piece of advice everybody repeats makes it worse. Here is what the owner surveys and the veterinary guidelines actually say. | 2026-10-17 · MeltPet · 13 min read

Last updated: 2026-10-17

The Short Version, Before the Evidence

Cats scratch for four reasons at once — to shed the old claw sheath, to stretch, to leave a visual mark, and to leave a scent mark — and none of those reasons can be trained out of an animal. So the goal is never to stop the behaviour. It is to move it onto something you own on purpose. Three changes do most of the work: put a sisal post at least 3 feet (0.9 m) tall where the cat is already scratching, stop punishing her for the rest of it, and stop carrying her to the post — a 2018 owner survey found that lifting a cat onto the post was linked to it being used less, not more.

If you have already bought a scratching post and watched your cat ignore it in favour of the sofa arm two metres away, you are in the majority, and the reason is almost never the cat. In the largest survey of cat scratching behaviour ever run — 4,331 owners reporting on 4,105 cats in 39 countries — researchers found a systematic mismatch between what owners provide and what cats use. Carpet was the substrate owners offered most often. Sisal rope was the one cats used most often when they had a choice.

That single mismatch explains a large share of the posts that get written off as useless. But substrate is only one of five things that decide whether a post gets used, and the other four are cheaper to change than a new post is to buy.

Four Jobs in One Behaviour

Scratching is not one thing a cat does for one reason. It is at least four functions running at the same time, which is why every attempt to simply suppress it fails or creates a new problem.

  • Claw maintenance. Scratching pulls off the dead outer sheath of the claw, which is how the claw stays sharp and how the mechanism that extends and retracts it stays in working order.
  • Stretching. A full scratch extends the forelimbs, the shoulders and the spine together. It is the feline equivalent of a long morning stretch, and it is why so much scratching happens right after a nap.
  • Visual marking. The shredded surface is itself the signal. It says a cat was here, and it stays legible for a long time.
  • Scent marking. The pads of the paws carry glands that deposit semiochemicals as the claws draw down. This is the part people miss, and it is the reason location matters so much.

The veterinary review of destructive scratching for the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery sets all four out together, and notes the detail that settles the argument about whether this is really about claws: declawed cats carry on performing the full scratching motion. The drive is not the claw. The claw is only the tool.

If you have ever watched your cat knead a blanket with her claws half out, you have seen the same set of instincts in a different context — kneading and scratching share the same nerve and gland anatomy, and both are expressions of a behaviour that is fundamentally about comfort and communication rather than damage.

Scent marking also explains why the target is never random. Cats scratch prominent objects near where they sleep and near the places other cats pass through — doorways, room entrances, the corner of the sofa that sits beside the hallway. In other words, exactly the objects you would least like them to choose. A cat is not being provocative; she is marking the most socially significant real estate in the house, because that is the whole point of a mark.

How Common It Actually Is

Before treating this as a behaviour problem, it is worth knowing how ordinary it is. A pilot survey of 116 cat owners published in 2018 found that 83.9% reported their cat scratching objects it should not. Furniture was the most common target (81.5% of those cats) followed by carpet (64.1%), and the scratching was more often directed at vertical surfaces than horizontal ones. If your cat is doing this, she is doing what four out of five cats in that sample did.

The stakes are real, though, and they are the reason this question gets taken seriously in veterinary behaviour rather than written off as a nuisance. Looking across shelter and clinic data in a clinical review of the problem, two figures stand out:

  • 12.4% — the share of cats relinquished to shelters where destruction of property was given as a reason.
  • 69–78% — the share of cases in which scratching is cited as the reason a cat is presented to a veterinarian for declawing.

Those are results about surrendered cats and about surgical decisions, not about your living room. But they explain why this subject attracts more heat than most cat behaviour topics: a scratch that nobody redirects is one of the few everyday cat behaviours that genuinely puts a cat's home at risk.

For scale on the other side, questionnaire work on client-reported feline behaviour problems puts scratching concerns at around 15% of cases — behind elimination problems and general destructiveness, both of which come up in about a quarter of cases. Scratching is common, consequential, and — this is the important part — almost entirely fixable with environmental changes rather than correction.

That is also why scratching sits in the same family of problems as a cat urinating outside the litter box. Both are normal behaviours being expressed in the wrong place, both are reliably worsened by punishment, and both are usually solved by giving the cat a resource that suits her rather than by discouraging the behaviour itself.

What Owners Buy and What Cats Use Are Two Different Lists

This is the most useful piece of research on the subject, because it compares the two lists directly. Owners were asked what scratching surfaces they provided, and separately what their cat actually used. The answers did not match.

Carpet was the most frequently offered substrate. Sisal rope was the most frequently used one whenever it was available. Cardboard, which most owners never think to offer, ranked third. The split by age is worth reading carefully, because there is a genuine reversal in it.

SubstrateCats up to 9 yearsCats 10–14 years
Sisal rope32.5%22.9%

For cats under ten, rope wins clearly. Among cats aged 10 to 14 the ordering flips and carpet is used most often. The authors of the review that summarised these numbers offer a distinctly unromantic explanation for the flip: it may reflect the substrates those cats were given as kittens rather than a change of preference with age — a cohort effect, in other words, rather than a senior-cat preference.

Which is a good reminder about how this kind of data should be used. It tells you what a large population did, not what your individual cat will do. Your cat is the sample size of one. Use the survey as a starting order of operations — sisal first, because it wins most often — and then let the cat tell you whether you were right.

The Post, Specified

The same body of survey work produced a description of the post cats actually use. It has five parts, and a startling number of posts sold in shops fail at least two of them.

  1. Substrate: sisal rope. Not loose-wound, not decorative — rope dense enough to offer real resistance as the claws drag down.
  2. Orientation: vertical. An upright post invites the full-body upward pull that the behaviour is built around.
  3. Height: at least 3 feet (0.9 m). This is the criterion most products fail. A post too short for the cat to reach up with straight forelegs cannot be used the way the behaviour requires.
  4. Two or more levels. Post-and-platform designs with a resting level were associated with the least inappropriate scratching reported by owners — 55.3% of that group reported a problem, against 62.6% of owners using every other type of post.
  5. Base width: 1 to 3 feet (0.3 to 0.9 m). Narrower bases were used more often than wider ones, which is counter-intuitive until you watch a cat check whether something is going to move before committing her weight to it.

Stability ties all of that together. A post that wobbles teaches the cat that the post is unreliable, and a cat who has learned that will go back to the thing that has never once moved: the sofa. If you buy nothing else this year, a heavy base matters more than a large one.

🛒 If you are replacing a post that never got used: look for a sisal-wrapped post at least 3 feet tall with two levels and a base you cannot tip with one finger. Ignore the carpeted kitten-starter posts entirely if your cat is already targeting the sofa arm — the height, not the pattern, is what is being rejected.

One caveat worth stating plainly, because it is where these specifications get over-applied: these are population-level tendencies from owner-reported surveys, not experimental preference trials. Cats vary, and there is a well-documented subgroup that prefers flat, horizontal scratching. That is what the next section is about.

Vertical or Horizontal: Read the Damage You Already Have

Before spending anything, go and look at what your cat is actually destroying, and at the angle of the marks. Cats who scratch upright surfaces and cats who rake flat ones are asking for different pieces of furniture, and the damage already tells you which one you own.

The university behaviour guidance behind this approach is unusually direct about it: match the substitute to the thing being scratched, rather than following a rule about which orientation is better. A horizontal scatcher given a beautiful tall post has been given a gift she has no use for.

Cardboard matters more than people expect here. If your cat is one of the many who will stand on a delivery box and shred it, you already know her substrate — and the cardboard box relationship that looks like an unrelated quirk is often the same behaviour finding a legal outlet. Flip or rotate a corrugated board once the flutes flatten out; a worn-out board is rejected for the same reason a wobbly post is.

Placement Beats Every Product Decision

If you take one thing from this article, take this: a perfect post in the wrong place loses to an imperfect post in the right one, every time. The 2016 survey found that inappropriate scratching remained common even in homes where a post was in the same room as the damage. Proximity is not the variable. Position relative to the cat's own routine is.

Because scratching is partly a scent and visual mark, cats choose targets the way they choose marking points: somewhere prominent, near where they rest, and near the routes other cats and people use. That gives you a practical rule for where the post goes.

  • Next to the thing being scratched. Not across the room, not in the hallway, not tucked beside the litter tray. If the sofa arm is the target, the post goes beside the sofa arm.
  • Beside sleeping spots. Cats scratch after waking up, because the stretch is part of getting up.
  • Near room entrances and the routes through the house. These are the marking points a cat cares about.
  • Somewhere she will walk past anyway. A post in a spare room is a post in a room she does not use.

This is the same principle that the veterinary environmental-needs guidelines build into their second pillar, which treats scratching areas as one of the cat's key environmental resources — food, water, toileting, scratching, play and rest — and requires that each of them be provided in multiples and separated from the others. Scratching is not decoration in that framework. It sits on the same list as food and water.

Once the post is in use, you can move it toward where you would prefer it. Do it slowly, in the direction of your eventual layout, and no more than about 3 inches (7.6 cm) a day. A post that relocates overnight is a new post as far as the cat is concerned, and the audit starts again.

The same logic governs the rest of the house's furniture arrangements, and it is worth reading alongside what happens to a cat's resources when a second cat arrives, because that is the situation in which placement stops being a preference and starts being a conflict.

The Advice That Backfires

Most guides on this subject tell you to pick your cat up and place her at the scratching post, so she learns what it is for. It is intuitive, it is repeated everywhere, and the survey data says it does the opposite.

In the 2018 pilot survey of 116 cat owners, owners were asked which strategies they had tried and how well each had worked. A wide range of methods was reported, both reward-based and punishment-based. Exactly one method showed a statistically significant association with the outcome, and it was in the wrong direction: cats whose owners physically picked them up and placed them at the post were significantly less likely to use it.

The explanation the authors offer is about sequencing. Scratching usually happens inside a behaviour chain — as part of waking up and stretching, or in the moment after some social tension between cats. A cat in the middle of that sequence is already moving toward a target. Being lifted interrupts the sequence, and in doing so it breaks the association between the post and whatever the cat was about to do there. What you have taught her is that the post is the place where a person grabs her.

So the instruction is the reverse of the folk wisdom. Do not carry the cat to the post. Put the post where the behaviour already happens, and let her arrive on her own feet. If you want to accelerate it, use scent and rewards — both of which are covered below — not your hands.

What Does Not Work, and Why

The same survey asked about punishment, and the list of methods owners reported is worth reading in full because it shows how much effort goes into approaches that were measured and found ineffective: yelling, clapping, spraying with water or air, shaking a rattle can, throwing things at the cat, and spanking. None of them was associated with a reduction in scratching.

There are two reasons this should not surprise anyone who has lived with a cat. The first is that scratching is not a discipline problem — there is no rule being broken from the cat's point of view, only a need being met at an inconvenient address. Punishing a need does not remove the need. The second is that punishment raises arousal. An animal in a heightened state of alertness marks more, not less, which is why households with an unresolved conflict between cats often see more scratching rather than less.

What does work as a temporary measure is changing the target rather than the behaviour. Double-sided sticky tape on a sofa arm, a sheet of aluminium foil, a plastic carpet runner laid pointy-side up, or an odour the cat dislikes applied to the object all make the current target less pleasant to use. Notice what these have in common: they act on the object, not on the cat. And notice the condition attached to every one of them — they only work if a better option is available nearby. Deterrents buy time while the post wins. Used alone, they simply move the problem to the next available surface.

The university guidance adds a warning worth passing on: do not use anything that could hurt the cat, and be careful with strong smells, because a scent applied heavily enough to matter can spread to the acceptable object you are trying to promote.

Declawing: What the Veterinary Bodies Actually Say

This is the section that most scratching articles handle with euphemism, so here is the plain version. In 2017 the American Association of Feline Practitioners issued a position statement strongly opposing declawing as an elective procedure. The statement's own framing is that declawing is the amputation of the third phalanx — the bone the claw grows from — and that scratching is a normal feline behaviour, both inherited and learned, which owners should be educated about rather than have surgically removed.

The American Veterinary Medical Association's policy, revised by its House of Delegates, is worded more cautiously but moves in the same direction. It discourages declawing as an elective procedure, supports non-surgical alternatives, and states that onychectomy is a surgical amputation which, if performed, requires multimodal perioperative pain management. Both bodies place the obligation on the veterinarian to counsel owners about the behaviour, the procedure and the alternatives first.

The risks the AAFP statement lists are worth knowing in full, because they are the reason the profession moved: acute pain and infection after surgery, nerve trauma, and longer-term complications including lameness, behavioural problems and chronic neuropathic pain. Those are not complications of a manicure. They are complications of removing a joint.

There is also the part that makes declawing a poor solution even on its own terms. Scratching does not stop after declawing, because the drive was never the claw. Declawed cats continue to perform the full motion, which is exactly what you would expect from a behaviour with a stretching and marking function. What changes is the damage, and the evidence base on what else changes is still thin — one review notes that the long-term effects on weight-bearing, chronic pain and musculoskeletal disease remain unknown, and that dedicated pain-assessment studies in declawed cats years after surgery are scarce.

The legal picture has shifted too. New York State banned elective declawing in 2019, following a series of city-level bans that began with West Hollywood in 2003, and several large veterinary hospital groups adopted policies restricting the procedure to medical necessity. None of that is an argument you need to make to your cat. It is context for why the advice you will get from a modern practice is overwhelmingly about posts and nail trims.

Reward, Don't Correct: What the Surveys Support

If punishment was the method that failed, positive reinforcement is the method that was measured working. Owners who rewarded their cat for scratching the preferred post — with praise, a treat, or play — reported an increase in that post being used. In the 2018 survey, which found no effective deterrent among the punishment methods, this remains the only training approach with survey support behind it.

Making the post smell right is the other lever, and here there is an actual controlled trial rather than a survey. A randomised, blinded crossover study published in 2013 tested a synthetic version of the semiochemical cats deposit from their interdigital glands — the scent that naturally accumulates on a scratched surface. Nineteen cats were tested twice each, with 38 test sessions in total, and the post treated with the semiochemical was scratched for longer and more often than the control post. Interestingly, the treated post did not get scratched sooner; what changed was how much the cat did once she started. The proposed application is exactly the problem this article is about: steering an existing scratching habit onto a chosen surface.

The cheap version of the same idea works for many cats: rub the post with catnip or silvervine, or attach a favourite toy to it for a few days so that it becomes part of the play circuit rather than a piece of furniture. If the household has ongoing tension between cats, or a recent change — a move, a new arrival, a new baby — then a pheromone diffuser in the main room is a reasonable adjunct while the environmental work is being done, because scratching increases when cats feel their territory is unsettled. 🛒 The Feliway diffuser is the version most vets suggest, and it works better as one part of a plan than as the plan itself.

Finally, finish play sessions on the post rather than away from it. Cats hunt in short bursts and end with a catch, and a wand toy teased up and around the post lets the post become the place where the sequence completes. It is the opposite of the carrying trick, and it uses the same insight about behaviour chains in the cat's favour.

Protecting the Furniture While the Training Works

Redirecting a scratching habit is not instant — it is a matter of weeks, not days — so it is worth knowing which measures reduce damage in the meantime without working against you.

  • Trim the claw tips about once a week. This is the most underrated item on the list. Taking off just the sharp point — carefully, staying well clear of the pink quick inside the nail — makes the same scratch mark much shallower, and it does not stop the cat scratching or distress her in any way.
  • Build up tolerance for paw handling first. Start by stroking the legs and paws while offering a treat, then progress to gentle squeezing so the claw extends. It is the same slow-accumulation approach used for any handling task, and it applies to cats at any age.
  • Consider nail caps if trimming is not happening. Soft plastic caps that fit over the claw are a humane alternative for owners who cannot clip claws regularly. They need replacing as the claw grows out, and they are an aid rather than a solution — the behavioural work still has to happen.
  • Use deterrents only on the target, and only temporarily. Sticky tape, foil, a plastic runner or a repelling odour on the object being scratched, with the post positioned right next to it.
  • Do not block the cat from the room entirely. Shutting her out of the living room removes the target and the territorial security at the same time, which tends to increase marking elsewhere.

If you have a kitten, the efficient moment to do all of this is now. Getting a cat used to claw handling and to a tall post at the same time, before any furniture habit is established, is far easier than reversing one — which is why a kitten setup list that omits a proper scratching post is a setup list with a predictable gap in it.

Multi-Cat Homes: The Same Problem, Different Arithmetic

Everything said above applies, and then the arithmetic changes, because in a house with more than one cat a resource is only a resource if it cannot be guarded. Two scratching posts pushed together against the same wall are one post with a spare, for the same reason that two litter trays side by side are one litter tray.

The environmental-needs framework is explicit about this: key resources — food, water, toileting areas, scratching areas, play areas and resting places — need to be present in multiples and separated from each other, so that no cat has to pass a potential challenger to use one. In a multi-cat home, that is the whole game, and it applies to scratching as much as to the litter tray.

Practically, that means:

  • More posts than cats, in different rooms rather than clustered.
  • Both orientations, since cats in the same house will have different preferences and the lower-ranking cat will rarely use the post the other one favours.
  • Posts along the routes cats travel — between the feeding area, the water and the litter trays — because those are the corridors where marking matters.
  • Watch for a change in pattern after any disruption. A new cat, a move, a renovation or a new baby commonly shows up first as new scratching, because marking increases when a cat's sense of territory is unsettled.

If you already have a house-soiling problem alongside the scratching, treat them as one issue rather than two. Both are what happens when a cat's key resources are not available in a way she can use without conflict, and the fix is the same audit of placement, count and separateness. Our breakdown of the nine causes of litter box avoidance, in diagnostic order, walks through that audit and is worth doing at the same time.

When Scratching Is a Symptom, Not a Habit

Most of this article assumes a stable pattern: the same surfaces, the same rooms, roughly the same amount of scratching, month after month. A change in that pattern is a different question, and it belongs with a veterinarian rather than a shopping trip.

Things that warrant a vet visit rather than a new post:

  • A sudden increase in scratching, especially with vocalising or pacing. Discomfort and anxiety both show up this way, and cats hide pain well enough that a behaviour change is often the first visible sign.
  • Scratching concentrated around the head, ears or neck, particularly with hair loss or scabbing. That pattern points toward skin or parasite problems rather than marking.
  • Over-grooming, bald patches, or chewing at the paws. Pain and stress in cats frequently surface as exaggerated grooming rather than obvious limping.
  • Urine marking on vertical surfaces at the same time. Scratching and urine marking are both territorial, and a cat doing both is telling you something about the household that a post will not fix.
  • An older cat who stops using the tall post she always used. Reduced mobility is worth ruling out, because a post that requires a full-body stretch is harder work than a flat mat. This is clinical reasoning rather than something the survey data establishes, and the survey's own explanation for the preference shift in cats aged 10 to 14 is that it reflects early-life exposure — but a change in ability is exactly the sort of thing that deserves a look rather than an assumption.

If a cat is unwell, unsettled, or in pain, no scratching post will fix it, because the scratching is not the problem. It is the report. The same principle applies to sudden bursts of night-time activity: the behaviour is real and worth understanding on its own terms, but a change from a stable baseline is a question for your vet, not a search engine.

A Seven-Day Starting Plan

If you want the short version in the order that actually reduces damage fastest:

  1. Days 1–2 — audit. Photograph every spot your cat scratches. Note whether the marks run up and down or forward and flat. This single step decides what you buy, and it costs nothing.
  2. Day 2 — count the resources. How many cats, how many posts, and are they spread out or clustered? If two posts are against the same wall, you have one post.
  3. Day 3 — reposition before you rebuy. Move the existing post next to the worst target. Most unused posts are not bad posts; they are posts in the wrong room.
  4. Day 4 — change the terms of the old target. Put a detention measure on the sofa arm — tape, foil or a runner — and stop any lifting, spraying or shouting, immediately and permanently.
  5. Days 5–7 — build the association. Reward with praise, a treat or a play session every time she uses the post, put catnip or a toy on it, and finish play sessions there.
  6. Week 2 onward — trim claws weekly, replace the post if it fails the height or stability test, and only start migrating it once she is using it consistently.

The reason this works when the correction-based approach does not is that it accepts the premise of the whole behaviour. A cat who scratches is not misbehaving; she is doing something she is built to do, in the only place available that satisfies the requirement. Give her somewhere better placed, correctly shaped and reliably stable, and the sofa stops being the best option. That is the entire trick, and there is no version of it that involves the word no.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my cat scratch the sofa and not the scratching post?

Usually because the post fails one of five criteria, and placement is the most common failure rather than the material. In the largest survey of cat scratching behaviour, owners frequently had a post in the same room as the damage and the scratching continued anyway, which rules out distance alone and points at position relative to the cat's routine. After placement, check height and stability: a post shorter than your cat's full stretch, or one that moves when she puts weight on it, cannot be used the way the behaviour requires. Sisal rope is the substrate cats used most often when it was offered, so if your post is carpet-covered and your cat is ignoring it, that is the third thing to change.

Does spraying water stop a cat scratching?

No, and it was measured. In a 2018 survey of 116 cat owners, owners reported spraying water or air, yelling, clapping, shaking a rattle can, throwing things and spanking, and none of those methods was associated with a reduction in scratching. The reason is twofold: scratching is a need being met rather than a rule being broken, so punishing it does not remove the need, and punishment raises arousal, which tends to increase marking rather than reduce it. Temporary deterrents on the object — sticky tape, foil, a plastic runner — do reduce use of a specific target, but only when a better option is available nearby.

Should I carry my cat to the scratching post?

No. This is the most widely repeated piece of advice on the subject, and it is the one thing in the survey data that significantly predicted the opposite of the intended result: cats whose owners physically picked them up and placed them at the post were less likely to use it. Scratching normally occurs inside a behaviour chain — on waking, during a stretch, or after social tension — and lifting the cat interrupts that sequence, breaking the association between the post and the behaviour. Put the post where the scratching already happens and let the cat arrive on her own.

What material do cats prefer for a scratching post?

Sisal rope, in most cases. In a survey of 4,331 owners reporting on 4,105 cats, sisal rope was the substrate used most often whenever it was available — 32.5% of cats up to nine years old, ahead of carpet at 25.1% and cardboard at 18.2%. The notable detail is that carpet was the substrate owners offered most often, which is a large part of why so many posts get ignored. There is one reversal: among cats aged 10 to 14, carpet was used most frequently, and the review authors suggest this may reflect the substrates those cats were given as kittens rather than a genuine change in preference with age.

How tall should a scratching post be?

At least 3 feet (0.9 m), so that your cat can reach up with her forelegs fully extended and pull down without the base tipping. The same survey evidence describes the post cats use most often as sisal rope, vertical, at least 3 feet tall, with two or more levels and a base 1 to 3 feet (0.3 to 0.9 m) wide — and narrower bases were used more often than wider ones, provided the post does not move under load. Stability matters as much as height: a post that shifts teaches the cat to prefer furniture, which never moves.

Does declawing stop a cat from scratching?

No, and that is one of the clearest findings in the literature. Declawed cats continue to perform the full scratching motion, because the behaviour is not driven by the claws — it combines claw maintenance, stretching, and visual and scent marking, and the scent comes from glands in the paw pads rather than the claw itself. On top of that, the American Association of Feline Practitioners strongly opposes declawing as an elective procedure and describes it as amputation of the third phalanx, while the American Veterinary Medical Association's policy discourages it as an elective procedure and supports non-surgical alternatives. The listed risks include infection, nerve trauma, and longer-term lameness, behavioural problems and chronic neuropathic pain.

Why has my cat suddenly started scratching more?

A change from a stable pattern is a different problem from a long-standing habit, and it is worth a veterinary visit. Common non-environmental causes include skin or parasite problems, especially when the scratching concentrates around the head, ears or neck, and pain or anxiety, which in cats often appears as a behaviour change rather than an obvious limp. Environmental causes are usually a disruption to the cat's sense of territory — a new cat, a move, a new baby, or building work — because scratching is partly a marking behaviour and marking increases when a cat feels less secure. If scratching has increased at the same time as urine marking on vertical surfaces, treat it as one territorial problem rather than two separate ones.
