🔇 How to Stop Excessive Barking

Without a shock collar. Without screaming. Without giving up. | 2026-06-14 · MeltPet · 5 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-14

The Neighbor Didn't Knock. She Left a Note.

When my friend Tom got a Sheltie —a Shetland Sheepdog, a breed literally created to bark at things —he lived in a duplex with paper-thin walls. The dog, a blue merle female named Ziggy, had exactly one volume setting. She barked at the mail carrier. She barked at squirrels. She barked at a plastic bag blowing across the street. She barked at the sound of Tom's phone vibrating on the coffee table.

Three weeks in, Tom found a handwritten note taped to his front door. It was polite. The subtext was "make the dog stop or I'm calling the landlord." Tom's first instinct was to yell at Ziggy to be quiet. That made her bark harder. Then he bought a citronella collar. Ziggy learned to bark, take the spray, and keep barking. Then he asked me for help.

The thing about barking that most training advice skips: barking is not one behavior. It's a symptom with at least four different root causes, and if you treat the wrong one, nothing gets better. Let's go through the categories, figure out which kind of barker you have, and what to do about it.

Type 1: The Window Sentinel

This dog barks at things he sees: the mailman, a dog walking past, a kid on a bike, a squirrel performing a heist on the bird feeder. Every bark is a report. "Something moved. I saw it. Are you seeing this?"

Shelties, terriers, and a lot of herding breeds fall into this bucket —they were bred to notice things and announce them. The modern suburban version of this job is staring out the front window and barking at the delivery truck.

The fix that actually works is a combination of two things. First: block the view. Frosted window film on the lower half of the window. A curtain. Moving the couch so the dog can't perch on the back of it and surveil the street. This alone cuts the barking triggers by 70–90% for most window sentinels. Second: teach a "thank you, I've got it" routine. When the dog barks, you walk to the window, look out, say "thank you" in a calm voice, and then call the dog away with a treat. Over time, the dog learns that one or two barks gets the human to check —and then the barking stops because the job is done. This technique comes from trainer Jean Donaldson and it's vastly more effective than trying to eliminate the alert entirely. Shelties gonna Sheltie. Give them a controlled version of the job instead of trying to delete it.

Type 2: The Bored Brawler

This dog barks because he has nothing else to do. He's been in the backyard for three hours. Or he's been in the apartment alone since 8 AM. The barking is self-reinforcing —it releases energy, it makes noise, it breaks the monotony. A bored dog in a yard will bark at a fence post. Not because the fence post is threatening. Because barking at the fence post is more interesting than lying on the patio doing nothing.

The fix here is not a training protocol. It's giving the dog a life. More exercise —not just a walk around the block, but running, fetch, off-leash time if you have access to a safe area. Mental exercise too: puzzle toys, frozen Kongs, scent work games, 10-minute training sessions before you leave for work. A dog whose brain and body are both tired doesn't stand at the fence barking at nothing. He sleeps. If your dog is barking from boredom, you're not going to train it out of him. You're going to exercise it out of him.

Type 3: The Fear Barker

This one looks different. Fear barking is higher-pitched, more frantic, often accompanied by backing away, tucked tail, whale eye (showing the whites of the eyes). The dog is not being protective or territorial. He's scared. Strangers approaching, other dogs getting too close, novel objects where they shouldn't be —the bark is saying "stay away."

Punishing a fear barker makes it worse. You're confirming that the scary thing is indeed scary —when it appears, bad things happen. Instead, you need desensitization and counterconditioning: pairing the scary thing with something good, at a distance where the dog notices but doesn't react. This is slow work. It takes weeks to months. A certified positive-reinforcement trainer or veterinary behaviorist is worth the money for fear-based barking. Don't DIY serious fear unless you've got experience. You can make it worse without meaning to.

Type 4: The Demand Barker

This dog barks at you. At dinner time. When you're holding a tennis ball. When you're on the couch and he wants up. The bark is directed at you, not at the window, and it usually comes with direct eye contact and a body posture that says "I am asking for something and I expect you to produce it."

Demand barking exists because it works. At some point, the dog barked, you gave him what he wanted to make him stop, and he filed that away as a successful negotiation tactic. The fix is uncomfortable: you have to stop rewarding it. Completely. For weeks. If the dog barks for dinner, you put the food bowl down and walk away until he's quiet for five seconds. Then you feed him. If he barks at you on the couch, you stand up and leave the room. The message has to be: barking makes the good thing go away. Silence brings it back. The first week is painful —the barking often gets worse before it gets better, because the dog is thinking "hey, this used to work, let me try HARDER." This is called an extinction burst and it's normal. Push through it. If you give in during the extinction burst, you've taught the dog that barking louder and longer is the winning move.

Ziggy, Six Weeks Later

Tom put frosted film on the front window. He started running Ziggy for 30 minutes every morning instead of a 10-minute walk. He taught "thank you, I'll check" —two barks, Tom walks to the window, looks out, says "thanks," Ziggy gets a piece of cheese. After two weeks, Ziggy was giving a single "woof" and then looking at Tom like "well? go check." The neighbor stopped calling. Ziggy still barks at plastic bags. She's a Sheltie. But she no longer barks for four hours straight at the window, and Tom didn't have to hurt her to get there.
