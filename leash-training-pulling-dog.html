🦮 Leash Training: Stop the Pulling

No prong collars. No 'be the alpha.' Just training that works. | 2026-06-20 · MeltPet · 5 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-03

My Arm Hurt After Every Walk. The Dog Weighed 35 Pounds.

The problem with leash pulling isn't that it's hard to fix. It's that most of the advice is either ineffective or unnecessarily harsh. The ineffective advice: "just stop walking when he pulls." That works in a training video where the dog is a Labrador who's already vaguely leash-trained. It doesn't work when your 70-pound rescue is lunging at a squirrel and you're holding on with one hand and trying not to drop your phone with the other.

The unnecessarily harsh advice: prong collars, choke chains, "correct him firmly so he knows who's in charge." These tools work by causing pain. A prong collar pinches the skin of the neck when the leash goes tight. A choke chain does exactly what it sounds like. Dogs stop pulling because pulling hurts. The problem is that these tools don't teach the dog what you actually want —they just punish the thing you don't want, and they damage the relationship you're trying to build. They also cause tracheal damage, increased intraocular pressure, and in some cases, behavioral fallout —the dog becomes fearful or aggressive because walks have become a painful, confusing experience.

There are two methods that actually work without hurting the dog. They're not secrets. They're used by every certified positive-reinforcement trainer. They just take consistency and a week of looking slightly ridiculous on your street.

Method 1: The Tree (For Dogs Who Pull Because They Want to Go Forward)

This is the simplest leash training method in existence and it works on about 70% of pullers. The rule: tension on the leash means you stop moving. Completely. You become a tree. You don't yank back. You don't say "no." You don't make eye contact. You just stop. The instant the leash goes slack —because the dog turned around, shifted weight, looked back at you —you mark it ("yes" or a click) and reward with a treat at your side and keep walking.

Day 1 of this method: you will not make it to the end of your driveway in under 10 minutes. That's normal. You are teaching a dog who has pulled for months or years that pulling no longer produces forward motion. The dog will be confused. She will try pulling harder because pulling used to work. Do not move when the leash is tight. Wait. The moment the leash goes even slightly slack, reward and walk. The dog is learning: tension = we stop. Loose leash = we go. Plus treats.

By day 3 or 4, most dogs figure out the pattern. By day 7, you should be able to walk a few blocks with minimal pulling. The key is consistency. Every single time the leash goes tight, you stop. If you let pulling work even occasionally, you've taught the dog that pulling is a slot machine —it pays off randomly, so it's worth trying forever.

Equipment note: do this on a standard 6-foot leash attached to a back-clip harness or a flat collar. Retractable leashes are incompatible with this method —they maintain constant tension, which teaches the dog that a tight leash is normal and has been linked to serious injuries for both dogs and owners. If you're using a retractable leash, retire it for the training period —a standard 6-foot leash is all you need. If you're using a retractable leash, retire it for the training period.

Method 2: The U-Turn (For Dogs Who Are Over Threshold Before You Leave the House)

Some dogs can't learn the Tree method because they never produce a loose leash. They're pulling at 100% tension from the moment you step outside. These dogs are over-aroused —the environment is so stimulating that they can't think. For these dogs, you need to redirect rather than stop.

The U-turn method: walk in one direction. The moment the dog hits the end of the leash, say "this way" in a cheerful voice, and turn 180 degrees and walk the other direction. The dog gets to the end of the leash again —turn again. You'll walk back and forth on the same 20-foot stretch of sidewalk for the first few sessions. The dog learns that pulling doesn't just stop forward motion —it redirects the entire walk in a direction she didn't choose. More importantly, she learns that paying attention to you predicts where you're going. A dog who's paying attention to you doesn't pull. A dog who's hyper-focused on the squirrel ahead of her does.

Reward heavily when the dog voluntarily checks in —looks back at you, walks beside you for even two steps. Those check-ins are the behavior you want to build. The U-turn method is more active than the Tree, which makes it better for high-energy dogs who get frustrated by stopping. It's also better for reactive dogs —if your dog pulls toward other dogs, you need a method that keeps her moving and thinking rather than standing still and fixating.

The Gear That Helps

A front-clip harness is the best tool for leash training. The leash attaches at the chest, not the back. When the dog pulls, the front attachment turns her body sideways toward you rather than letting her lean into the pull like a sled dog. It doesn't cause pain. It doesn't restrict breathing. It just makes pulling mechanically ineffective. The Freedom No-Pull Harness and the Balance Harness are both well-designed. Head halters (Gentle Leader, Halti) work on the same principle —control the head, the body follows —but most dogs need a desensitization period before they'll tolerate something on their face.

What not to use: a back-clip harness on a pulling dog. The back attachment point gives the dog maximum leverage to lean into the pull. It's good for trained dogs who already walk nicely. It's counterproductive for training.

🛒 The right gear, first time: The Freedom No-Pull Harness has both front and back clips and a velvet-lined underside that prevents chafing —it's the one most professional trainers use. The Balance Harness is a lighter alternative with better sizing for odd-shaped dogs (deep-chested breeds, short-legged breeds). Both sit in the same mid-range bracket. If you go the head halter route, the Gentle Leader is the standard —budget a week of desensitization (letting the dog sniff it, putting it on for 10 seconds with treats, building up slowly) before you clip a leash to it. Buy from Chewy or Amazon —local pet stores sometimes only carry back-clip harnesses, which won't help.
