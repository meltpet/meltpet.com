🦁 Maine Coon vs 🐱 Ragdoll

22 pounds of lynx energy, or a soft shadow that goes limp in your arms? Same size class, completely different roommates. | 2026-08-11 · MeltPet · 9 min read

Last updated: 2026-08-11

Two Giants, Zero Similarities Beyond the Size Chart

Here's the thing nobody tells you about big cats: "big" isn't a personality. A Maine Coon and a Ragdoll can both top twenty pounds, and they could not be less alike once they're in your house.

I tracked a Maine Coon named Fergus for the breed guide —22 pounds of fur, chirps, and the confidence of a creature that knows it could take you in a fight. He doesn't go limp when you hold him. He tolerates you holding him, the way a king tolerates a ceremonial scepter. Meanwhile the Ragdoll down the street is a different species of cat entirely: pick her up and she melts, boneless, trusting you with her life.

Same size class. Different roommates. This is which one is better for you —not which is "better," because that question doesn't have an answer.

Temperament: A King vs. A Shadow

The fastest way to tell these two breeds apart isn't the fur —it's what happens when you walk into the room after a long day.

A Maine Coon will look up from whatever he's doing —usually something he shouldn't be doing, like opening a cabinet —acknowledge your arrival with a chirp, and go back to supervising. He's affectionate on his terms. He'll follow you into the bathroom not because he's anxious, but because he has opinions about your water usage and wants to be consulted. Maine Coons are famously described as dog-like, and it's accurate: they're loyal, they greet you at the door, they can even be taught to fetch. But they keep their dignity. A Maine Coon does not need you; he has decided to allow you.

A Ragdoll is the opposite. Ragdolls were bred for exactly one thing: human contact. The breed's signature move —going completely limp when you pick them up —isn't a trick, it's a personality trait that breeders selected for over decades. Your Ragdoll will follow you from room to room, sit on your keyboard while you work, and sulk audibly when you close the bathroom door. This is a cat that needs company the way a dog does. Leave one alone for ten hours a day, and you'll come home to over-grooming, litter box protests, or a cat that greets you with genuine distress.

If you want a cat that treats you like family, both work. If you want a cat that treats you like its whole world, that's the Ragdoll. If you want a cat that respects your personal space but still checks on you, that's the Maine Coon.

Grooming: The Fur Math Nobody Puts in the Ad

Both breeds are longhaired. The difference is how the fur is built.

The Maine Coon has a double coat: a soft, dense undercoat for insulation plus long waterproof guard hairs on top. It's magnificent. It also means that twice a year —spring and fall —your house will be colonized by fur tumbleweeds that drift across the floor like tumbleweed in a western. Weekly brushing keeps a Maine Coon manageable, but during coat-blowing season you'll want two or three passes a week, and you should budget for a good vacuum. The undercoat is what mats; if you skip brushing for a month, you're cutting mats out with scissors.

The Ragdoll coat is the cheat code: silky long guard hairs with almost no undercoat. That single difference means they shed noticeably less, mat far less, and need about a third of the grooming time. A weekly pass with a metal comb keeps a Ragdoll looking like a show cat. They still mat in the usual trouble spots —behind the ears, in the armpits, along the britches —but the mats are easier to work out before they become permanent.

Neither breed is hypoallergenic. But if grooming time is your real constraint —and for most owners it should be —the Ragdoll wins this category without breaking a sweat.

Health: They Share the Same Heart Problem

Here's the uncomfortable part of comparing these two breeds: they both carry a genetic form of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) —the most common heart disease in cats, where the heart muscle thickens and eventually fails. They even share the same gene mutation (MYBPC3). A DNA test exists, but it doesn't catch every case, which is why responsible breeders of both breeds run annual cardiac ultrasounds on their breeding cats and will show you the parents' results. Anyone who won't show you cardiac screening is the red flag, not the bargain.

Beyond HCM, the breeds diverge:

  • Maine Coon: hip dysplasia (those big bodies stress the joints), and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a degenerative muscle disease that breeders can now test for. The health-tested kittens from a breeder with documented cardiac ultrasound, hip scores, and genetic screening typically cost $1,500–3,000. The "cheap" kitten costs $500–1,500 —and the HCM diagnosis at age four runs $3,000–8,000 in cardiology visits and medications.
  • Ragdoll: bladder stones —specifically calcium oxalate stones, which can't be dissolved with diet and often require surgical removal ($1,500–3,000). Polycystic kidney disease appears less often than in Persians, but it does appear. A Ragdoll from health-tested lines runs $1,200–2,500.

The pattern here is the same for both breeds: the upfront price is the smallest number in the equation. Screened parents cost more at purchase and almost always cost less over the cat's life. Run the math on the monthly cost calculator with a big-cat food budget before you commit —a 20-pound cat eats like a small dog.

Living Situations: Apartment vs. Alone Time

Both breeds adapt to apartments better than most cats, but for different reasons and with different caveats.

The Maine Coon is fine in an apartment as long as you can handle the fur and give him vertical space —a tall cat tree, a window perch, shelves. He's not destructive when entertained, and he has enough self-respect not to need you every hour. If you work long hours, a Maine Coon copes.

The Ragdoll is also an apartment cat, but with a catch that deserves capital letters: Ragdolls hate being alone. Their trusting, non-aggressive temperament makes them genuinely bad at defending themselves outdoors —most breeders require indoor-only homes —and their need for company means a Ragdoll left solo for ten-plus hours will develop stress behaviors. If you're gone long days and you want a Ragdoll, the honest answer is to get two (bonded siblings are ideal). If you can't do two cats, that's a point for the Maine Coon.

One more thing about the Maine Coon outdoors: he's big, but that doesn't make him street-smart. Both breeds are safer as indoor cats. The difference is the Ragdoll will tell you he's unhappy about being an only cat, and the Maine Coon will just chirp at you from the window and get on with his day.

The Real Cost Difference (It's Not Just the Purchase Price)

Maine Coons cost more upfront, but the ongoing math is what most people miss.

  • Food: A 20-pound Maine Coon eats more than a 15-pound Ragdoll. Premium kibble for a big cat runs roughly $40–60/month; expect the Maine Coon on the higher end. The cat calorie calculator will give you the actual portion math for your cat's weight.
  • Grooming: If you groom at home, both are cheap —a comb and patience. If you plan to pay a professional for a longhaired cat, the Maine Coon's double coat will cost more per session and need more sessions.
  • Insurance: This is where breed-specific risk shows up. HCM is in both breeds, which means both are candidates for pet insurance —a $3,000–8,000 heart diagnosis is exactly the scenario insurance exists for. A young Maine Coon or Ragdoll typically costs $20–40/month for a decent accident-and-illness policy. The math on insurance vs. savings changes completely once a breed has a known genetic risk.
  • Vet baseline: Routine care is roughly the same for both —annual exams, vaccines, preventives. The divergence is the emergency and specialist line, which is where the breed's genetic risks show up.

Over a 12–15 year lifespan, the honest estimate for either breed is $12,000–20,000 in routine care plus whatever the health lottery adds. The Maine Coon's higher purchase price is real, but it's not the deciding number —the health screening is.

The Bottom Line: Which One Is Actually Right for You?

Stop looking at size charts. Ask yourself these four questions instead:

  • Do you want a cat that drapes over you like a blanket? → Ragdoll. That's the breed's whole job.
  • Do you want a cat that's more like a quiet dog —loyal, independent, chirpy? → Maine Coon.
  • Are you away from home 8+ hours a day? → Get the Maine Coon, or get two Ragdolls. A solo Ragdoll in an empty apartment is a sad cat.
  • Can you commit to brushing 1–3 times a week for the next 15 years? → If the honest answer is "no," get a Ragdoll. If it's "yes," either breed works.

The pattern behind every honest comparison of these two breeds is the same: the Maine Coon is a cat that lets you into his world. The Ragdoll is a cat that moves into yours. Both are extraordinary. They're just not the same roommate.
