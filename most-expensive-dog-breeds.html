💸 Most Expensive Dog Breeds Ranked by What They Cost to Keep, Not What They Cost to Buy

Nine breeds with a published breakdown, ranked by real monthly cost. | 2026-09-30 · MeltPet · 10 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-30

How to read this page: every monthly figure below is lifted from a cost breakdown MeltPet has already published on that breed's own guide — none of it is estimated for this article. The ranges are 2026 US national averages. Annual figures are the year totals published on each breed's own guide — for the French Bulldog that is the sum of its itemised lines, and dividing it by twelve is where the monthly column comes from, so the two columns agree to within a dollar. Emergencies are excluded and discussed separately, because a single one can cost more than several years of everything else combined.

The Number Every "Most Expensive Breeds" List Gets Wrong

Search for the most expensive dog breeds and you will get a list ranked by purchase price. A Tibetan Mastiff, a French Bulldog, a Chow Chow, a Samoyed. It is a satisfying list to write and almost useless to read, because purchase price is the smallest number in the equation.

Put two dogs side by side. One costs $3,000 to buy and $900 a year to keep. The other costs $1,000 to buy and $4,000 a year to keep. Over ten years the first dog costs $12,000 and the second costs $41,000. The expensive dog was the cheap one, and nothing in the purchase price suggested it.

That gap is not hypothetical. A Golden Retriever costs $1,000 to $3,500 from a health-tested breeder — or $200 to $500 from a breed rescue — and then $2,220 to $4,260 a year, every year, for ten to twelve years. The purchase price is overtaken before the second year is out, and after that it never appears again. The French Bulldog is starker still: the breed guide is blunt that the purchase price, whether $2,000 or $8,000, is the smallest number on the page.

So this page ranks the other way. It uses monthly cost, from breeds where we have actually published an itemised breakdown — not a size-band average, not a national survey figure applied to a breed it was never measured on.

How This Ranking Is Built

Three rules, so you know exactly what you are looking at.

Only breeds with a published breakdown. Nine breeds on this site have a full month-by-month cost table: all three Poodle sizes, the Golden Retriever, the Labrador Retriever, the French Bulldog, the Dachshund, the Sphynx and the Bengal. Those nine are ranked. Every other breed is left out rather than given a made-up number, which is why this list is shorter than the ones you will find elsewhere.

Sorted by the midpoint of the range. A range of $215–$370 and a range of $185–$355 overlap heavily. Sorting by midpoint is a defensible tie-breaker; sorting by the top of the range just rewards whichever breed has the widest uncertainty. Where two breeds land within a few dollars of each other, treat them as tied — the last three entries in this table are effectively one group.

Insurance included, emergencies excluded. The monthly totals include pet insurance, preventives, food, grooming and routine vet care. They exclude the one-off events that are the real financial risk, and those get their own section below.

Why Grooming Beats Food at the Top of This Table

Most people assume a big dog costs more because it eats more. For the Standard Poodle, that is wrong, and it is wrong by a wide margin.

Food for a Standard Poodle runs $45–$80 a month. Grooming runs $80–$140. The coat is the expense, and it is not a one-off — a Poodle's hair grows continuously the way yours does, which is exactly why the breed does not shed and exactly why it needs a groomer every five weeks, forever. The breed guide puts thirteen years of professional grooming at $12,500–$21,800, which is more than most people spend on a car and roughly the purchase price of several dogs.

This is the part of the ranking that is genuinely actionable. If a Standard Poodle is otherwise the right dog for you, learning to do the maintenance yourself between professional appointments does not remove the cost, but it changes the frequency. The breed sits at the top of this table because of a decision you can partly make, not because of a genetic lottery you cannot.

Now compare that to the French Bulldog at number four. Its premium is allergy treatment at $50–$150 a month, and there is no equivalent lever. You can shop around for a groomer. You cannot decide your dog will not have atopic dermatitis.

What the Monthly Number Leaves Out

Everything above is a routine year. The one-off events are where breeds actually separate, and they are large enough that a single one can cost more than several years of routine care.

The breed guide is blunt about the consequence: many insurers treat IVDD as pre-existing in a chondrodystrophic breed, so the policy that looks affordable may specifically exclude the thing you are insuring against. This is the real shape of dog costs. A cheap routine year is not the same as a cheap breed. It is a cheap routine year attached to a potentially expensive bad one — and insurance is the only mechanism that converts the second into the first. The Three Line Items That Actually Move the Number

Across all nine breeds, the same three lines do almost all of the work. If you are budgeting for a breed that is not on this list, these are the ones to get right.

1. Grooming frequency, not grooming price. A breed that needs a groomer every five weeks and one that needs one every eight weeks can look similar on a price list and differ by hundreds of dollars a year. Ask how often, then multiply by twelve over twelve years. That is the number.

2. Insurance, which is priced by breed. The Golden Retriever's insurance line runs $35–$50 a month, the Dachshund's $28–60, and the French Bulldog's $480–$960 a year — the highest band of any breed on this page. Insurers know the breed-level risk data better than any blog does, and the premium is that knowledge priced. If a breed's premium looks strangely high, that is the market telling you something.

3. Breed-specific gear and environment. This is the line people forget entirely because it does not exist for a mixed-breed dog of average proportions. Dachshund ramps at $8–15 a month amortised. Sphynx sweaters and the incremental heating cost of keeping a house at 73°F instead of 68°F, at $10–25 a month. A Bengal's puzzle feeders and cat trees at $20–40 a month, because a bored Bengal does not nap, it dismantles. These are not luxuries. They are the cost of owning that specific animal rather than a generic one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most expensive dog breed to own?

Of the nine breeds with a published, itemised monthly breakdown on this site, the Standard Poodle is the most expensive at $215–$370 per month ($2,580–$4,440 a year). The driver is not food, which runs $45–$80 a month. It is grooming: a Poodle in full coat needs a groomer every five weeks, and that line alone is $80–$140 a month.

Is purchase price a good way to judge how expensive a breed is?

No, and it is the single most misleading number in the category. A Golden Retriever costs $1,000–$3,500 from a health-tested breeder, or $200–$500 from a breed rescue, and then $2,220–$4,260 a year to keep — so the purchase price is overtaken inside the first two years. A dog that costs $3,000 to buy and $900 a year to keep is cheaper over ten years than a dog that costs $1,000 to buy and $4,000 a year to keep. Purchase price tells you what a breeder charges. It does not tell you what the dog costs.

Why do Poodles and Goldens cost more than other breeds?

For different reasons, which is the point. The Standard Poodle's premium is almost entirely grooming — a coat that grows continuously and needs professional attention every five weeks. The Golden Retriever's premium is spread across food and insurance: a large-breed dog with a genuine breed-level cancer risk pays more in premiums and eats more per meal. Same total at the top of the table, two completely different causes, and two completely different things you can control.

What is not included in these monthly figures?

Emergencies, and this matters more than any other caveat on the page. The monthly totals include routine care, preventives, insurance and grooming. They exclude one-off events, and a single event can cost more than several years of routine care. IVDD surgery in a Dachshund runs $7,000–$12,000. BOAS surgery in a French Bulldog runs $2,500–$5,000, and IVDD another $5,000–$12,000. A cruciate ligament repair in a large breed runs $2,500–$5,000 per knee, rising to $4,000–$6,000 once pre-operative imaging, hospitalisation and eight weeks of rechecks are counted in. This is exactly what insurance exists to absorb, and it is why the breeds that look cheapest monthly are not always the cheapest breeds.

Which dog breed is the cheapest to own?

Of the breeds with a published breakdown, the Dachshund has the lowest monthly total at $79–$195. That number is real, but it comes with a caveat that belongs in the same sentence: roughly one in four Dachshunds develops IVDD, and the surgery that treats it runs $7,000–$12,000. A low monthly number paired with a high single-event risk is not the same as a cheap breed. It is a cheap routine year attached to an expensive bad year.

Does insurance change the ranking?

It changes the numbers but not the order. The figures in this table already include a typical insurance line for each breed, because that is how our breed guides publish them. It is worth knowing that the premium itself is priced by breed: the Golden Retriever's insurance line is $35–$50 a month, the Dachshund's is $28–60, and the French Bulldog's runs $480–$960 a year — and in the Dachshund's case, many policies exclude the spinal condition that makes the breed risky in the first place. Always read the exclusion list before you pay.
