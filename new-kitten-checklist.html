🐱 New Kitten Checklist

Everything You Actually Need —Week One Edition | 2026-06-22 · MeltPet · 8 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-14

They're Smaller Than You Think. And Faster.

I've helped hundreds of people through their first weeks of pet ownership. Dog people prepare. They buy crates and beds and training books six weeks before the puppy arrives. Cat people? Cat people often show up at the shelter on a Saturday, fall in love with a six-week-old fluffball, and drive home with nothing but a cardboard carrier and a can of food the shelter handed them. I've done it myself. It's not ideal.

Kittens are small enough to fit in your palm, fast enough to vanish under a couch in half a second, and curious enough to explore every square inch of your home —including the gap behind the refrigerator and the inside of your box spring. They need more gear than you think, and the first week sets the tone for the next 15–20 years of your relationship. Let's do this right.

🏠 Kitten-Proofing: They're Looking for Trouble

I've seen a kitten climb inside a recliner mechanism. I've seen one eat six inches of ribbon and need $3,000 surgery. I've seen one wedge itself behind a dishwasher and refuse to come out for four hours. Kittens are tiny chaos agents with no self-preservation instinct. Here's what to fix before they arrive:

  • Plants. Lilies are lethally toxic to cats —even the pollen. Pothos, philodendron, dieffenbachia, and poinsettias cause oral irritation, vomiting, and worse. Check every plant in your home against the ASPCA's toxic plant list. When in doubt, it goes outside or gets hung from the ceiling where the cat can't reach it. And kittens can reach anywhere.
  • Cords and strings. Dangling blind cords: cut the loop or tie them up high. Charging cables: unplug when not in use or run through cable management sleeves. Dental floss, sewing thread, ribbon, rubber bands, hair ties: locked in a drawer. String-like objects are the #1 cause of linear foreign body surgery in cats. The intestine accordions up the string and perforates. It's a $2,000–$5,000 surgery and it's entirely preventable.
  • Small gaps. Behind the refrigerator. Under kitchen cabinets (the toe-kick gap). Behind the washer/dryer. Inside reclining chairs. Under dressers with a 2-inch gap. Block these or accept that you'll be fishing a kitten out of them at 2 AM. A rolled-up towel works for most gaps.
  • Windows and screens. Screens that pop out with light pressure need to be secured or the window stays closed. Kittens press their full body weight against screens chasing birds. A second-story fall can break a kitten's jaw or pelvis.
  • Cleaning supplies. Move them to a cabinet with a childproof latch. Cats walk on counters. They'll step in a spill you didn't notice and lick it off their paws. Pine-based cleaners (Pine-Sol) are especially toxic to cats.
  • Medications. One acetaminophen (Tylenol) tablet kills a cat. One ibuprofen causes kidney failure. All human medications go in a latched cabinet. Pill bottles on nightstands are not safe.
  • Toilet lids. Keep them down. Kittens can fall in and drown in an open toilet. It happens more often than people admit.
  • Trash cans. Kitchen trash goes in a cabinet or gets a lid the cat can't open. Chicken bones, string from roasts, foil with meat juices —all of it is dangerous and all of it is irresistible to a kitten.

⏱️ The First 48 Hours: A Timeline

You've brought the kitten home. The carrier is on the floor. Now what? Here's the hour-by-hour breakdown, from someone who has done this more times than I can count:

Hour 0–1: The Safe Room

Set up one room —a bathroom, spare bedroom, or home office —as the kitten's base camp. This room has: litter box in one corner, food and water in the opposite corner (cats won't eat near their bathroom —nobody wants to), bed, scratching post, and a couple of toys. Close the door. Open the carrier and let the kitten walk out on its own. Don't pull it out. Don't hover. Sit on the floor at the kitten's level, maybe 3–4 feet from the carrier, and wait. Read a book. Scroll your phone quietly. Let the kitten decide when to approach.

If the kitten hides —behind the toilet, under a shelf, inside the carrier —that's completely normal. Leave it alone. Put food and water nearby. It'll come out when it's ready. A kitten that hides for the first 4–6 hours is not broken. It's being a cat.

Hour 2–3: First Contact

Once the kitten has explored the room a bit —sniffed the perimeter, located the litter box, maybe eaten a few bites —you can try gentle interaction. Extend one finger toward the kitten's nose. Let it sniff. If it rubs against your finger, pet gently along the cheeks and behind the ears. Avoid the belly, tail, and paws for now. Keep sessions short —5–10 minutes —then let the kitten decompress. Overhandling on day one creates stress that's hard to undo.

Place the kitten gently in the litter box so it knows where it is. Don't be surprised if the kitten doesn't use it immediately. Show it again after meals and after naps. Most kittens figure it out instinctually, but that instinct needs a reminder of where "here" is.

Hour 6–48: First Night

Kittens are crepuscular —most active at dawn and dusk. Your kitten may cry at night. This is normal. It was sleeping in a pile of littermates 24 hours ago and now it's alone. A few things that help: a warm water bottle wrapped in a towel (mimics littermate body heat), a stuffed animal roughly kitten-sized, and a shirt you've worn all day in the bed. Your smell is comfort.

Do not put the kitten in your bed unless you're prepared for it to be in your bed for the next 15 years. Once that habit forms, it's permanent. If that's what you want, fine. If not, the kitten sleeps in its safe room.

Day 2: Routine Begins

By day two, the kitten should be eating, drinking, and using the litter box with some confidence. Now is when the real kitten personality shows up: the 6 AM sprint across your pillow, the attack on your charging cable, the discovery that curtains are climbable.

Start handling paws, ears, and mouth gently and briefly —10 seconds at a time, followed by a treat. This pays off enormously for future vet visits and nail trims. A cat that's been paw-handled since week one will let you clip its nails without a fight. A cat that hasn't will shred your arms at the vet.

This is a general timeline. Your vet will adjust based on the kitten's age at adoption, health status, and whether it's indoor-only or indoor-outdoor. Write down the dates. Missing a booster means restarting the series.

Keep the kitten strictly indoors until at least 2 weeks after the final FVRCP booster. Their immune system isn't fully online before then. One encounter with an unvaccinated outdoor cat can end badly.

🍽️Feeding a Kitten: The Rules That Actually Matter

Kitten nutrition isn't complicated, but the consequences of getting it wrong are permanent. A malnourished kitten grows into an adult cat with lifelong skeletal and immune problems. Here's what matters:

  • Kitten food only. Food must say "for growth," "kitten," or "all life stages" on the label. Adult maintenance food does not have enough protein, fat, or calories for a growing kitten. The difference is significant —kitten food has roughly 30–50% more calories per cup than adult food.
  • Frequency: Under 6 months = 3–4 small meals per day. 6–12 months = 3 meals. Over 12 months = 2 meals. Tiny stomachs, massive energy demands. Free-feeding dry food is convenient but makes it hard to monitor intake and contributes to adult obesity.
  • Wet vs. dry: Wet food is better for hydration. Cats have a low thirst drive —they evolved from desert animals and get most of their water from prey. A dry-food-only kitten drinks more than an adult on the same diet but still may not drink enough. Wet food solves this. If you feed primarily dry, add water or broth to it and make sure fresh water bowls are in multiple locations.
  • Switching foods: If you're changing from what the kitten was eating before, do it over 7–10 days. Day 1–3: 75% old food, 25% new. Day 4–6: 50/50. Day 7–9: 25% old, 75% new. Day 10: 100% new. A sudden switch causes diarrhea, and a dehydrated kitten is an emergency.
  • What not to feed: Milk (most cats are lactose intolerant), raw fish (destroys thiamine), onions/garlic/chives (toxic, damages red blood cells), chocolate, grapes/raisins, xylitol, alcohol, caffeine, raw dough. The safest rule: if it's not cat food, don't give it to the cat.

🐱 Socialization: The Window You Can't Get Back

The critical socialization window for kittens is 2–9 weeks. If you're getting an 8-week-old kitten, the breeder or shelter has already done most of the heavy lifting —one more reason to get a kitten from a responsible source that handles kittens daily. But weeks 8–16 are still hugely influential, and that's your window. Use it.

  • Handle the kitten daily. Paws, ears, mouth, belly —brief, gentle, paired with treats. A cat that's been handled everywhere as a kitten will tolerate vet exams without sedation.
  • Introduce the carrier as a normal object. Leave it out with the door open and a soft bed inside. Feed treats in it. A carrier that only appears for vet trips becomes a terror object. A carrier that's always around with good things inside is just furniture.
  • Expose to normal household sounds. Vacuum, blender, doorbell, hairdryer —from a distance at first, gradually closer. Pair scary sounds with treats. A cat that hears the vacuum for the first time at 6 months will bolt. A kitten that's been desensitized will nap through it.
  • Introduce visitors. Have friends over —different people, different voices, calm behavior. Let the kitten approach, not the other way around. A kitten that meets 10 different people before 14 weeks is far less likely to become a stranger-fearful adult.
  • If you have other pets: Keep them separated for 3–5 days minimum. Let them smell each other under the door. Swap bedding so they get used to each other's scent. First face-to-face introductions should be brief, supervised, and end before anyone gets tense. With resident cats, this process can take 2–4 weeks. Rushing it creates lasting hostility.

⚠️ The Mistakes New Kitten Owners Make Most

I've made some of these myself. Others I've watched friends make and paid the vet bill for. Learn from our mistakes:

  1. Using hands as toys. That wiggling-fingers game is adorable when the kitten is 8 weeks old and weighs 2 pounds. It's less adorable when the cat is 12 pounds and sinking its teeth into your hand. Never use your hands as play objects. Hands are for petting, treats, and holding. Toys are for attacking. This distinction is the single most important thing you can teach a kitten.
  2. Punishing instead of redirecting. Kitten on the counter? Hissing, yelling, or spraying with water doesn't teach "counter is off-limits" —it teaches "human is unpredictable and scary." Instead, gently place the kitten on the floor and redirect to a scratching post or toy. Consistency over punishment, every time. Cats don't understand discipline. They understand patterns.
  3. Skipping the initial vet visit. "The shelter gave him a clean bill of health" is not the same as establishing care with your own vet. A kitten that seems healthy can have a heart murmur, parasites, or early ringworm that the shelter missed. Your vet's baseline exam catches things early when treatment is cheaper and easier.
  4. Declawing. Declawing is amputation of the last bone of each toe. It's banned in many countries and several U.S. states for good reason —it causes chronic pain, alters gait, and increases biting behavior. Trim nails regularly instead. Provide scratching posts. Use soft plastic nail caps if scratching is destructive.
  5. Only one litter box. Number of cats + 1 is the rule. One kitten = two boxes. Different rooms. Scooped daily. Fully emptied and washed monthly. Most litter box problems are owner problems, not cat problems.
  6. Letting the kitten outdoors. Indoor cats live 12–18 years on average. Outdoor cats live 2–3 years. Cars, coyotes, other cats, poison, disease —the list of things that kill outdoor cats is long and entirely predictable. If you want your cat to experience outside, build a catio or harness-train them. Don't open the door and hope for the best.

🤔 Common Questions About New Kittens

What supplies do I need before bringing a kitten home?

The non-negotiable list: a secure carrier, food and water bowls (stainless steel or ceramic —plastic harbors bacteria and causes chin acne), age-appropriate kitten food (ask the breeder or shelter what they're currently feeding and buy that exact food), at least one litter box plus unscented clumping litter, a scratching post, a few toys, and a cozy bed. A breakaway collar with an ID tag, nail clippers, and a soft brush round out the essentials.

Set everything up before the kitten arrives. The carrier should be lined with something soft. The safe room should be ready. The food and water bowls should be filled. The litter box should be set up with 2–3 inches of litter. The first 48 hours are disorienting enough without you realizing at 10 PM that you forgot to buy a litter scoop. I've done exactly that. Don't be me.

How do I kitten-proof my home?

Get on your hands and knees —literally —and look at the world from kitten height. If it fits in a kitten's mouth, it goes in a kitten's mouth. If there's a gap, the kitten will find it. If something dangles, it's a toy.

Secure dangling cords and blind strings with cord management sleeves or by tying loops short. Remove toxic plants —lilies are the most dangerous; even the pollen can cause fatal kidney failure in cats. Pothos, philodendron, dieffenbachia, and poinsettias are common and should also go. Lock away cleaning supplies, laundry pods, and all human medications in a latched cabinet. Cover small gaps behind appliances and inside recliners. Keep toilet lids down. Put kitchen trash in a cabinet or get a cat-proof bin. Dental floss, thread, rubber bands, and hair ties go in drawers —these are linear foreign body risks that require surgery to remove.

The rule isn't "will the kitten find it?" The rule is "the kitten will definitely find it, so remove it first."

What should I do in the first 48 hours with a new kitten?

Set up a single safe room —a bathroom, spare bedroom, or home office works well —with food, water, litter box, bed, scratching post, and toys all in one contained space. Open the carrier and let the kitten come out on its own timeline. Don't pull it out. Don't hover. Sit on the floor at kitten level and wait. If the kitten hides, leave it alone —it'll come out when it's ready.

Place the kitten gently in the litter box so it registers the location. Feed the same food the kitten was eating before you brought it home. Keep other pets completely separated for at least 3–5 days —they'll smell each other under the door and that's enough for now. Handle paws, ears, and mouth briefly and gently, paired with treats. Keep interaction sessions short, especially on day one. The only goals for the first 48 hours: the kitten eats, drinks, uses the litter box, and starts to feel safe. Everything else can wait.

When should a new kitten see the vet?

Within the first 3–5 days of coming home. Even if the shelter or breeder gave you a health record, get your own vet's independent baseline. The first visit includes: body weight and condition scoring, checking for ear mites and fleas, listening to heart and lungs, abdominal palpation, and confirming the vaccination and deworming schedule. If you adopted an adult cat, your vet should also run a FeLV/FIV test if one wasn't done or if the results are unknown.

Kittens need a series of FVRCP vaccines starting at 6–8 weeks, with boosters every 3–4 weeks until at least 16 weeks of age. The rabies vaccine is given at 12–16 weeks. Spay or neuter surgery is typically scheduled between 4–6 months —earlier is increasingly common and safe, and prevents the risk of an accidental first heat (which can happen as early as 4 months in female cats).

How often should I feed a kitten?

Kittens under 6 months need 3–4 small meals spread across the day. Their stomachs are tiny —roughly the size of a walnut at 8 weeks —and their calorie demands per pound of body weight are triple an adult cat's. From 6 to 12 months, 3 meals per day works for most kittens. After 12 months, twice-daily feeding is appropriate.

Feed a food specifically labeled "for growth" or "all life stages." These formulas have the higher protein, fat, and micronutrient density that developing bones, muscles, and organs require. Wet food is better than dry for hydration —cats evolved from desert-dwelling ancestors and have a naturally low thirst drive. If you do feed dry food, provide fresh water in multiple locations (cats drink more when water isn't right next to their food —an evolutionary preference for water sources away from prey).

Free-feeding dry food all day is convenient but teaches poor habits. Measured meals from the start give you better control over intake, make it easier to spot appetite changes early, and build the routine that prevents adult obesity.
