🐶 New Puppy Checklist

What you actually need. Not what the pet store wants you to buy. | 2026-06-05 · MeltPet · 5 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-14

I Watched a Friend Spend $400 on Puppy Stuff She Never Used. Here's What She Actually Needed.

Pet stores love new puppy owners. The aisles are full of things that look essential and aren't: designer beds the puppy will destroy, automated feeders you don't need, seventeen varieties of treats that all do the same thing. My friend Jess dropped $400 at a big-box pet store the day before picking up her Lab puppy. Two weeks later, the bed was shredded, the automatic feeder was in a closet, and the puppy was sleeping on a $12 fleece blanket from Target —which was, according to the puppy, perfect.

I've put together a lot of puppy kits over the years. Here's what you actually use and what collects dust.

The Non-Negotiables

A crate. Wire crate with a divider panel so you can adjust the size as the puppy grows. Too big and the puppy will use one end as a bathroom. Too small and you'll be buying a new crate in three months. The divider solves both problems. Put a cheap fleece blanket inside —nothing with stuffing the puppy can ingest. The crate is not a punishment. It's a den. Feed meals in it. Throw treats in it randomly. Make it the place where good things happen and the puppy will choose to go in on her own. If you crate-train properly, you will use this crate for the dog's entire life.

Enzymatic cleaner. Nature's Miracle or similar. Not regular household cleaner —those remove the stain for you but the enzymatic smell markers remain for the dog, which means she'll pee in the same spot again. Enzymatic cleaner breaks down the proteins in urine and feces that signal "bathroom here" to a dog's nose. Buy a gallon. You'll use it.

Chew toys. A Kong (the classic red kind —stuff it with kibble and a little peanut butter and freeze it), a rope toy for tug, a few rubber chew toys of different textures. Puppies teethe. If you don't provide appropriate things to chew, they'll chew your baseboards, your shoes, and your fingertips. Rotate the toys so there's always something "new" —three toys out, three in a closet. Swap every few days.

A leash and a flat collar. Not a retractable leash. Not a harness yet —you can get one later if the dog pulls. A 6-foot nylon leash and a simple buckle collar with an ID tag that has your phone number on it. Breakaway collar for safety. The tag goes on before the puppy comes home.

Food and water bowls. Stainless steel. Not plastic. Not ceramic (some glazes contain lead). Stainless is unbreakable, doesn't harbor bacteria in scratches the way plastic does, and is dishwasher-safe. Heavy-bottom or a non-slip mat underneath so the puppy can't flip them.

Puppy food. Ask the breeder or shelter exactly what the puppy is eating and buy a bag of that. Don't switch foods in the first week —the stress of a new home plus a new diet equals diarrhea, and a dehydrated puppy is an emergency. If you want to switch to a different food, do it gradually starting week two.

🛒 One cart, done: Everything on the list above — crate with divider, enzymatic cleaner, KONG puppy, 6-foot leash, stainless bowls — is stocked at Amazon. Amazon is good for price-comparing individual items. The wire crate with divider panel is the single biggest upfront purchase —MidWest and Frisco both make decent ones in the $40–$70 range. Buy the crate sized for the adult dog, not the puppy; the divider makes it work at any stage. The enzymatic cleaner is Nature's Miracle or Rocco & Roxie —either works, get the gallon jug, not the spray bottle.

The Puppy-Proofing That Actually Matters

Puppies find things you don't know exist. They get behind furniture. They wedge under cabinets. They discover the one electrical cord you thought was hidden. The rule is simple: if it's within 18 inches of the floor and you care about it, move it.

Electrical cords: tape them down, run them through cord covers, or block access entirely. A puppy who chews through a live cord gets mouth burns and possibly cardiac arrhythmia. This happens. I know a puppy who spent two nights in the ICU after finding a laptop charger.

Plants: move them up or out. Lilies, sago palm, azaleas, oleander, and many common houseplants are toxic. Check the ASPCA toxic plant list for every plant in your home.

Trash cans: inside a cabinet or with a heavy, dog-proof lid. Chicken bones, moldy food, plastic wrappers that smell like meat —all of it is dangerous and the puppy will find it.

Stairs: baby gates. Puppies can climb stairs before they can safely descend them. A tumble down a flight of stairs at 8 weeks can break bones. Gate the top and bottom.

First Night: You Won't Sleep. That's Normal.

The puppy just left her mother and littermates. She's in a strange place that smells wrong, with creatures she doesn't fully recognize. She will cry. This is normal. It is not cruel. You are not traumatizing her by letting her sleep in a crate in your bedroom or in a pen nearby. Put the crate where you can reach a hand down to it. A warm water bottle wrapped in a towel simulates littermate body heat. A shirt you've worn all day in the crate gives her your scent. A ticking clock wrapped in a towel allegedly mimics a heartbeat —some puppies respond, some don't care.

Set an alarm for every 2–3 hours for a potty break. An 8-week-old puppy can hold her bladder for roughly 2–3 hours overnight. Carry her outside —don't let her walk, or she'll pee on the way. One boring potty trip, no play, right back to the crate. She'll cry again. This is the part where you don't give in. If you take her out every time she cries, you're teaching her that crying gets her out of the crate. That lesson takes about three repetitions to learn and months to undo.
