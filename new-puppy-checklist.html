🐶 New Puppy Checklist:
The First 7 Days Survival Guide

What to buy, what to do each day, and the real first-month vet bill. | 2026-06-05 · MeltPet · 12 min read

Last updated: 2026-09-18

The Pet Store Is Not On Your Side

There is one number worth knowing before you buy a single thing for a new puppy, and it isn't about gear. The Royal Kennel Club surveyed people who had just bought a puppy and found that 20% spent no time at all researching where the puppy came from, and 36% took 20 minutes or less to decide. Of the people who chose in 20 minutes or less, 22% suspected afterwards that they had bought from a puppy farm. And 15% of the puppies chosen that fast got sick, needed ongoing veterinary treatment, or died within six months — three times the rate of puppies whose owners spent an hour or more on the decision.

That is what an impulse puppy actually costs. Not a wasted purchase.

The gear is a smaller problem, but it follows the same logic. Pet store layouts are merchandised, not designed. The $80 orthopedic bed sits at eye level. The divider-panel crate —the single most useful object you will buy— is on the bottom shelf in a box with no photograph on it. I watched a friend drop $400 the day before picking up her Labrador. Two weeks later the bed was shredded, the automatic feeder was in a closet, and the puppy was asleep on a $12 fleece blanket.

What follows is the version that survives contact with an actual puppy: what to buy before pickup day, what to do on each of the first seven days, and the real first-month veterinary bill —exams, vaccines, deworming, microchip, preventives.

📦 The Non-Negotiables: Buy It Before Pickup Day

The rule is that nothing gets bought on the way home. You will be holding a nervous eight-week-old in a box, it will be six in the evening, and you will grab whatever is nearest the register. That is exactly how people end up with a puppy-sized crate they replace in ten weeks.

Two of these deserve more than a table cell. The first is the crate. Buy the size that fits the adult dog, then use the divider to give the puppy only as much room as she needs to stand, turn around, and lie down. Puppies will not soil a space that small. Given the whole crate, they will use the far end as a bathroom and you will spend three weeks unteaching it. If someone tells you to start with a small crate and buy a bigger one later, ask them how much they think crates cost. One crate, one divider.

The second is the enzymatic cleaner. Buy the gallon, not the spray bottle. A puppy having four to six accidents a day for three weeks will empty a spray bottle in about nine days. Use it correctly, too: soak the spot, leave it for fifteen minutes, then blot. If you scrub and move on, you have spread the uric acid around rather than destroying it.

On toys, the useful trick is rotation. Keep three out and three in a closet, and swap every few days. An eight-week-old puppy has the attention span of a housefly, so a toy she hasn't seen since Tuesday is a new toy. It is also why you don't need eleven of them.

🗓️ The First 7 Days, Day by Day

Puppies come home at about eight weeks. Everything below assumes that. If you are bringing home a twelve-week-old or a six-month-old from a shelter, the same sequence applies with the timelines compressed.

Day 1 — Pickup Day and the First Night

Bring a towel that smells like you and put it in the carrier. Ask the breeder or rescue for a small bag of the food she is eating, a copy of any vaccination records, and —this one always gets forgotten— a piece of bedding from the litter. Your puppy has just been separated from her mother and littermates and put in a car with strangers. That bedding is the only familiar smell she has left.

Do not stop at a pet store on the way home. Do not invite six people over. Keep the arrival boring. Put her down in a small, already-prepared area —the kitchen behind a baby gate works well— and let her walk it. Sit on the floor. Don't pick her up every thirty seconds.

The first night is the hard one. She will cry. That is normal, it is not cruel, and it does not mean you are doing it wrong. Put the crate where you can reach a hand down to it, ideally beside your bed. A hot water bottle wrapped in a towel mimics littermate body heat. A shirt you have worn all day goes inside. Set an alarm every two to three hours and carry her outside —carry, don't let her walk, or she will urinate on the way. One boring trip: no play, no talking, straight back in. If you take her out every single time she cries, you have taught her that crying opens the crate door. That lesson takes about three repetitions to learn and weeks to undo.

What to expect: almost no sleep. What not to do: put her in your bed. Once that habit forms it is permanent, and a sixty-pound dog who has slept in your bed since she was eight weeks old is not a decision you can revisit later.

Day 2 — The Vet Visit

Book it before pickup day, ideally for day two or three. Bring the vaccination records and the food bag so the vet can read the label. The exam covers weight, temperature, heart and lung sounds, eyes, ears, the umbilicus, and a fecal float —puppies are almost always born with roundworms, and deworming at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks is standard.

Two things about this visit get done wrong. First, carry her into the clinic and keep her on your lap in the waiting room. Parvovirus survives in soil for years, and the waiting room floor is where sick dogs have stood. Second, decide about insurance now, not next month. Anything written into her record today becomes a pre-existing exclusion tomorrow. Our own first-year experiment lost ear coverage for six months over an ear infection noted in a shelter record before the policy started.

What to expect: a bill, a weight baseline, and a vaccine schedule with dates on it. Photograph the schedule. Missing a booster means restarting the series.

Day 3 — The Crate Stops Being a Box

Start making the crate the best place in the house. Feed every meal inside it with the door open, and don't close the door while she is eating for the first few days. Toss a treat in at random moments as you walk past. The goal is that she goes in on her own to check whether something good has appeared.

Once she walks in willingly, close the door for five seconds, treat, open. Then fifteen seconds. Then a minute. Then step out of sight for ten seconds and come back. If she panics, you moved too fast —go back a step and repeat it slower. A crate-trained dog is easier to house-train, easier to travel with, less stressed at the vet, and cheaper to board. This is the highest-return twenty minutes a day in the first month.

What not to do: never use the crate as punishment, and don't leave her in it for more than an hour or two at a stretch during the day this week. At eight weeks she genuinely cannot hold it longer than that.

Day 4 — The Potty Schedule That Actually Works

Forget "every two hours." The useful rule for a young puppy is age in months plus one equals the maximum hours between bathroom breaks. An eight-week-old is two months old, so three hours maximum —and that is during the day, awake and drinking. Overnight she may stretch to four, not because her bladder is bigger but because she is asleep.

Take her out after every one of these: waking, eating, drinking, playing, training, and any time in the crate. That is six triggers and it will feel like all you do. Go to the same spot in the yard each time and stand still —walking around is distracting. Praise the instant she finishes, not when she comes back inside, or you have trained her to come inside to collect the reward.

When there is an accident inside, clean it with enzymatic cleaner rather than disinfectant, and never rub her nose in it. She cannot connect a punishment to something she did forty seconds ago. All you will produce is a puppy who toilets out of sight, which is harder to fix than the original problem.

Day 5 — Socialization, With One Hard Limit

The socialization window closes somewhere between 12 and 16 weeks. Whatever she meets inside that window she will treat as normal for the rest of her life; whatever she doesn't, she may fear forever. If she comes home at eight weeks, you have four to eight weeks and no more. This is the most time-sensitive item in this article.

The hard limit is parvovirus. She is not fully protected until two weeks after her final DHPP booster at 14–16 weeks. That means no dog parks, no pet store floors, no walks on ground where unknown dogs walk, and no meeting unvaccinated adult dogs. It does not mean confining her to the house. Carry her: to a café patio, a hardware store, a friend's apartment, a parked car with the window down. Let her watch traffic, strollers, wheelchairs, beards, hats, umbrellas, and delivery trucks from your arms. Invite vaccinated, healthy adult dogs into your own yard. Handle her feet, ears, and mouth every day.

A useful target is a hundred different people before sixteen weeks. Different ages, different voices, people in hats, people with canes. Six people five times each does not count. Put the number on the fridge if you have to.

Day 6 — Handling, Ears and Teeth

Ten seconds at a time, paired with a treat, several times a day: lift each lip and look at the teeth, rub the inside of each ear, press each toenail, open the mouth, touch the tail. A puppy handled this way will let you brush her teeth and clean her ears without a fight, and will let the vet examine her without sedation. A dog who has not been handled this way will make you regret it at the clinic for twelve years, and it is a fifteen-minute job to prevent now.

Start the toothbrush this week, with dog-specific enzymatic toothpaste and a finger brush. Not human toothpaste —xylitol, which is in most of it, is toxic to dogs. Two or three sessions of ten seconds in week one, building to a proper brush three times a week, is enough. Periodontal disease affects the majority of dogs by age three, and the treatment is a general anaesthetic plus a bill several times the price of a toothbrush.

Day 7 — First Review, and What's Coming

Seven days in, check four things: is she eating normally, is her stool firm and well-formed, is she gaining weight, and is she sleeping more at night than she did on day one. If the answer is yes to all four, week one went well. If her stool is soft she is probably fine —stress and a food change both cause loose stool— but call the vet if there is blood, if it lasts more than two days, or if she stops eating. Keep an eye on her weight with the dog weight calculator, because "gaining weight" is much easier to confirm with a number than with a feeling.

Then look at the calendar, because the first month contains more veterinary care than any other month of her life. The next section prices it.

Three things are not in that $225–$425, and they are the ones that turn a budget into a problem:

  • The 14–16 week visit. DHPP #3 plus rabies, $70–$150. It falls in month two or three rather than month one, but it is already scheduled, and rabies is required by law in most U.S. states.
  • Spay or neuter. $200–$500 at a private clinic, sometimes $50–$150 at a low-cost clinic. It lands around 4–6 months.
  • Anything unplanned. A puppy who eats a sock and needs intestinal surgery is a $2,000–$5,000 night. That is the actual argument for pet insurance —enrol before the first exam, because a diagnosis becomes a pre-existing exclusion the moment it is written down. 🚫 Puppy-Proofing: The 18-Inch Rule

The whole thing in one sentence: if it is within 18 inches of the floor and you care about it, move it. Then get down on the floor and look around from that height, because your idea of "out of reach" was formed standing up.

  • Cords and cables. Tape them down, run them through covers, or block the outlet entirely. A laptop charger, a phone cable, and a lamp cord are all chewable at puppy height. Chewing a live cord causes mouth burns and can cause cardiac arrhythmia, and the puppy who spent two nights in an ICU over a charger was not an unusual case.
  • Plants. Lilies, sago palm, azalea, oleander, and a long list of ordinary houseplants are toxic to dogs. Check everything in the house against our toxic food and plant checker and the ASPCA list, and when in doubt put it somewhere she cannot reach —which, for an eight-week-old, means at least four feet up.
  • Medications. All of them, including vitamins and anything in a blister pack. Locked away, not at the back of the counter. Ibuprofen and acetaminophen are both dangerous to dogs at doses a human would take without thinking.
  • Trash. Inside a cabinet, or in a bin with a lid heavy enough that she cannot nose it open. Chicken bones, mouldy food, and meat wrappers are the classic middle-of-the-night emergency.
  • Stairs. Gate the top and the bottom. Puppies climb before they can safely come down, and a fall at eight weeks breaks bones.
  • Small gaps. Behind the fridge, under the toe-kick of kitchen cabinets, behind the washing machine, inside a reclining chair. Rolled towels handle most of them.
  • Shoes and remote controls. Your shoes carry the smell of everywhere you have been, which makes them the most interesting object in the house. Closet, door shut. Same for remotes, headphones, and anything with a soft rubber grip.
  • Toilet lids down. Not for the reason you think. A puppy who learns the toilet bowl is a water source also decides the toilet brush is a toy.

One thing here is not about safety: gate off a zone you can tolerate. Trying to keep a puppy off every surface in the house during week one means correcting her constantly, and a puppy who is corrected constantly becomes timid. Pick one room, make it safe, and give her the run of that room. The etiquette rules for when she finally meets other dogs can wait until her vaccines are done.

⚠️ The 7 Mistakes That Cost the Most

Not the most embarrassing. The most expensive —in money, in behaviour you will be unteaching in six months, or both.

  1. Buying the puppy-sized crate. You will buy a second one within twelve weeks. One crate, adult size, divider panel.
  2. Cleaning accidents with household cleaner. The stain disappears, the scent marker doesn't, and she re-soils the same square foot for weeks. Enzymatic cleaner, soaked for fifteen minutes, is both the fix and the prevention.
  3. Taking her to the dog park before her final vaccine. Parvovirus can kill an untreated puppy, and it survives in soil for years. Carry her, host vaccinated adult dogs at home, and wait until two weeks past the 14–16 week booster. Socialization and vaccination are not in conflict —the answer is a carried puppy, not an isolated one. If she is already struggling with being alone, separation anxiety in dogs is worth reading before the crying becomes a habit.
  4. Punishing accidents after the fact. She cannot connect a punishment to something she did forty seconds ago. Punishment produces a dog who toilets behind the couch, which is harder to fix than the original problem.
  5. Using your hands as toys. Adorable at two pounds, a bite-history problem at sixty. Hands pet, feed, and hold. Toys get attacked.
  6. Waiting on insurance until after the first vet visit. One notation in the record —a mild ear infection, a heart murmur under observation, a suspect hip— becomes a permanent exclusion. Enrol the day before pickup.
  7. Buying the mountain. The orthopedic bed, the automatic feeder, four kinds of treats, the stroller. The measured list in this article is $180–$380. Everything past it is chance, and most of the extras are quietly retired by week three. Put the difference in the vet fund instead —month one costs $225–$425 before you have bought a single toy.

🤔 Common Questions About New Puppies

What do I need before bringing a puppy home?

Eight things, and none of them are on the endcap: a wire crate with a divider panel sized for the adult dog, a flat fleece blanket for inside it, a gallon of enzymatic cleaner, chew toys in three textures, a flat collar with an ID tag that goes on before pickup, a 6-foot leash, two stainless steel bowls, and the exact food the breeder or rescue has been feeding. Add two baby gates and cord covers for safety. Measured against real prices, that is $180–$380. You do not need an orthopedic bed, an automatic feeder, or a stroller. Puppies need supervision, routine, and patience —all of which are free and much harder to supply than a credit card.

How do I puppy-proof my house for a puppy?

Get on the floor and look at everything at puppy height —18 inches and below. Cords get taped down or covered, because chewing a live cable causes mouth burns and sometimes cardiac arrhythmia. Shoes, remote controls, and headphones go in closets. Medications and cleaning supplies get locked in a latched cabinet. Trash cans go inside cabinets or get a lid she cannot nose open. Every houseplant gets checked against a toxic plant list and moved at least four feet up. Toilet lids stay down. Baby gates go at the top and bottom of every staircase. The rule is not "will she find it" —she will. The rule is "move it before she does."

How much does the first month with a puppy cost?

$225–$425 at a typical U.S. clinic, covering two vet visits and the first month of preventives. The first visit at eight weeks includes the wellness exam, DHPP #1, deworming, a fecal and giardia test, and a microchip, and runs $145–$260. The second visit at 10–12 weeks adds DHPP #2 for $60–$120. Heartworm and flea/tick preventive cost $20–$45 a month and are dosed by weight. Add $180–$380 of starting supplies and the adoption fee. Spay or neuter at $200–$500 and the 14–16 week rabies visit at $70–$150 both land after month one. For scale, our tracked first year of dog ownership came to $5,237.

When should a new puppy see the vet, and what vaccines does she need?

Within 72 hours of coming home, ideally on day two or three. Core vaccines start at 6–8 weeks with DHPP —distemper, hepatitis, parainfluenza, and parvovirus —and repeat at 10–12 weeks and again at 14–16 weeks. The final dose matters most, because maternal antibodies can neutralise the earlier ones. Rabies is given at 14–16 weeks and is required by law in most U.S. states. Leptospirosis, canine influenza, Lyme, and Bordetella are lifestyle vaccines; ask about them based on whether your dog will swim, hike, or attend daycare. Keep the puppy off unknown ground until two weeks after the final DHPP booster, because parvovirus survives in soil for years.

How do I crate train a puppy in the first week?

Feed every meal inside the crate with the door open, and toss a treat in at random moments as you walk past. Once she walks in willingly, close the door for five seconds, treat, open. Then fifteen seconds, then a minute, then step out of sight for ten seconds. If she panics, you moved too fast —go back a step. Put the crate beside your bed for the first week and set an alarm every two to three hours to carry her outside for a boring, play-free bathroom trip. Never use the crate as punishment. A crate-trained dog is easier to house-train, travels better, and is far less stressed at the vet.
