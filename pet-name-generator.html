How to Actually Pick a Name Your Pet Will Answer To

I've spent five years in pet food, and I've met a lot of dogs named things their owners regret. A few rules that actually hold up:

Two syllables, ideally. One-syllable names are fine for commands but easy for a dog to confuse with other sounds. Three-plus syllables will inevitably get shortened. "Maximilian" becomes "Max" within a week —so just name him Max. Two syllables is the sweet spot: easy to say fast, distinct enough to cut through noise.

Avoid names that sound like commands. Bo sounds like "no." Kit sounds like "sit." Ray sounds like "stay." Your dog doesn't know you named her Rae —she just hears "stay" and looks confused. Say the name next to your most-used commands out loud and see if they rhyme.

For cats: high-pitched endings work. Names ending in an "ee" sound (Mochi, Suki, Ziggy, Cookie) naturally fall into the frequency range cats pay most attention to. This isn't magic —it's acoustics. A cat's hearing peaks around 8 kHz, and high-front vowels ("ee", "ih") produce more energy in that range than low-back vowels ("aw", "uh"). That's part of why "Kitty" works on almost every cat.

The three-day test. Use the name for three days before committing. Say it out loud in every tone you'll actually use: the happy greeting tone, the "what are you eating" panic tone, the calling-them-in-from-the-yard tone. If you feel stupid, keep looking. If it feels natural, you've got it.
