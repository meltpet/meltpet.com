Build Your Pet's Raw Meal Plan

How Much Raw Food? The 2–3% Rule, Then the 80/10/5/5 Split

An adult dog or cat eats 2–3% of their ideal body weight in raw food per day. From there, the Prey Model Raw (PMR) ratio — often called "80/10/10" — splits that daily amount into: 80% muscle meat, 10% edible bone, 5% liver, and 5% other secreting organ (kidney, spleen, pancreas). That's the whole structure. Everything else is detail.

Why each part is non-negotiable:

  • Muscle meat (80%) — protein and calories. Includes heart, which counts as muscle meat (not organ) and is a rich taurine source.
  • Edible bone (10%) — the calcium source. Bone balances the phosphorus in meat; the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio is what keeps a pet's own bones and teeth healthy. Get this wrong and you get metabolic bone disease, especially in growing animals.
  • Liver (5%) — vitamin A and B vitamins. Liver is essential, but it's also high in vitamin A, which is why it's capped at 5% — more is not better.
  • Other secreting organ (5%) — kidney and spleen supply iron, zinc, copper, and folate that muscle meat lacks. This is the part most beginners skip, and skipping it causes real deficiencies over time.

The model mirrors the proportions of a whole prey animal — roughly what a cat or a wild canid would eat if it caught a mouse or a rabbit. That's the idea: approximate the diet the animal evolved on, not a processed pellet.

How the Calories Work (So the 2–3% Isn't a Blind Guess)

The raw-feeding community's 2–3% rule lines up with the veterinary calorie math. A pet's energy need is RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75, multiplied by a life-stage factor (roughly 1.6 for a neutered adult dog, 1.2 for a neutered adult cat, higher for puppies, kittens, and nursing mothers).

Raw food averages about 1.5–1.7 kcal per gram. A 10 kg (22 lb) dog needs roughly 500–550 kcal/day → about 300–340 grams of raw food → right at 3% of body weight. The two methods agree because they're describing the same animal. The calculator above shows you both: the calorie target and the gram amount.

Then you tune by results, not by the formula. Weigh the pet weekly. If they gain unwanted weight, cut the daily amount by 10%. If they get too thin, add 10%. Body condition — how the ribs feel under your hands — beats any calculator.

The Four Things That Go Wrong With Raw Diets

Raw feeding isn't dangerous because it's raw. It's dangerous when it's unbalanced. These are the four failures that actually hurt animals:

1. Calcium–phosphorus imbalance. Meat is high in phosphorus and low in calcium. Without bone (or a calcium supplement), the body leaches calcium from its own bones to balance blood chemistry — softening bones, especially in growing puppies and kittens. This is the #1 nutritional failure in homemade raw diets. The 10% bone is not optional.

2. Taurine deficiency. Cats cannot synthesize enough taurine and will develop dilated cardiomyopathy (heart failure) and blindness without it. Taurine is abundant in heart and dark meat, but it degrades with freezing and grinding, so most cat raw recipes add it as a supplement. The calculator lists it as mandatory for cats — do not skip it.

3. Vitamin A excess (or deficiency). Liver is the vitamin A source, and 5% is the sweet spot. Too little liver → deficiency; too much → vitamin A toxicity (joint pain, bone abnormalities). This is why liver is a precise 5%, never "a big scoop."

4. Pathogens. Raw meat can carry Salmonella, Listeria, and E. coli. Freeze meat for 3+ weeks to reduce parasite risk, use dedicated utensils, wash surfaces, and skip raw entirely if anyone in the house is pregnant, elderly, or immunocompromised. Pathogen risk is real but manageable; nutritional imbalance is the more common — and more silent — problem.

Supplements: The Five That Matter

A PMR diet is meat-based, which means it's short on a few things a whole prey animal would supply through fur, feathers, blood, and stomach contents. Most raw feeders add these five. Doses are starting ranges per day — confirm against your specific supplement's label, because concentrations vary by brand.

SupplementWhyStarting dose
TaurineHeart + eye healthCats: 250–500 mg/day (mandatory). Dogs: 250–500 mg/day (optional, large breeds)
Vitamin EAntioxidant; balances the polyunsaturated fat in fish oil1–2 IU per kg body weight / day
Omega-3 (fish oil)Anti-inflammatory; corrects the omega-6-heavy profile of farmed meat~50 mg combined EPA+DHA per kg body weight / day
ZincSkin + immune function; farmed meat is low in zinc15–30 mg per 1000 kcal
ManganeseBone + connective tissue1–2 mg per 1000 kcal
Iodine (kelp)Thyroid function0.35–0.7 mg iodine per 1000 kcal — kelp varies wildly by product, start low and read the label

Doses are general starting points, not prescriptions. Supplement concentration differs by brand. This is why a board-certified veterinary nutritionist review matters.

⚠️ Before You Feed Raw — Read This

  • This is a planning tool, not veterinary advice. Have the final recipe reviewed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist before feeding.
  • Raw diets are not recommended for households with infants, the elderly, pregnant people, or anyone immunocompromised.
  • Freeze meat for at least 3 weeks before feeding to reduce parasite risk (freezing does not reliably kill bacteria).
  • Never feed cooked bones (splinter hazard). Raw edible bone only, and size it to your pet — a choking hazard if too large or too small.
