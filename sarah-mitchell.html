Pet food R&D veteran. Clinical research methodology nerd. The human behind MeltPet's evidence obsession. | 2026-07-29

Five Years in a Lab That Smelled Like Kibble. Here's What I Learned.

I spent half a decade inside the pet food industry — not in marketing, not in sales, but in the unglamorous part where ingredients get weighed, formulations get tested, and claims get verified (or quietly abandoned). I've watched a roomful of people argue for 45 minutes about the digestibility coefficient of a single protein source. I've seen a formula change that saved a brand $0.03 per bag — and triggered a wave of customer complaints about stool quality that took six months to trace back to the change. I've sat in meetings where someone presented a marketing claim so misleading I had to excuse myself and go stare at a wall for ten minutes.

I left that world because I wanted to be on the other side of the information asymmetry. Pet owners deserve to know what's real and what's a marketing budget with a lab coat on. That's what MeltPet is for. Every article on this site pulls from peer-reviewed research, industry datasets most people never see, and the hard-won skepticism that comes from being inside the machine.

The Research Lens

Before pet food, I trained in clinical research methodology — not as a veterinarian, but on the data side: study design, statistical analysis, the thousand ways bias creeps into results when nobody's looking. That training shaped everything about how I approach pet health information.

When a study says "dogs fed Diet A showed improved coat condition," I want to know: who funded it? How many dogs were in the trial? Was it blinded? Were the coat condition assessments done by the same person who knew which dogs were in which group? Was "improved coat condition" measured with an instrument or estimated by a human with a clipboard? These questions sound tedious, and they are — but they're also the difference between a finding you can trust and a finding that dissolves under scrutiny.

Every article published on MeltPet goes through this filter: would I stake my reputation on this claim if someone pulled the original source? If the answer is no, the claim doesn't get published. That's why you won't find generic "studies show" language here without a citation you can follow. It's also why some topics — things other pet sites publish without hesitation — never appear on MeltPet. If the evidence isn't there, I'd rather leave the page blank than fill it with plausibility.

Gus, the Tabby Who Keeps the Science Honest

All the research training in the world doesn't matter if you forget what it's actually about. I live with a brown tabby named Gus — a former stray who showed up at my door one January with a torn ear and an unshakeable conviction that he belonged inside. He was right. He's been my constant shadow for years now, and he's the reason every article on this site starts from a real question a real pet owner would actually ask.

Gus kneads my ribcage at sunrise. He treats 2AM like an Olympic qualifier. He follows me to the bathroom with the same intensity other cats reserve for laser pointers. And when I'm writing about something — zoonotic risk, dietary phosphorus and kidney disease, the actual evidence behind feline facial pheromone diffusers — I don't think about search volume or keyword density. I think about whether what I'm writing would actually help someone sitting on their living room floor at midnight, googling "why is my cat vomiting yellow" with a sick animal in their lap.

If the article wouldn't help that person, it doesn't belong on this site.

What I Write About (And What I Don't)

My expertise is in pet food formulation, ingredient evaluation, and clinical research methodology. That means I can tell you — with citations — what the difference is between chicken meal and chicken by-product meal, why "grain-free" became a marketing term rather than a nutritional category, and how to read a pet food label so you actually understand what's in the bag. I can explain why certain cat breeds are predisposed to kidney disease and what the evidence says about dietary phosphorus restriction as a preventative measure. I can walk you through the real cost of owning a dog — not the idealized version, but the receipt-tracked version that includes the emergency vet visit nobody budgets for.

What I won't do: diagnose your pet remotely, tell you what to feed without knowing your animal's specific health status, or pretend certainty where the evidence is thin. Some questions don't have clean answers yet. When that's the case, I'll tell you what we know, what we don't, and how to think about the gap.

For corrections, questions, or source requests: reach out through the About page. I read everything.
