🐕 Separation Anxiety in Dogs

Your dog isn't bad. He's terrified. Here's what works. | 2026-06-10 · MeltPet · 6 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-07

Jake Ate Through a Door. Not the Doorframe. The Door.

My friend Sarah adopted a two-year-old Border Collie mix named Jake from a county shelter. Sweet dog. Smart —figured out the treat puzzle in 30 seconds flat. The first time Sarah left for work, Jake chewed through the drywall next to the front door. The second time, he shredded the doorframe. The third time —after being confined to the kitchen with a baby gate —he ate through the hollow-core door itself. Not scratched. Chewed a hole big enough to fit his entire body through.

Sarah came home to a bloody-mouthed dog, a destroyed kitchen, and a note from her landlord taped to her apartment door. The neighbor in 2A had called in a noise complaint. Jake had been howling for four hours straight.

Her first thought: "I adopted a bad dog." Her vet's first thought: "This dog is having a panic attack." Same behavior. Completely different diagnosis.

It's Not Boredom. It's Not Spite. It's Panic.

Separation anxiety affects somewhere between 14 and 20 percent of dogs, depending on which study you read. The mechanism is simple and brutal: the dog has formed an attachment so strong that your absence triggers a genuine fight-or-flight response. Cortisol spikes. Heart rate elevates. The dog is not making choices about what to chew. He is trying to escape the terrifying situation of being alone.

The destruction pattern tells you the difference between anxiety and boredom. An anxious dog destroys exit points —doors, windows, the crate itself. He's trying to get out and find you. A bored dog destroys fun things —couch cushions, shoes, the trash. He's entertaining himself. An anxious dog starts showing distress within minutes of your departure —pacing, whining, drooling, trembling, sometimes losing bladder control even if he's fully house-trained. A bored dog might be fine for two hours and then decide the remote control looks interesting.

If your dog follows you from room to room when you're home, can't settle even when you're nearby, and starts panicking during departure routines —grabbing your keys, putting on shoes —that's anxiety. The dog isn't being difficult. He's afraid.

The Protocol: Systematic Desensitization

Veterinary behaviorists use a protocol called systematic desensitization. The idea is simple: you teach the dog that being alone is safe by never letting the alone-time exceed what he can handle. Every time the dog panics, you lose ground. The entire training structure is built around keeping the dog under threshold —calm, or at least not panicking —at all times.

Here's what the first week actually looks like, based on the protocol Dr. Karen Overall developed at Penn Vet and what I've seen work with dozens of dogs since:

Day 1—: Decouple the departure cues. Pick up your keys. Put them down. Walk to the door. Touch the handle. Sit back down. Do this 10–20 times a day, in random order, with zero emotional reaction from you —no goodbyes, no reassurances. The goal is to make these cues meaningless. Right now your dog is a detective —he knows the exact sequence of events that precedes you leaving, and the anxiety starts well before you reach the door. You need to break that association. Keys don't mean you're leaving. Shoes don't mean you're leaving. They're just objects you touch sometimes.

Day 3—: Absences measured in seconds. Walk to the door. Step outside. Close the door. Count to five. Come back inside like nothing happened. Do it again. Count to ten. Then five again. Randomize the duration. The dog needs to learn that you leaving doesn't predict how long you'll be gone —and that you always come back. If the dog stays calm at 30 seconds, try 45. If he whines at 45, go back to 20 and build slower. The rule is: never end a training session on a panic. If you miscalculate and the dog freaks out, wait for a moment of quiet, come back in, and next time make the absence shorter.

Week 2–4: Minutes, finally. Once the dog can handle 10–15 minutes alone, the progress curve finally starts to bend upward. This can take two weeks of daily work. It can take a month. The dog sets the pace, not you. By week four, most dogs on this protocol can manage 1–4 hours if the desensitization has been consistent and there haven't been any panic-level failures.

The Stuff That Makes This Possible

You cannot do this protocol if the dog is being left alone for eight hours every weekday. Every panic episode during your workday erases the training gains. You need coverage —a pet sitter, doggy daycare, a friend, a neighbor who works from home —for at least the first month. This is the part that makes people give up. The training itself is simple. Arranging your life so the dog is never alone long enough to panic during training is the hard part. It's also the non-negotiable part. This same desensitization approach works for other behavior issues — the tree method for leash pulling uses the identical behavioral principle: stay under threshold, reward calm, build duration slowly.

Exercise helps. A tired dog has a lower anxiety baseline. A 30-minute walk or a vigorous fetch session 20 minutes before a training absence makes a real difference. It doesn't cure separation anxiety, but it shifts the starting conditions in your favor.

Medication is not a failure. For moderate to severe cases, veterinary behaviorists frequently prescribe daily SSRI medication (fluoxetine, clonidine) alongside the training protocol. The medication reduces the panic response enough for the training to actually work. A dog in full panic mode can't learn that being alone is safe. Medication brings the anxiety down to a level where learning is possible. Most dogs don't need it forever —6–12 months is typical, tapered gradually once the behavior is solid. Talk to your vet or a veterinary behaviorist. This isn't drugging your dog into submission. It's giving him the neurological space to learn that he's safe.

🛒 Gear that helps the protocol work: A KONG Extreme stuffed with frozen peanut butter and kibble buys you 20–40 minutes of focused solo chewing —use it during training absences so the dog associates your departure with something good. A Adaptil diffuser releases a synthetic version of the calming pheromone nursing mother dogs produce —it won't cure separation anxiety on its own, but it takes the edge off for some dogs during the early weeks of training. A ThunderShirt (gentle compression wrap) helps about 40% of anxious dogs —worth trying, and you can return it if it doesn't help. None of these replace the protocol. They just make the protocol possible.

What Not to Do

Don't punish the dog for destruction you find when you get home. He can't connect the punishment to something he did six hours ago. He'll just learn that you coming home is scary, which makes the anxiety worse. I know how infuriating it is to come home to a destroyed door. Yelling at the dog will not fix it and will likely make the separation anxiety more severe.

Don't crate a dog with separation anxiety unless he's already crate-trained and the crate is a safe space. A panicking dog in a crate will injure himself trying to escape —broken teeth, torn nails, bent bars. Crating an anxious dog without prior positive crate conditioning is dangerous. If the dog isn't already crate-trained, use a dog-proofed room instead.

Don't get a second dog hoping it'll solve the problem. Sometimes it helps. Sometimes you end up with two dogs with separation anxiety. It's an expensive gamble, not a treatment plan. Try the protocol first.

Jake, One Year Later

Sarah did the protocol. She sent Jake to daycare for six weeks while she built up his tolerance in the evenings and on weekends —five-second absences, then thirty seconds, then five minutes. She put him on fluoxetine for eight months with her vet's supervision. She taped a sign on her door apologizing to the neighbors and left them her phone number so they'd call her instead of the landlord if Jake made noise.

Jake is now six. He's fine alone for a full workday. The medication is long since stopped. He still follows Sarah from room to room —that part is just his personality, and it probably always will be. But he no longer panics when she leaves. He chews a Kong and goes to sleep. That's the goal: not a different dog. The same dog, calm.
