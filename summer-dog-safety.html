☀️Summer Dog Safety

Heat kills dogs every summer. It's preventable. Here's how. | 2026-06-16 · MeltPet · 7 min read

Last updated: 2026-07-14

The ER Vet I Know Dreads July. She's Told Me Why.

A dog's normal body temperature is 101–102.5°F. At 106°, the cellular damage begins —proteins denature, enzymes stop working, the lining of the gut breaks down and bacteria leak into the bloodstream. At 108°, death is likely even with aggressive treatment. Heatstroke in dogs is fast, brutal, and almost entirely preventable. The emergency vet I know says July is her worst month. Not because of fireworks injuries. Because of heat. Every single case, she told me, the owner said the same thing: "I didn't think it would happen this fast."

The Car. We All Know This. People Still Do It.

On a 75°F day, a car parked in the shade with the windows cracked reaches 105°F in 20 minutes. On an 85°F day, 120°F in under 10 minutes. Cracking the windows does almost nothing —the greenhouse effect heats the interior faster than the gap can vent it. Leaving the AC running helps, but AC compressors fail, engines stall, and dogs have died in cars where the AC cut out and the owner came back 30 minutes later to a dead dog. If you can't bring the dog inside wherever you're going, leave her at home. There is no safe version of "just a few minutes."

The Pavement Test Nobody Does

Press the back of your hand against the pavement. If you can't hold it there for 7 seconds, it's too hot for your dog's paws. Asphalt on a 90°F day can hit 140°F. At 125°F, skin burns in 60 seconds. At 140°F, burns happen almost instantly. Dog paw pads are tougher than human skin but not heatproof —they blister, peel, and get second-degree burns just like we do. Walk your dog in the morning before the pavement heats up or in the evening after it cools. If you have to walk midday, keep to grass. Booties help if your dog tolerates them.

The Breeds That Can't Handle Heat at All

Brachycephalic breeds —French Bulldogs, English Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers —have compromised airways even when they're healthy. They can't pant efficiently. Panting is how dogs cool themselves. When the air temperature approaches the dog's body temperature, panting stops working entirely because there's no thermal gradient to dissipate heat. For a Frenchie with borderline airways on an 85°F day, a 10-minute walk can trigger heatstroke. These breeds should be kept indoors in air conditioning when the temperature exceeds 80°F. Not a suggestion. These dogs die every summer because someone thought a quick walk would be fine.

Heavy-coated breeds —Huskies, Malamutes, Samoyeds —are built for Arctic temperatures. Their double coats insulate against heat as well as cold, but the insulation has limits. Don't shave them —the coat protects against sunburn and removing it can cause the coat to grow back wrong, permanently damaging the insulation properties. Instead, brush out the undercoat thoroughly, provide constant shade and water, and limit exercise to cool mornings and evenings.

Heatstroke: What It Looks Like and What to Do in the First 5 Minutes

Excessive panting that sounds different —louder, more frantic, the dog's mouth stretched wider than normal. Bright red or dark purple gums and tongue. Thick, ropey saliva. Disorientation —the dog stumbles, doesn't respond to her name, can't walk straight. Collapse. Seizures.

If you see any of these: get the dog out of the heat immediately. Soak her with cool water —not ice-cold, which constricts blood vessels and traps heat in the core. Focus on the groin, armpits, neck, and paw pads where major blood vessels are close to the surface. Do not wrap her in wet towels, which trap heat like a sauna. Offer water but don't force it. And go to the vet immediately —even if the dog seems to recover, the internal damage from heatstroke can kill hours or days later. Kidney failure, DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation —blood clots throughout the body), and cerebral edema are the delayed killers. A dog who "seemed fine" after a heat episode can crash overnight. Go to the vet.

The Water Your Dog Swims In Might Kill Him. Not the Way You Think.

Every summer, perfectly healthy dogs drown in lakes and pools. Not because they can't swim. Because they can't find the exit.

Not all dogs can swim. Bulldogs sink like rocks —their body density and airway compromise make swimming nearly impossible without a life jacket. Dachshunds paddle but their short legs don't generate enough thrust to keep their long spine horizontal. Even strong swimmers like Labs and Goldens can drown if they can't find the pool steps. A dog swimming in circles, front paws clawing at the pool edge, is a dog who will exhaust himself and go under within minutes. Teach your dog where the exit is. Walk her to the steps from the water 20 times. Make it muscle memory. A pool alarm or fence is non-negotiable if you have a dog and young kids —or a dog who might fall in when nobody's watching.

Blue-green algae is the one that terrifies veterinarians. It's not algae. It's cyanobacteria, and it produces a neurotoxin that kills dogs within hours of ingestion. A dog drinks from a scummy pond on a hot day, starts seizing 30 minutes later, and is dead by evening. There is no antidote. The blooms look like green paint spilled on the water's surface, or pea soup, or blue-green scum along the shoreline. They concentrate near the edges where dogs drink. If you see anything that looks like algae on a body of water, do not let your dog near it. Not wading, not drinking, not fetching a stick that lands in the water. The toxin can kill from licking wet fur after a swim. The Facebook posts from owners who learned about blue-green algae the hard way will keep you up at night. Read them once. You won't need a second warning.

Salt water is its own threat. A dog who swallows too much seawater at the beach can develop salt toxicity —vomiting, diarrhea, stumbling, seizures. Bring fresh water to the beach. Rinse your dog after swimming. And if she's gulping seawater, leash her and take a break. A few mouthfuls won't hurt. A dog obsessively drinking ocean water for an hour can die from it.

Dehydration: It's Not Just About the Water Bowl

A dog who's 5% dehydrated starts showing signs. At 10%, it's a medical emergency. The skin tent test takes two seconds: gently pinch the skin between your dog's shoulder blades, lift it, and let go. It should snap back instantly. If it stays tented or slides back slowly, your dog is dehydrated. Gums should be slick and wet. Tacky or dry gums —vet, now.

A bowl of water in the yard isn't enough on a 90-degree day. Water heats up, dogs don't drink hot water, and a dog running around in the sun loses fluids faster than she'll voluntarily replace. Put ice cubes in the bowl. Put out two bowls in different locations —dogs drink more when water is available in multiple places. For dogs that are picky about water temperature, a splash of low-sodium chicken broth makes them drink. This is what dehydration looks like before the tent test catches it: the dog stops peeing as often, her nose is dry, she's panting with a dry tongue instead of a wet one, and she's less interested in playing. Catch it here, before it becomes a vet visit.

Keeping a Dog Cool Indoors When the AC Can't Keep Up

Cooling mats work —the gel-filled ones that don't need electricity —but they work better when the dog has a choice to leave them. A dog trapped on a cooling mat in a crate is a dog who might get too cold. Put the mat next to her bed, not under it. She'll move on and off as she needs.

Wet towels are worse than nothing. They trap a layer of hot, humid air against the dog's body once they warm up, creating a sauna. A fan pointed at a dry dog helps. A fan pointed at a wet dog helps even more —evaporative cooling works on fur the same way it works on skin. A frozen water bottle wrapped in a thin towel, placed next to (not under) a resting dog, is worth five cooling mats. Dogs who run hot —Huskies, Malamutes, any double-coated breed —will lie next to frozen bottles for hours. Rotate two or three from the freezer.

The frozen Kong trick: stuff a Kong with wet food, plug the small end with peanut butter, freeze it solid. Gives a dog 30 minutes of work in a cool, stationary position when she'd otherwise be pacing and overheating. Do this at 2 PM on a 95-degree day and the dog naps afterward instead of panting circles around the living room.
