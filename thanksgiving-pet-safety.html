Thanksgiving Pet Safety: What Dogs and Cats Should Never Eat

Keep the celebration simple for your pet: regular food, a secure kitchen, a quiet place away from guests, and a plan before the first plate hits the table.

Last updated: 2026-11-10

Think your pet ate something unsafe? Use our Toxic Food Checker for a quick category check, then call your veterinarian, an emergency clinic, or a poison-control service. Do not wait for symptoms or try to make your pet vomit unless a veterinary professional directs you to.

Start With the Safest Rule: Your Pet Does Not Need a Thanksgiving Plate

The holiday meal is built around exactly the things that cause trouble for pets: rich fat, salty stock, onions and garlic hidden in recipes, bones, desserts, and a kitchen full of dropped food. The easiest plan is also the kindest one: serve your dog or cat their normal meal, then keep the human food on the table.

If you decide to share a taste, make it deliberately boring: a small piece of plain, fully cooked, boneless, skinless turkey. No gravy, no seasoning, no stuffing, no skin, and no pan drippings. That is not a special diet recommendation; it is simply the lower-risk choice among a table full of higher-risk ones.

The Kitchen Is the Main Risk Zone

Most holiday incidents are not a pet being handed a dramatic piece of chocolate. They are a dog pulling a trash bag down, a cat licking a pan, a guest dropping food, or a plate left at pet height while everyone is answering the door.

  • Put the turkey carcass, bones, foil, skewers, string, and food scraps in a latched bin or outside container as soon as you are done.
  • Keep baking ingredients and sugar-free products off the counter; check labels for xylitol before they become a shared snack.
  • Tell guests one simple rule: please do not feed the pet from the table.
  • Assign one adult to do a final floor and coffee-table sweep before the evening winds down.
  • If someone does get sick on a rug or sofa, use an enzymatic stain and odor remover instead of a household cleaner: the residue that ordinary cleaners leave behind is what invites a repeat visit.

Guests, Doors, and Decorations Matter Too

A busy house can be stressful even for a friendly pet. Prepare a quiet room before anyone arrives: familiar bedding, water, a safe chew or enrichment toy, and a closed door. This is not punishment—it is a way to let your pet opt out of the noise, new smells, children, coats, and repeated door openings.

Watch exits. A pet that normally stays near you can bolt when the door opens repeatedly. Make sure collar information and microchip registration are current before the gathering, not after a pet goes missing. Keep flowers, decorative gourds, corn cobs, candles, and potpourri out of reach too; not food does not mean safe to swallow.

If Something Was Eaten: The First Five Minutes

  1. Remove the remaining food or packaging so there cannot be a second helping.
  2. Save the ingredient list, wrapper, and an estimate of how much was eaten.
  3. Note the time and your pet's species, size, age, health conditions, and medications.
  4. Call your regular veterinarian, a local emergency clinic, or poison control promptly.
  5. Do not induce vomiting, give milk, oil, salt, charcoal, or another home remedy unless a veterinary professional tells you to.

Go straight to emergency care for collapse, trouble breathing, repeated vomiting, tremors, seizures, marked weakness, a swollen painful abdomen, or choking. With a possible toxin, do not wait for those signs to appear before you call.

An after-hours emergency visit is the expensive end of this problem, and the bill arrives before the diagnosis does. If that uncertainty would change what you do at eleven at night, it is worth understanding what a pet insurance policy actually covers before the holiday rather than in a waiting room.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my pet have a little turkey?

A small amount of plain, cooked, boneless, skinless turkey is the lower-risk option if you choose to offer a taste. It is still safer—and often easier on the stomach—to keep to the usual diet.

Can dogs eat Thanksgiving stuffing?

No. Stuffing commonly contains onion, garlic, stock, herbs, butter, fat, or ingredients that cannot be separated once cooked. Give your dog a pet-safe treat instead.

What about pumpkin?

Plain pumpkin is different from pumpkin pie. Pie filling and desserts may contain sugar, fat, spices, chocolate, alcohol, or sweeteners. Do not treat a holiday dessert as plain pumpkin.

Should I make my pet vomit after a dangerous food?

Not unless a veterinarian or poison-control professional specifically instructs you to. The correct response depends on the food, amount, time, and pet; making an animal vomit can create a second emergency.
