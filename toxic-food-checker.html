Why "Can My Dog Eat X" Is the Wrong Question

Most people search for "can my dog eat grapes" while their dog is actively eating grapes off the floor. The better question is usually: how much did she eat, how much does she weigh, and how fast can I get to a vet?

The toxicity of a food to a dog depends on three variables: the dog's weight, the amount consumed, and the concentration of the toxic compound in that specific food. A square of milk chocolate is a non-event for a 90-pound Labrador. A square of baking chocolate can kill a 10-pound Chihuahua. Both are "chocolate." The dose makes the poison.

This tool gives you a quick answer —safe, caution, or dangerous —plus the specifics you need to decide whether to induce vomiting, call the vet, or just stop worrying. But it's a triage tool, not a substitute for a veterinarian. When in doubt, call. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center is staffed 24/7 at (888) 426-4435. They'll ask your dog's weight, what she ate, how much, and when —have that information ready.

Why Dogs Can't Eat What We Eat

Dogs and humans diverged about 94 million years ago. We evolved different liver enzymes, different gut lengths, and different metabolic pathways. A dog's liver is bad at breaking down certain plant compounds that humans handle easily —theobromine in chocolate, persin in avocado, allicin in onions and garlic. What's a mild stimulant to us is a lethal neurotoxin to them.

Dogs are also much smaller than adult humans, which means a smaller dose hits harder. And they don't have the learned aversion that humans develop —a dog who's gotten sick from eating garbage once will probably eat garbage again. The combination of metabolic vulnerability and lack of caution is what makes certain human foods so dangerous.

On the flip side, some "people food" is perfectly fine —even healthy —for dogs. Plain pumpkin, blueberries, cooked eggs, lean chicken. If you're going to share your plate, know which side of the line each food falls on. That's what this tool is for.
