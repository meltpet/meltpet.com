When to Spay or Neuter?

Almost every clinic says six months. The published breed-by-breed data says something different for almost every breed — and almost nothing at all for cats.

Last updated: 2026-10-30

The Short Version: Cats and Dogs Give Different Answers

For cats, the timing question is mostly logistics. For dogs, it is a health question, and the answer changes with breed and with sex. Those two sentences are the whole article, and they are worth stating plainly because the advice people actually receive — six months, for everything, everywhere — collapses them into one.

The cat half is the easier half. The largest cohort study of the question followed 1,660 cats that were gonadectomised and adopted from a shelter before they were a year old, for as long as eleven years after surgery, and tested 47 medical and behavioural conditions against the age at which the operation happened. Cats operated on before 5.5 months had no increase in death, no increase in relinquishment, and no increase in any serious medical or behavioural condition. They had decreases in several. That is close to a clean answer: for a cat, waiting until six months buys you nothing measurable except a second appointment.

The dog half is not clean, because it is not one question. In the same research programme, using one hospital's records and one set of diagnostic criteria, neutering a male Golden Retriever before six months was associated with hip dysplasia in 14.67 per cent of dogs, against 4.07 per cent in intact males of the same breed. Neutering a female German Shepherd between six and eleven months was associated with urinary incontinence in 7.22 per cent, against zero in intact bitches. In Labrador Retrievers the joint signal is present and the cancer signal is absent. In Boston Terriers it runs the other way round. Same operation, same decade, same database — different answers, because the animals are different.

Before you read further, one distinction that decides how to use any of this. These are population statistics about groups of dogs. They tell you which choice carries more recorded disease in a few thousand animals at one American teaching hospital. They do not tell you what will happen to the puppy in front of you, and they say nothing about the outcome of leaving an animal entire — which has its own risks, its own arithmetic, and its own literature. A page that gave you a single recommended age for all dogs would be hiding the interesting part.

Where "Six Months" Comes From

The six-month figure has a history, and knowing it explains why the data can contradict it without anyone being careless. It is not a threshold that emerged from a disease study. It is a surgical and population-management convention that hardened into a default.

The 2004 shelter cohort papers describe it directly: they call six to eight months "the traditional age" and set their whole comparison up as early-age gonadectomy versus gonadectomy at that traditional age. A 2007 review in the same journal opens by noting that elective gonadectomy is one of the most common veterinary procedures in the United States and that the optimal age for it is increasingly questioned, in the United States and elsewhere. That sentence is worth reading twice, because it tells you the question was live twenty years ago and is still live now.

Why six months, then? Three practical reasons, none of them about joints. First, it is roughly when a puppy has finished its primary vaccination course, so it is an appointment the owner is already making. Second, it is a size at which familiar surgical technique and familiar anaesthetic doses apply, which mattered much more before modern monitoring became routine in general practice. Third, and most importantly for shelters, it is early enough to guarantee that an adopted animal leaves the building unable to breed — which is the outcome the procedure was scaled up to deliver.

That third reason is not a small one, and it is not a reason to sneer at the convention. Shelter medicine solved a real problem by moving gonadectomy earlier, and in cats the data has since vindicated that move. But it means the six-month rule was optimised for population control and surgical logistics, and then inherited by private practice as though it were a health recommendation. It was never tested as one.

The consequence is a category error that runs through most pet writing on this subject. "The evidence says six months" and "the evidence does not say six months" are both wrong, because the evidence was never organised around that number as an outcome. What the evidence is organised around is a comparison: neutered at this age versus left intact, within this breed, in this sex. Once you read it that way, the breed differences stop being noise and start being the finding.

What the Surgery Actually Changes

For the diseases in the tables that follow, two mechanisms matter, and only one of them is well supported by experiment.

Growth plates. Gonadal hormones are part of the signal that closes the growth plates at the ends of long bones. Remove the source of those hormones before the plates shut and closure is delayed. The classic demonstration is a 1991 study of 32 mixed-breed dogs divided into three groups: neutered at seven weeks, neutered at seven months, and left intact. Growth-plate closure was delayed in all neutered dogs compared with intact dogs, at a significance level the authors report as p less than 0.000001 — and it was delayed longer in the seven-week group than in the seven-month group, at p less than 0.000045. The rate of growth was unaffected, but the extra time at the growth plate produced measurably longer forearm bones in the dogs neutered at seven weeks.

That is the mechanism the orthopaedic findings are usually hung on: slightly longer bones, slightly different joint alignment, and in a dog already predisposed to hip dysplasia or a cruciate tear, alignment is not a trivial variable. The 2020 breed paper states the hypothesis in almost exactly those words. It remains a hypothesis — a plausible one with a demonstrated upstream step, not a demonstrated chain.

Hormone-responsive tissue. The second mechanism is the one used to explain the cancer findings: mammary tissue and some tumour types are influenced by sex hormones, so removing them earlier should reduce exposure. This is where the story gets weaker, and there is a whole section on it further down, because the strength of that claim has been badly overstated in popular writing and differs sharply between dogs and cats.

And one thing the surgery does not do, which surprises most people. In that same 1991 experiment, gonadectomy did not influence food intake, weight gain, or back-fat depth over the fifteen months of the study. The dogs did not eat more and did not get fatter as a direct result of the operation. Whatever drives the weight gain that owners and vets observe after neutering, it is not a simple metabolic consequence of removing the gonads in a controlled feeding situation — which means it is at least partly a management variable, and management variables can be managed.

There is a morphological footnote in the same paper that is often quoted out of context: the pars glandis of the penis measured 11.1 millimetres across in males neutered at seven weeks, against 16.3 at seven months and 21.0 in intact males. That is a real anatomical difference and it is not a disease. It belongs in a conversation about what early surgery does, not in one about what it harms.

How These Numbers Were Made, and How to Read Them

Nearly every figure on this page comes from one research programme working through one hospital's medical records: the Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital at the University of California, Davis. That is a strength and a limitation, and you should know both before you use a number from the tables.

The design is a retrospective cohort. Dogs and cats that came through the hospital between 2000 and roughly 2012 were sorted by breed, sex, and the age band in which they had been neutered — under six months, six to eleven months, one year (meaning twelve to under twenty-four months), two to eight years, or left intact. Each animal was then checked for a fixed list of diagnoses. For the joint analysis the list was hip dysplasia, cranial cruciate ligament tear or rupture, and elbow dysplasia. For cancers it was lymphosarcoma, mast cell tumour, haemangiosarcoma and osteosarcoma. In females, mammary carcinoma, pyometra and urinary incontinence were added.

The comparison is always against intact animals of the same breed and same sex, never against a breed-wide average. That single design choice is what makes the percentages comparable across breeds and is the reason a Golden Retriever number and a Chihuahua number can sit in the same table without being nonsense.

Statistics were survival analyses rather than simple proportions, because an animal neutered at two years has less time at risk behind it than one neutered at two months. The groups were compared with Kaplan-Meier life-table analysis, then log-rank and Wilcoxon tests; joint disorders were tested with log-rank first because their risk is roughly constant across life, cancers with Wilcoxon first because their risk rises with age. The threshold for significance was p less than 0.05, two-tailed. In the tables reproduced below, an asterisk marks a group whose incidence was significantly above the intact group for that breed and sex.

Two structural features of the data determine how much weight each row can carry. The first is the denominators. Every percentage is printed next to the fraction it came from, and those fractions are small in the early bands. Four cases out of fifty-three dogs is 7.55 per cent; it is also four dogs. A single additional case would move it by nearly two points. Where you see a large percentage on a small denominator, read it as a signal about direction rather than as a precise risk.

The second is follow-up. The mean date of last entry per patient ranged from about 4.5 to 5.5 years, which means the dataset is dominated by diseases that appear early. That is deliberate — the authors argue the early-onset conditions are the ones that cost caregivers most, emotionally and financially — but it has a specific consequence for mammary cancer, whose median age at diagnosis in dogs is around 10.1 years. A study that watches most animals for five years cannot see most mammary cancers. The mammary rows in these tables are therefore the least complete, and should be read as such.

One more thing worth stating because it is routinely lost in retellings: these are records of diagnoses made at a referral hospital. An animal that never came back does not appear as a case. Retrospective cohorts under-count whatever does not return, and there is no way to correct for that from the paper alone.

Read the male hip dysplasia line first: 14.67 per cent in the before-six-months group, 4.07 per cent in intact males. That is a difference of about ten and a half percentage points, and the two groups are the same breed, the same sex and the same hospital. The 2013 paper that first reported these Golden numbers gives the comparison a p-value of 0.04 on the log-rank test for early-neutered versus intact males, and 0.003 for cruciate tears in the same comparison.

Note the zero in the male cruciate row. Not one intact male Golden in this dataset had a recorded cruciate tear — 0 out of 226. That makes the intact baseline for that specific disorder unusually fragile: any tear at all in a neutered group becomes an infinite ratio against zero. It is also, for a Golden owner, the most striking single number on the page, because it says the intact group had no problem to make worse.

Females show the same shape with a different emphasis. Cruciate tears were recorded in 10.89 per cent of bitches neutered before six months, against zero of 165 intact bitches, with a log-rank p-value of 0.001 in the 2013 paper. The cancer table refuses to arrange itself into a neat story. In males, the significant lymphosarcoma elevation is in the six-to-eleven-month band, not the earliest one. The 2020 breed paper, summarising across its much larger sample, puts it as follows: about 5 per cent of intact male Goldens had one or more of the cancers studied, against 10 per cent of males neutered before six months and 12 per cent of males neutered at six to eleven months, at p less than 0.01 for the two neuter periods combined; and in females, neutering at any age was associated with an occurrence of one or more cancers two to four times the 5 per cent level seen in intact females. In plain terms: for this breed, the cancer argument does not favour early surgery, and it does not clearly favour late surgery either. It argues for not being confident. The breed has its own numbers elsewhere on this site: the Golden Retriever guide covers hip and elbow scoring, the breed cancer rates and its cost profile.

Three things stand out, and the third is the one to remember.

The male cruciate signal is real but rests on a small group. Four tears in 53 dogs neutered before six months is 7.55 per cent, against 12 in 531 intact males — 2.26 per cent. The direction matches the Goldens. The denominator does not: 53 dogs is not many, and the confidence interval around 7.55 per cent is wide enough that the number should be read as "this happened" rather than "this happens to seven and a half per cent of dogs".

The female hip dysplasia signal is the clearest thing in the Labrador table. Three age bands are significantly elevated — 5.36, 5.05 and 4.26 per cent — against 1.74 per cent in intact bitches. That is a consistent gradient across the first two years, not a single spike in the earliest band, which is the pattern you would expect if the mechanism were gradual rather than a discrete event at a particular age.

And then: not one cancer category was significantly elevated, in either sex, at any age. Mast cell tumour in males neutered before six months was 3.77 per cent against 2.25 in intact; haemangiosarcoma in intact females was 0.29 per cent, the lowest figure in the table. The 2020 summary paper states it flatly: in male and female Labrador Retrievers and German Shepherd Dogs, there was no evident increase in cancers above that of the dogs left intact.

Sit with that for a moment. Two retrievers, one hospital, one decade. In Goldens, neutering is associated with more cancer in both sexes. In Labradors, it is associated with none. Any article — including, in principle, this one — that gives you a single sentence about "neutering and cancer in dogs" is telling you something that the best data available says is false at the level of the breed. If you own one of the two retrievers compared above, the Labrador guide carries the breed-specific health and cost figures that sit alongside these tables.

The shape is unmistakable, and it is a shape rather than a number. Males neutered before a year: 17.72 per cent with one or more joint disorders, against 6.59 per cent in intact males. Males neutered at one year: 8.62 per cent. Males neutered between two and eight years: 4.49 per cent — below the intact group. The elevation is concentrated in the first year and gone by the second band.

That gradient is the reason this dataset is more persuasive than a single elevated percentage. If early neutering were simply a marker for owners who are more attentive to their dogs' health, or for dogs that came from worse circumstances, you would expect the elevation to be roughly flat across neutering ages, because those owners neuter at all ages. It is not flat. It falls to intact levels as soon as the surgery moves past the growth period. Something about the timing itself is doing the work.

Females show the same concentration: 16.36 per cent with one or more joint disorders when pooled before twelve months, 2.86 per cent at one year, 5.56 per cent in the two-to-eight band, 5.13 per cent intact.

And, as with the Labradors, the cancer table produced nothing. Intact males had one or more cancers in 13 of 450 dogs, 2.89 per cent; no neutered band exceeded that significantly. Intact females had 1 of 158, 0.63 per cent. The authors of the 2020 multi-breed paper put German Shepherds alongside Labradors in the "no evident increase in cancers" group.

Same breed and cohort. Pyometra is recorded only in intact bitches, by definition of the condition; the zeros in neutered rows are structural, not a finding. Urinary incontinence is the one condition where an early-surgery penalty is statistically significant.

Read the pyometra row carefully, because there is a trap in it. Pyometra cannot occur in a spayed bitch, so those zeros are guaranteed by the definition and are not evidence of anything. What the table does show is the size of the thing you are avoiding: 4 of 163 intact bitches, 2.45 per cent, were diagnosed with pyometra during follow-up. That is the concrete, non-rhetorical benefit of spaying a female dog, and it is a genuine one.

Against it sits the incontinence row, which is the one place in this entire literature where early surgery produces a statistically significant harm that everyone agrees about. Zero of 156 intact bitches. 4.65 per cent before six months. 7.22 per cent at six to eleven months — the only significant figure in the row. 2.78 per cent at a year. 1.05 per cent between two and eight years. That is a monotonic gradient in exactly the direction the mechanism predicts, and it is the finding most likely to change a real decision.

Urinary Incontinence: The Risk That Keeps Replicating

Single studies can be wrong in ways you cannot see from the paper. The reason the incontinence finding deserves its own section is that it has been found more than once, by different groups, with different designs — and in this literature that is rare enough to be worth naming.

The German Shepherd cohort gives one estimate: a significant elevation in bitches neutered at six to eleven months, and a gradient that falls as the age at surgery rises. The 2004 shelter cohort of 1,842 dogs, drawn from a completely different population, found the same thing in the same direction: among female dogs, early-age gonadectomy was associated with an increased rate of cystitis, and decreasing age at gonadectomy was associated with an increasing rate of urinary incontinence. Those authors concluded, on the basis of that finding alone, that for female dogs, delaying gonadectomy until at least three months of age may be beneficial.

Note what that sentence is and is not. It is a caveat attached by researchers who were broadly supportive of early gonadectomy, in a paper whose overall conclusion was that the procedure offers more benefit than risk for male dogs. It is not an argument against spaying. It is an argument that the urethral sphincter mechanism in a bitch responds to the timing of the surgery, and that this is the cost side of the ledger that is easiest to demonstrate.

What makes incontinence the decision-relevant harm is not its severity — it is usually manageable with medication — but its arithmetic. It is largely lifelong, it starts young in these cohorts, and it is the outcome owners most often say they would have decided differently about. A dog that leaks while asleep is not a medical emergency, but it is a daily, permanent, house-wide fact.

Two honest limits. First, the mechanism is not settled: the leading explanation involves the effect of gonadal hormones on urethral closure, and the risk is known to be higher in larger breeds and in animals with a shorter urethra, which is one reason the German Shepherd data cannot be transferred to a Chihuahua. Second, these cohorts did not follow animals long enough to say whether the effect is purely a question of timing or a question of whether the surgery happens at all — an intact bitch in the German Shepherd cohort had zero cases in 156 dogs, but those dogs were, by definition, still exposed to pyometra and to whatever else the next five years brought.

The Other 32 Breeds: Where Timing Barely Matters

The 2020 paper covers 35 breeds, counting three Poodle varieties separately. Three of them — Golden Retriever, Labrador Retriever and German Shepherd — have their own full papers, which is why this page can print their tables. The other 32 are summarised, and the summary contains the finding that most contradicts the way this subject is usually discussed.

For ten small breeds, joint disorders were close to zero in both intact and neutered animals. The paper names them: Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, Chihuahua, Corgi, Dachshund, Maltese, Pomeranian, Toy Poodle, Pug, Shih Tzu and Yorkshire Terrier. In the same ten, cancers were low in both groups. For an owner of one of these breeds, the orthopaedic argument for delaying surgery does not exist in this dataset — there was almost no orthopaedic disease to prevent.

Two small breeds break the pattern on the cancer side. The Boston Terrier and the Shih Tzu both showed a significant increase in cancers with neutering. The Boston Terrier is also the paper's clearest example of a sex reversal: neutering females at the standard six months did not increase joint disorders or cancers over intact dogs, but in males, neutering before a year of age was associated with a significant increase in cancers. Same breed, opposite directions, one year apart in age at surgery.

The Cocker Spaniel runs the other way. In males, neutering at six months was not associated with an increase in joint disorders or cancers. In females, there was a significant increase in the risk of cancers, to 17 per cent, with neutering before two years.

The Poodles are the neatest illustration of how little "breed" alone tells you, because the only difference between them is size. In the Standard Poodle, neutering males at one year was associated with a highly significant increase in the risk of a cancer, mainly lymphosarcoma, to over six times that of intact males. In the Miniature Poodle, there was no increase in cancers at all, but a significant increase in joint disorders, mainly cruciate tears, with neutering at six to eleven months. Two varieties of one breed, opposite findings, different systems.

And then there is the word "choice". In the paper's summary table, each breed gets a suggested age for males and for females. Where no increase in joint disorders or cancers was associated with neutering at any age, the guidance is not a number — it is that those wishing to neuter should decide on the appropriate age themselves. Where neutering before six months carried an increased risk but neutering after six months did not, the default recommendation is beyond six months. The guidelines are not a schedule; they are a map of where the risks are uneven.

For a breed not among the 35, the authors suggest finding one or two genetically close relatives and using their pattern as an estimate — while warning explicitly that even related breeds can differ a great deal. Their own example is the one on this page: the Golden and the Labrador, adjacent in the breed tree, agreeing on joints and disagreeing completely on cancer. That warning is the most important sentence in the paper for anyone whose breed is not listed.

Two breeds in that dataset are worth a specific note because they were included for a different disease. Corgis and Dachshunds were examined for intervertebral disc disorders as well as the standard list, on the grounds that they are the breeds known to be at risk. If you own one of them, the breed guides at the links below are a better place to start than a general page about neutering: the Corgi guide and the Dachshund guide both deal with the spinal question directly.

The Mammary Cancer Argument Is Weaker Than You Were Told

If you have read anything about spaying a female dog, you have read that spaying before the first heat nearly eliminates mammary cancer risk, that after the first heat the protection falls sharply, and that after the second it is mostly gone. Those numbers circulate everywhere. What is much less widely reported is what happened when someone actually went looking for the studies behind them.

A 2012 systematic review did exactly that, following Cochrane methodology, with two independent reviewers assessing risk of bias. It started from 11,149 search results. Thirteen reports in peer-reviewed English-language journals actually addressed the association between neutering, or age at neutering, and mammary tumours. Nine of those thirteen were judged to carry a high risk of bias; the remaining four were judged moderate. Of those four: one found an association between neutering and a reduced risk, two found no evidence of an association, and one reported "some protective effect" without presenting numbers.

The reviewers' conclusion was that, given the limited evidence available and the risk of bias in the published results, the evidence that neutering reduces the risk of mammary neoplasia — and the evidence that the age at neutering has an effect — is limited. That is a careful sentence, and it is a long way from "spay before the first heat and you are protected".

The primary data in this literature points the same way. The 2020 breed paper reports that, through eleven years of age, the occurrence of mammary carcinoma in intact females was rarely above 6 per cent and frequently 2 per cent or less; and that in females neutered before six months there was, as expected, no occurrence at all. The German Shepherd table above shows the same thing at a smaller scale: 4.1 per cent in intact bitches, 1.11 per cent in those neutered at six to eleven months, 2.7 per cent at a year, 4.9 per cent in the two-to-eight-year band. There is no clean age gradient in that row, and the largest figure is in a late-neutering group.

There is also a mechanical reason the mammary numbers are the weakest in the dataset: mammary cancer is a late disease, with a median age at diagnosis around 10.1 years in dogs, and these cohorts followed most animals for four and a half to five and a half years. A study that ends before the disease usually starts cannot see it. That does not make the protective effect false. It makes it unmeasured.

Now the contrast that matters, because it inverts the usual advice. In cats, the mammary argument is genuinely strong. A case-control study of 308 cats with histologically confirmed mammary carcinoma and 400 controls found intact cats significantly over-represented in the cancer group, with an odds ratio of 2.7 and a confidence interval of 1.4 to 5.3. Cats spayed before six months of age had a 91 per cent reduction in risk compared with intact cats; those spayed before one year had an 86 per cent reduction, with an odds ratio of 0.14 and a confidence interval of 0.06 to 0.34. Parity had no effect.

So the claim that is strong in cats is weak in dogs, and the claim that is strong in dogs — the orthopaedic one — is absent in cats. Most general pet writing states both claims for both species and gets four things wrong at once. If you take one thing from this section, take this: the surgery is the same operation but the risk profile is species-specific, and the mammary argument is not a reason to schedule a dog's surgery early.

What This Data Cannot Tell You

A page that prints percentages without printing their limits is not being careful, it is being confident. Here are the limits, in the order they matter.

It is retrospective and unrandomised. Nobody assigned these dogs to neutering ages by coin flip. Owners who neuter at four months differ from owners who wait two years — in breed choice, in household, in how often they bring the dog to a referral hospital, possibly in income. Every association in these tables is contaminated by that, and no amount of statistical adjustment fully removes it. The gradient argument in the German Shepherd section is an attempt to reason around this, not a proof that it is solved.

It is one hospital, and a referral hospital at that. A secondary and tertiary facility admitting over 50,000 cases a year sees a different case mix from a general practice. Dogs that arrive there may be sicker, or referred for the exact problems being counted. Whether the percentages transfer to your local population is unknown.

Thirty-five breeds is not the dog population. The authors say plainly that most registered breeds were not covered, and that the breeds chosen were the most popular, the ones with the largest records, or ones included to sample a range of sizes. Some of the largest and smallest breeds have small numbers. A breed guide is a better source for a breed not on the list, and a related breed is an estimate rather than an answer.

Breed is not breed line. Two Golden Retriever lines can differ more in hip dysplasia prevalence than the Golden differs from the Labrador. Nothing in this dataset sees that variation, and the authors flag it themselves when they discuss mammary cancer and suggest that genetic and breed-line differences may simply not be portrayed in a hospital database.

Follow-up is short for late diseases. Four and a half to five and a half years, against a mammary cancer median diagnosis age of about ten. Everything here is dominated by what happens early. If delayed neutering protects against a cancer that appears at nine, this data would not know.

And it says nothing at all about leaving an animal entire. This is the omission that makes most popular writing on the subject dishonest in both directions. Intact animals in this dataset carried pyometra (2.45 per cent in German Shepherd bitches), and they carry risks this study did not measure: pregnancy, roaming injuries, testicular neoplasia, and — for the population — the arithmetic that animal shelters exist to manage. A decision framed only as "when to operate" quietly assumes the operation happens. For a companion animal the operation usually should happen. The open question is when, and for some breeds whether the timing matters at all.

The Cat Data Says Almost the Opposite

Everything above is about dogs. The cat literature asks the same question and returns an answer so different that it is worth stating separately rather than as a footnote.

The study is a retrospective cohort of 1,660 cats that were gonadectomised and adopted from a large animal shelter before they were a year old. Follow-up ran as long as eleven years after surgery, with a median of 3.9 years. Adopters completed questionnaires about behaviour and medical history, and veterinary records were reviewed where possible. The analysis tested 47 medical and behavioural conditions against the cats' age at gonadectomy, contrasting those operated on before 5.5 months with those operated on later.

The finding, in the authors' own summary: gonadectomy before 5.5 months of age was not associated with increased rates of death or relinquishment, or with the occurrence of any serious medical or behavioural condition, and may provide certain important long-term benefits, especially for male cats.

The specific directions are worth listing, because several of them are the opposite of what you would guess from the dog data:

The authors' clinical recommendation follows from this: shelters can safely gonadectomise cats at a young age, and veterinarians should consider recommending routine gonadectomy for client-owned cats before the traditional age of six to eight months.

Two things follow, and the first is a myth that needs retiring. It is often said that male cats should be left until six months so that the urethra can develop, because early neutering causes obstruction. If that were a large effect, a cohort that tested 47 conditions in males operated on before 5.5 months would be the place it showed up. It reports no increase in serious medical conditions in that group at all — and several decreases. That is not proof the effect is zero, but it is the strongest available evidence against a claim that has been used to delay surgery for decades.

The second is that the cat case for operating is strong on exactly the axis where the dog case is weakest. In cats, the mammary carcinoma signal is real and large — a 91 per cent reduction when spaying happens before six months, 86 per cent before a year — and there is no orthopaedic penalty to weigh against it. In dogs it is the reverse: the orthopaedic signal is strong and the mammary evidence survives systematic review only weakly. The two species are not variants of one rule. They are two different problems that happen to share an operation.

If you want the longer version of the cat side — what neutering status actually does to lifespan, with a coefficient attached — that is on the cat lifespan page, where being neutered was associated with 1.07 additional years in a later-life model.

Why Cats and Dogs Diverge

It is tempting to treat the cat result as the dog result with a smaller animal plugged in. The data does not support that, and the reasons are visible.

Scale and skeletal maturity. The orthopaedic hypothesis rests on growth plates still being open when the hormones are removed. A four-kilogram cat reaches skeletal maturity much earlier in absolute time than a thirty-kilogram retriever, so the window in which the growth-plate mechanism can operate is shorter and closes sooner. The same six-month appointment lands in a very different place in the growth trajectory for the two species.

The disease mix is different before the surgery is. The dog signal in these tables is dominated by three orthopaedic conditions — hip dysplasia, cruciate tear, elbow dysplasia — that are largely large-breed problems. The cat cohort shows no orthopaedic penalty at all, and it is not obvious that it would be able to: cats in that dataset were not being screened for the feline equivalents in a way that would generate recordable diagnoses.

Population pressure differs. Cat gonadectomy in shelters is driven by an overwhelming surplus, and the procedure is routinely done at eight to twelve weeks in that setting. Dog gonadectomy in shelters is driven by the same pressure but meets more resistance from owners of large purebreds, who have been told the breed needs to mature. The cat literature therefore got its early-age data from a large, homogeneous, well-followed population, and the dog literature got its data from breed-specific referral records. Different questions got different study designs, and the designs partly created the contrast.

And the one place where cats show a strong hormonal effect is cancer, not joints. Feline mammary carcinoma is hormonally responsive in a way that the systematic review could not confirm for dogs. That single difference flips the direction of the advice: in cats, earlier is better on the outcome that matters; in dogs, later is better on the outcome that matters, and the cancer outcome is a coin flip that the evidence cannot resolve.

Weight: The One Thing You Can Actually Control

Almost every conversation about neutering eventually reaches weight gain, and almost every version of that conversation is wrong in the same way — it treats the gain as a metabolic consequence of the operation, and therefore as unavoidable.

The controlled evidence does not support that. In the 1991 study of 32 dogs followed for fifteen months, gonadectomy did not influence food intake, weight gain, or back-fat depth. The neutered dogs ate the same and carried the same fat as the intact dogs. If the operation had a direct metabolic effect large enough to matter, a fifteen-month controlled comparison with three groups is where it should have appeared.

The observational evidence is even less co-operative. In the 2004 shelter cohort of 1,842 dogs, obesity was among the conditions that decreased in early-neutered animals, alongside separation anxiety, escaping behaviour and relinquishment. That runs against most clinicians' experience and against other published work, and the honest reading is that questionnaire-based cohorts and clinic populations are measuring different things. It is a useful reminder that "everyone knows" is not a finding.

What is not in dispute is that energy requirement falls as an animal matures and as lean mass and activity change, whether or not the gonads are removed. The practical consequence is the same either way: the amount you were feeding a six-month-old puppy is not the amount an eighteen-month-old needs, and the difference is large enough that continuing the puppy ration produces a fat adult. That is a management problem with a management solution.

Which means the useful action is measurement, not prediction. Weigh the animal monthly for the first year after surgery, score body condition rather than body weight, and adjust portions against a measured requirement rather than a bag. A cheap pet scale that reads to a hundred grams removes the guesswork from the first year, and it is the single most useful purchase attached to this decision.

If the animal is already carrying excess weight, the arithmetic that matters is on the weight-loss page for cats, which works through target weight, resting energy requirement and the eighty-per-cent feeding figure. For dogs, the weight checker does the same job from a body condition score.

If You Are Deciding This Week

The tables do not produce a universal rule, but they do constrain the decision in each of the situations people actually face.

You are adopting a shelter animal and the surgery is already done. There is nothing to decide, and that is fine. The papers above are why shelters do it early: for cats it is vindicated by the data, and for dogs the shelter population argument is strong enough that the individual timing question has already been traded away. What you can still act on is weight management in the first year, and that is covered above.

You have a large-breed male puppy from a breeder. If the breed is one of the three with full tables — Golden, Labrador, German Shepherd — the joint data gives a consistent reason to wait past the first birthday, and the German Shepherd gradient shows the risk falling away as you do. For other large and giant breeds the evidence is thinner but the direction is the same, and the mechanism does not care which breed carries it. Ask the veterinarian when that individual dog's growth plates are closed rather than assuming a calendar date; the papers' age bands are a proxy for that, not a substitute for it.

You have a small-breed puppy. In ten named small breeds there was almost no joint disease in either group, which removes the main argument for waiting. The exceptions to check before you book are the Boston Terrier and the Shih Tzu, both of which showed a significant cancer increase with neutering, and the Cocker Spaniel if the animal is female.

You have a female dog of any breed. The incontinence data is the one thing that argues for delay, it is strongest in large breeds, and it is the finding most likely to be replicated. If the animal is a German Shepherd the numbers are on the page above. If it is a giant breed, the argument is at least as strong.

You have a cat of either sex. The timing question is close to a logistics question. Before 5.5 months is supported by the only large cohort that tested it, with decreases in spraying and aggression and no increase in serious disease. The urethral-development argument that has been used to delay male cats for decades does not appear in that cohort's results.

You have an adult animal that is still entire. Almost nothing on this page applies directly, because every table is about age at surgery in young animals. What changes with an adult is the balance of the reasons: pyometra prevention in females is a live and growing risk that accumulates with every season, mammary protection is largely spent, and the orthopaedic window has closed. That is a different conversation and it is not one a data table can finish — but it is worth having before the next heat rather than after.

If You Are Booking the Appointment

A few practical notes, in the order they tend to matter.

Write the date down. Every number on this page is indexed to age at surgery. If you move house, change clinics, or need to fill in an insurance form in six years, the exact date is the one fact that makes your animal's history legible. It also lets you place the animal in the correct band if you ever read a paper like the ones above and wonder where it falls.

Ask what recovery involves, not just what the operation involves. The two weeks afterwards are the part owners under-plan for. Most clinics send an animal home in a cone or a recovery suit; the suit is easier on some animals and worse on others, and which one your dog tolerates is worth asking about before the day rather than after. Confine, restrict stairs, and check the incision daily.

Sequence it against insurance, not against the calendar. Most policies exclude pre-existing conditions and treat a not-yet-diagnosed predisposition differently from a documented one. That makes the order of events — cover first, surgery second — more consequential than the month you pick. The insurance analysis works through when cover actually pays for itself, and the monthly cost calculator puts the procedure in the context of the first year's total.

And budget the whole first year, not the invoice. For a puppy or kitten, the surgery is one line among several, and it tends to land in the same quarter as the final vaccinations and the first dental check. The first-year dog cost breakdown and the new puppy checklist both put it in sequence; for cats, the kitten checklist does the same.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best age to spay or neuter a dog?

There is no single best age, and the largest breed-by-breed dataset says so explicitly. For ten small breeds, joint disorders were close to zero whether dogs were neutered or left intact, so six months costs nothing measurable. For Golden Retrievers, Labrador Retrievers and German Shepherds, joint disorders were significantly higher in dogs neutered before a year, with German Shepherd males at 17.72 per cent for one or more joint disorders when pooled before twelve months against 6.59 per cent intact — and the elevation was gone in dogs neutered at one year or later. The practical answer is to look up the breed before booking.

Is six months too early for a large-breed dog?

In the breeds with published tables, yes, it is the earliest and worst-performing band. Male Golden Retrievers neutered before six months had hip dysplasia in 14.67 per cent of 75 dogs against 4.07 per cent of 221 intact males, and cruciate tears in 8.99 per cent against zero of 226 intact males. German Shepherd males neutered before a year had one or more joint disorders in 17.72 per cent against 6.59 per cent intact. The risk fell to intact levels in the one-year and later bands, which is what makes waiting a defensible choice rather than a superstition.

When should you spay or neuter a cat?

Before 5.5 months is the best-supported answer. In a cohort of 1,660 cats gonadectomised and adopted before a year of age and followed for up to eleven years, cats operated on before 5.5 months showed no increase in death, relinquishment or any serious medical or behavioural condition, and decreases in abscesses, aggression toward veterinarians, sexual behaviour and urine spraying. The authors recommended routine gonadectomy before the traditional six to eight months. There is no orthopaedic penalty in the cat data at all.

Do dogs get more joint problems if they are neutered early?

In some breeds, clearly and consistently. The mechanism is thought to run through delayed growth-plate closure: a 1991 controlled study of 32 dogs found closure was delayed in all neutered dogs compared with intact, and delayed longer when surgery happened at seven weeks rather than seven months. Ten small breeds showed almost no joint disease in either group, so the effect is breed-dependent rather than universal. Where it appears, it appears as a gradient that fades as age at surgery rises.

Does spaying prevent mammary cancer in dogs?

The evidence is much weaker than the claim suggests. A 2012 systematic review started from 11,149 search results and found 13 reports that actually addressed the question; nine were judged high risk of bias and four moderate. Of those four, one found a reduced risk, two found no association, and one reported a protective effect without numbers. In cats the picture is different and much stronger: spaying before six months was associated with a 91 per cent reduction in mammary carcinoma risk, and before a year with an 86 per cent reduction.

Does neutering make pets gain weight?

Not in the controlled evidence. In a fifteen-month study of 32 dogs in three groups, gonadectomy did not influence food intake, weight gain or back-fat depth. In a 1,842-dog shelter cohort, obesity was actually among the conditions that decreased in early-neutered animals — which conflicts with common clinical experience and shows how much study design shapes this answer. Energy requirement falls with maturity and activity regardless of gonadal status, so the practical fix is the same: weigh monthly, score body condition, and adjust portions.

Is it too late to neuter an older dog?

Not too late, but the reasoning changes, because every table on this page is indexed to age at surgery in young animals. In an adult female the mammary window is largely spent and the orthopaedic window is closed, which leaves pyometra prevention — 2.45 per cent of intact German Shepherd bitches in one cohort — as the concrete and accumulating benefit. For an adult male the cancer and joint arguments mostly do not apply. That is a different decision from the puppy one and deserves its own conversation rather than a rule.
