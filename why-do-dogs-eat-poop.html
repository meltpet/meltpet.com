💩 Why Do Dogs Eat Poop?

One in six of them does. Almost nobody talks about it. The science is stranger than the taboo. | 2026-08-14 · MeltPet · 6 min read

Last updated: 2026-08-14

My Neighbor's Golden Ate Poop for Eight Years. The Vet Never Once Mentioned It.

Her name was Honey, and she was the friendliest dog on the block. She'd greet you at the fence, tail going like a windshield wiper, and then — if you watched long enough — she'd circle back to a fresh pile in the corner of the yard and quietly eat it before trotting off, pleased with herself.

Her owner tried everything the forums suggested: pineapple in the food, pumpkin, a sprinkle of "meat tenderizer." None of it worked. So she just... managed it. Picked up the yard twice a day. Never let Honey off-leash near the dog park. Carried it like a secret for eight years.

Here's what she never heard, and what almost no pet article tells you: Honey was not abnormal. She was a one-in-six dog.

The Number That Should Reframe the Whole Conversation

In 2018, a research team at UC Davis led by Benjamin Hart — one of the most respected veterinary behaviorists in the field — published a study with a title that tells you everything about how little we understand this behavior. They called it "The paradox of canine conspecific coprophagy."

The word coprophagia is just the clinical term for eating feces. The "paradox" is this: the behavior is common, but it shouldn't be. Eating another animal's stool exposes a dog to intestinal parasites — roundworm, hookworm, whipworm, Giardia. From an evolutionary standpoint, it looks like self-sabotage. So why would one in six dogs do it on the regular?

The team surveyed the owners of roughly 1,500 dogs and came back with numbers that upend most of the advice on the internet:

  • 16% of dogs were "serious" stool eaters — caught eating feces six or more times.
  • 23% were observed eating stool at least once.
  • 85% of stool-eating dogs preferred it fresh — less than two days old.

Every "Obvious" Explanation Failed

When you ask an owner why their dog eats poop, you get the same theories every time. The dog's missing a nutrient. The food is cheap. The dog wasn't house-trained right. The UC Davis team tested all of them, and the results were humbling:

"The results show that diet, age, sex, and neuter status were not associated with coprophagia. The behavior was also unrelated to a dog's level of house-training."

— Hart et al., Veterinary Medicine and Science (2018)

Let that sink in. The dogs eating poop were not the ones on cheap food. They weren't the unneutered males or the poorly trained ones. They were, statistically, indistinguishable from every other dog — except for one thing. The poop-eaters tended to be what the study politely calls "greedy eaters": dogs that inhaled their food, guarded it, lived for the next meal.

So the pattern isn't "sick dog, bad diet." It's "food-motivated dog." Which points somewhere very different.

The Wolf-in-the-Den Theory

Here's the part that reframes the whole behavior.

A mother dog doesn't think her puppies' poop is disgusting. She eats it. So do wolves. A nursing bitch will consume her puppies' feces to keep the den clean — in the wild, a den full of fresh stool is a dinner bell for predators and a breeding ground for parasites. This is normal, universal, and hard-wired. It's not a behavior disorder. It's den housekeeping.

Hart's hypothesis is that coprophagia is a leftover of that ancestral program. Some dogs, especially the food-motivated ones, simply never got the memo that the den is now a living room and the "cleanup" is no longer required. The preference for fresh stool — under two days old, before the smell and parasites really set in — fits this perfectly. A wolf cleaning a den doesn't wait three days. It eats it while it's still, well, warm.

This is why the "greedy eater" correlation matters. A dog whose entire personality orbits food is more likely to run that ancient cleanup subroutine. It's not a deficiency. It's a leftover instinct, the same way grass-eating is — a behavior that made sense for an ancestor and now just looks gross to us.

Not All Poop Is the Same — The Four Flavors

Before you can fix it, you have to know which poop your dog is after. They're not all equal, and they don't all mean the same thing.

When It's Actually a Vet Emergency — Not a Quirk

Every article on this topic skips the single most important distinction, so here it is in plain terms:

A dog that has eaten poop since puppyhood is almost always behavioral. A dog that starts eating poop as an adult, out of nowhere, is a different case entirely.

Coprophagia is a real clinical sign of a handful of conditions that all share one thing: the dog's body isn't absorbing the food it's being fed, so the dog is starving on a full stomach. The list is short but serious:

  • Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) — the pancreas stops making digestive enzymes. Food goes in, comes out mostly undigested, and the dog loses weight eating twice as much.
  • Intestinal parasites — roundworm, hookworm, and whipworm steal nutrients and can drive appetite through the roof.
  • Malabsorption — IBD and other gut conditions that leave calories unabsorbed.
  • Endocrine disorders — diabetes, Cushing's, and hypothyroidism can all spike hunger.
  • Severe underfeeding — a dog on a very low-calorie diet, or the wrong portions for its size, will eat anything with a calorie in it.

The tell is the combination: sudden onset + weight loss + ravenous appetite + sometimes soft, greasy, or undigested-looking stool. That combination is not a training problem. It's bloodwork and a fecal test, and the sooner the better. A quality food won't help if the dog's own body is failing to absorb it — which is its own topic, but the short version is the label matters less than what the dog's gut actually does with it.

How to Actually Stop It (In the Right Order)

Most advice starts at step four and skips steps one through three. That's why it fails. Here's the order that works:

  1. Rule out the medical. A vet visit, a fecal test, and bloodwork if the onset was sudden or there's weight loss. Training can't fix EPI.
  2. Remove the supply. Pick up stools immediately — the single most effective intervention, full stop. If there's no poop, there's no habit. This is also why routine deworming and parasite prevention are a recurring line item worth budgeting for, not an optional add-on.
  3. Feed properly. A complete AAFCO diet at the right calories. Underfeeding is a real trigger, and the fix costs nothing but a measuring cup.
  4. Teach "leave it." High-value treats beat poop every time, but only if the cue is solid before the dog is nose-down in the yard.
  5. Kill the boredom. A dog left alone in a yard with nothing to do will invent a hobby. Sometimes that hobby is eating poop. Enrichment and exercise fix a surprising number of cases.
  6. Skip the punishment. Rubbing a dog's nose in it doesn't teach disgust. It teaches the dog to eat faster and hide the evidence. Same reason shock-collar "corrections" for this are counterproductive.

And one honest note on the supplements you'll see recommended — pineapple, pumpkin, For-Bid, CoproBan. The evidence is thin and mixed. The theory is they make the stool taste bitter or unappealing. For some dogs it helps; for Honey next door, it did nothing. Worth a try only after the first three steps are already solid. If your dog's a cat-poop enthusiast, the more reliable fix is a covered litter box, a self-cleaning box, or simply putting the box where the dog can't reach it — no supplement required.

The Takeaway

Your dog isn't broken, and you're not a bad owner. One in six dogs does this, and for most of them it's a wolf-era den-cleaning instinct that never switched off — the same category as eating grass, only harder to watch.

The split that actually matters is the one almost nobody gives you: puppy-onset = behavioral, adult-onset = rule out the medical first. If you remember nothing else, remember that. Manage the supply, feed a complete diet, teach "leave it," and skip the punishment. And if your adult dog suddenly starts eating stool while losing weight, that's not a behavior blog problem. That's a Tuesday appointment with your vet.
